# Fish visit the brook but seldom cross

The brook between Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake works less like a highway between two lakes than like a short outlet stream with three kinds of user. The first is a **resident core** that holds the same pool or run for years: large redbreast sunfish and some pumpkinseed, sub-adult smallmouth bass and blacknose dace. The second is a set of **seasonal visitors** that arrive on a temperature calendar: white suckers and fallfish come to spawn in spring, smallmouth to nest from late May, and chain pickerel on summer excursions of days to weeks. The third is **pulses of young fish** passing mostly downstream in spring and after storms. True changes of lake are rare. In the only natural northeastern lake chain with full numbers, **43 of 4,649 recaptured fish (0.9 %) had moved to another lake over a mean of about two years**, 31 of them white suckers. Stream sunfish crossed a single 74-m riffle at well under 1 % a year. Both figures are about an order of magnitude below the **5–15 % a year** the simulation now gives most species, even allowing that Hot Brook's lakes sit closer together. Big bass in the deep outlet pool are the textbook outlet funnel: wherever a lake pours prey into a stream, piscivores gather. In Maine, chain pickerel and smallmouth bass were the only predators of tethered salmon smolts and took 23 % of them within an hour. Upper supplies the brook with three things. The first is lake zooplankton, half to three-quarters of which is stripped within 200 m, mostly by fish by day. The second is dense filter feeders and mussels. The third is its own surface temperature, about 24 °C in late July, which makes the brook warmwater habitat with no cooler refuge beyond its deepest water. In a dry year the outlet stops at a sill. The riffles close first, prey and predators crowd the 1.1-m pool as it warms and loses oxygen, and smallmouth summer survival falls by 10–15 points. Fish return within about two weeks once water flows again. The local angler's account agrees with the literature on every point, and the passing minnow schools are most likely young fallfish, not dace. The simulation should cut most species' rates of moving to the other lake, move the pickerel's brook use from spring to summer, and replace open ranging with explicit brook modes for bass and pickerel. It should make dace safe on the riffles by having predators shun them, add pulses of young fish, and add a fill-and-spill dry year, in which the brook stops when Upper falls below its outlet sill. Its dace density, fallfish nest cap, sucker run and depth sorting by size are sound.

## A resident core, seasonal spawners and passing schools share the brook

No study has surveyed fish in a short brook joining two natural lakes, in Maine or anywhere else. Every rule below is assembled from Great Lakes tributaries, a river and lake pair in Ontario, a ship canal, small warmwater creeks and European lake–stream systems. That gap matters, so the inferences are flagged. The general pattern is clear, though. Streams beside a lake carry extra species, mostly as **small size classes of lake species**. On Minnesota's Kabetogama Peninsula these were black crappie, smallmouth bass, yellow perch and burbot, and richness rose closer to the lake as young fish dispersed out of it ([Schlosser & Kallemeyn 2000](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70022221)). Adirondack inlet and outlet streams held **17 taxa against 18 in their lakes** ([Daniels et al. 2008](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml)). Hot Brook's brook should therefore share the lakes' species list. Its size structure should lean toward young fish, plus a few large fish holding the pools.

### Old redbreast and small bass hold the same pools for years

Redbreast is the stream sunfish of the Northeast. It is "more tolerant of current than other sunfishes", often the dominant sunfish in larger rivers, and "uncommon to rare in lakes" in Connecticut ([CT DEEP, redbreast](https://portal.ct.gov/DEEP/Fishing/Freshwater/Freshwater-Fishes-of-Connecticut/Redbreast-Sunfish)). That fits the angler's mostly-redbreast brook; pumpkinseed are the lake sunfish that spill in.

The angler's **18–21 cm fish are large**. Connecticut's survey maximum is 23 cm, against a common 4–6 inches. In Yoho Lake, New Brunswick, a lake of Hot Brook's type (131 ha, mean depth 3.66 m), redbreast averaged **12.5 cm**, and the oldest class sampled (6+) averaged **16.1 cm** in September ([Gautreau & Curry 2012](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-19-4/18-Gautreau.shtml)). The brook's pool sunfish are therefore probably 7–10 years old or more, the population's oldest fish (inference). By Yoho's length–weight equation they weigh about 110–180 g (derived).

Stream sunfish are faithful to their pools. In Gerking's Indiana creeks, longear sunfish kept to **30–60 m** of stream, and only **5 of 191 recaptured longears crossed a 74-m riffle in four years**. Smallmouth ranged about twice as far (**60–120 m**), and only 2 of 19 crossed ([Gerking 1953](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf)). Elsewhere stream smallmouth are just as sedentary. In Idaho, **76 % of recaptures were in the same pool**. Radio-tagged tributary residents in Arkansas had a median linear home range of **0.3 km**, and no Wisconsin fish moved more than **200 m** in summer ([Hafs 2007](https://www.bemidjistate.edu/directory/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2023/02/Hafs-A.W.-2007.-Smallmouth-bass-survival-movement-and-habitat-use-in-response-to-seasonally-discontinuous-surface-flow.-Ma.pdf)). At about 200 m, the whole brook is one stream bass's home range.

Depth then sorts the fish by size. Minnesota stream smallmouth used mean depths of **0.85 m as adults, 0.57 m as juveniles and 0.34 m in their first year** (Aadland 1993, via [Hafs 2007](https://www.bemidjistate.edu/directory/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2023/02/Hafs-A.W.-2007.-Smallmouth-bass-survival-movement-and-habitat-use-in-response-to-seasonally-discontinuous-surface-flow.-Ma.pdf)). That is almost exactly the brook's 1.1-m pool, 0.5-m run and 0.3-m riffles. Large bass held pools deeper than 50 cm on **72 of 75 pool-dates** ([Power 1987](https://ib.berkeley.edu/labs/power/publications/Power_1987_PredatorAvoidance.pdf)).

The angler asked whether the bass live in the brook or only spawn there. The answer is both, in different fish. In Lake Ontario's tributaries, stream-spawning smallmouth "usually set up home ranges downstream of spawning areas and often stayed in tributaries all summer" ([Lake Ontario tributary telemetry, 1988](https://soar.suny.edu/entities/publication/cef37268-63b2-4fbf-9e14-87e9fea7c510), seen as a search summary). At Millers Lake, Ontario, lake residents and river spawners coexisted. Most males kept to one or the other for life, and about half of repeat nesters nested within 20 m of their old nest ([Barthel et al. 2008](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/divergent-life-histories-among-smallmouth-bass-inhabiting-a-connected-river-lake-system)). In the Lake Washington Ship Canal, **82 %** of tagged bass left for the lake between June and October, and fish **under 350 mm were the likeliest to stay** in the channel ([Tabor et al. 2012](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/Northwest-Science/volume-86/issue-2/046.086.0205/Seasonal-Movements-of-Smallmouth-Bass-in-the-Lake-Washington-Ship/10.3955/046.086.0205.full)). Lyons & Kanehl's review adds that young-of-year and age-1 fish do not migrate. Adults leave streams below about **15.6 °C**, "particularly when accompanied by rainfall and increased flows" ([Lyons & Kanehl 2002, as quoted](https://paflyfish.com/threads/stream-smallmouth-bass-migratory-behavior.14680)).

The brook should therefore hold three kinds of bass. A few lake bass nest in the run or pool tail, and some of them stay all summer below their nests. Sub-adults use the brook as a summer range. Lake residents only visit the pool to feed. Bass in the Ontario tributaries were less active than in the lake and least active in July, which fits sit-and-wait fish holding one pool.

### Spawners arrive on a temperature calendar

The brook's spring is a sequence of runs keyed to temperature. White suckers spawn on tributary gravel bars at about **10 °C**, from mid-April to May ([Northfield relicensing, Table 1](https://www.northfield-relicensing.com/content/Documents/2015_USRS_3_3_13.pdf)). Fallfish start at **15 °C**, do best at 17 °C and stop if the water falls back below 15 °C. Maine fishery workers found that fallfish "move from larger waters into streams to spawn" ([Trial et al. 1983, fallfish HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-48.pdf)). White perch spawn in April–May at **10–15.5 °C**, in shoreline shallows or "the larger tributaries" ([Mass.gov, white perch](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/white-perch)), and they were rare in outflow traps ([Elser](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/ELSER-174.pdf)).

Smallmouth enter Lake Erie's tributaries in mid-to-late April, three to four weeks before nesting, and stay through May ([NYSDEC](https://dec.ny.gov/things-to-do/freshwater-fishing/places-to-fish/great-lakes-niagara-river-st-lawrence-river/lake-erie-smallmouth-bass-fishing)). Hot Brook's bass nest at 15–16 °C in late May, so their brook entry falls in early to mid-May (inference). Blacknose dace begin spawning on riffle gravel at **21 °C** in the north ([Trial et al. 1983, dace HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-41.pdf)). That is about 20 June on the sim's calendar, after the fallfish window, so the two spawning crowds do not overlap.

**Chain pickerel are the exception: no evidence was found of pickerel spawning runs into streams.** They scatter eggs over vegetation at 8–11 °C and leave stream channels in floods ([Fishes of Texas](https://txstate.fishesoftexas.org/esox%20niger.htm)). Their spawning habitat is flooded lake margin and marsh, which Hot Brook's cobble brook does not offer.

### The passing schools are most likely young fallfish

The angler sees schools of small minnows crossing between the lakes in summer and is unsure whether they are young fallfish or dace. The evidence points to **young fallfish**. Blacknose dace are Connecticut's commonest fish of small headwater streams, "typically absent from large rivers and ponds" ([CT DEEP, blacknose dace](https://portal.ct.gov/DEEP/Fishing/Freshwater/Freshwater-Fishes-of-Connecticut/Blacknose-Dace)). They are bottom-oriented riffle fish, and no study describes them schooling. Minnows crossing riffles in artificial streams went mostly singly, in a mean group of **1.2 fish** ([Schaefer 2001](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/9696)). In Adirondack lakes, dace were among the minnows less likely to occur where pike or bass lived. Native minnow richness in those lakes was about **one-third** of that in lakes without such predators ([Findlay et al. 2000](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=effect%20of%20introduced%20piscivores%20on%20native%20minnow%20communities%20in%20Adirondack%20lakes)). Open lake water full of pickerel and bass is the last place a dace school would travel.

Young fallfish are silvery, with a dusky side band ending in a spot at the tail base and coarse scales (fewer than 50 in the lateral line). They favour faster current than adults, and adults and young-of-year "disperse downstream" after spawning ([VT fallfish sheet](https://VTFISHANDWILDLIFE.COM/sites/fishandwildlife/files/documents/Fish/baitfish/fallfish.pdf); [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Semotilus_corporalis/); [MassWildlife](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/fallfish), seen as a search summary). The angler can settle the question on sight. A dace has a **jet-black band from the tip of the snout through the eye**, tiny scales (53–70) and a small mouth under an overhanging snout ([VT dace sheet](https://VTFISHANDWILDLIFE.COM/sites/fishandwildlife/files/documents/Fish/baitfish/blacknose-dace.pdf); [FishBase](https://www.fishbase.se/summary/2943)). Hot Brook's list holds no shiners or creek chub. Silvery mid-water schools are therefore young fallfish, young-of-year yellow perch (with vertical bars) or young suckers, not dace.

### The angler's summer matches the literature point by point

Each of the angler's observations has a documented counterpart. Mostly redbreast, 18–21 cm, staying in the pools: the current-tolerant stream sunfish, the population's oldest fish, faithful to home pools for years. Smaller bass staying: the canal bass under 350 mm that stayed in the channel, and the juvenile stream depth of 0.57 m. Larger bass in the deep pool by the upper lake, apparently waiting for minnow schools: the outlet funnel described below, compounded by the large bass's need for water deeper than 50 cm, which the brook offers only in that pool. Pickerel "passing through and hanging out": the one telemetry study of chain pickerel movement beyond a lake (next sections). And the uncertainty over whether bass live there or only spawn reflects a real split between individual fish. Nothing in his account conflicts with the published record. Where the literature is silent, as on the identity of the schools, his account is the best data available.

## Fish visit the brook often but change lakes rarely

### The only natural northeastern chain moved 0.9 % of recaptured fish in two years

Daniels et al. tagged fish in three Adirondack lakes in the Moose River watershed, New York. The lakes average 63 ha and lie a mean 3.5 km apart, joined by streams. Of **33,875 fish tagged** from the mid-1990s, **4,649 were recaptured**. Of these, **43 turned up in a lake other than the one they were tagged in: 31 white suckers (about 2.7 % of sucker recaptures), 8 brown bullheads, 2 yellow perch, 1 pumpkinseed and 1 rock bass.** Time at large before a fish appeared in another lake ranged from **21 to 3,251 days, with a mean of about 715**. Moves went both ways: **24 upstream, 15 downstream**, and 5 between the two upstream lakes. Stocked largemouth bass spread about **one lake every 2–5 years**, while stocked smallmouth were **never** caught outside their stocking lake ([Daniels et al. 2008](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml)). The movement rate is **0.9 % of recaptured fish over about two years, roughly 0.5 % a year**, and nine in ten movers were suckers or bullheads.

**This corrects the project's earlier note.** That note took "about 1 % of tagged fish" from a popular summary ([Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/news/fish-gotta-swim)). It then argued that Hot Brook's short brook should be far more permeable and proposed **5–25 % a year** for most species. The new note restates the paper's figure as **about 0.13 % of all tagged fish**. **That number should not enter the simulation.** About 86 % of tagged fish were never seen again, so their movements are unknown. Dividing the 43 movers by every tagged fish counts each unrecaptured fish as a stayer, which makes 0.13 % a floor, not a rate. The defensible reading is about 0.9 % of fish with a known fate, over a mean of two years, between lakes 3.5 km apart. The paper's own recapture rate is also unresolved: it prints 20.4 %, while 4,649 / 33,875 is 13.7 %.

Hot Brook's lakes are only about 200 m apart, and connectivity raises movement. In a 10-lake Wisconsin chain, walleye movement rose from **11 % to 21 % to 50 %** as a lake's connections rose from one to three. Muskellunge moved so freely that the author recommended managing them chain-wide ([Weeks 2006](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/81151)). Hot Brook's real rates should therefore sit above the Adirondack ones, by an amount nobody has measured.

The earlier note's 10–25 % for perch rested on two sources. One was a 16-lake Minnesota chain, where bluegill emigrated at 3–36 % a year by angler returns ([Parsons & Reed 2005](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1577/M04-021.1)). The other was Seattle's urban canal, where 80 % of tagged perch moved lakes ([WRIA 8 presentation](https://www.govlink.org/watersheds/8/committees/2305/WRIA8_predator_presentation_May-18-2023.pdf)). The new note suggests both connections are wide, navigable channels, unlike a riffled brook. Neither is a natural northeastern brook.

### Riffles act as behavioural barriers, and size and distance pick the movers

Gerking's Indiana riffle shows how a short brook can still separate two populations. Only 5 of 191 longears crossed it in four years, yet **26 of 35 longears transplanted across it went back over it within two weeks**: "the riffle was a behavioral rather than a mechanical barrier" ([Gerking 1953](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf)). Hot Brook's brook has two riffles. Lake fish are just as attached to their home areas. Of **4,557 fish marked at one point in Douglas Lake, Michigan, not one** was recovered anywhere else in the lake (Rodeheffer, via Gerking). In Lake Mattamuskeet, North Carolina, bass from the west side of the lake were never detected on the east side ([NCWRC 2021](https://ncwildlife.gov/media/3109/download)).

The realistic candidates for crossing are therefore fish whose home areas overlap Upper's outlet basin or Lower's inflow mouth. The simulation already weights crossing that way: the chance falls by a factor of e for each 1 km from the brook's mouth (`F3.ROAM_L`).

Size matters too. Stray longears were larger than resident fish in all four of Gerking's years. Nine in ten Adirondack movers were suckers and bullheads, and larger suckers were the nightly commuters between two Adirondack lakes ([Marcy-Quay & Kraft 2021](https://eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-28-4/11-Marcy-Quay.shtml)). The Minnesota bluegill chain found no size effect, so the size evidence is mixed but leans toward larger fish moving.

### Young fish leave Upper in spring and in storm pulses

Most fish that leave a lake through its outlet are young. The best counts come from catch-boxes below the spillways of four soft-water Maryland ponds (26–45 acres, 2–4 ft mean depth), which caught everything going over ([Elser](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/ELSER-174.pdf)). At Wye Lake only **3 % of 4,899 escaping largemouth** were of legal size. "The greatest loss is in the spring of the year, especially May": **72–85 % of a season's outflow left in April–May** at two of the ponds, and **82 % of the bass leaving Wye Lake in July 1959 left in a single storm.** Pumpkinseed went the other way in the calendar, leaving in autumn: 52 of 53 at one pond went in September–October. Bass were over-represented among the leavers, while golden shiners "seem to prefer to stay". The outlet's form mattered as much as anything: losses ran from **1–6 fish per acre per season** over turbulent drop-board weirs to **85–223** over smooth spillways.

A review of 57 papers on escapement from North American reservoirs found annual escapement ranging from 0 to 100 %. It was generally higher at higher discharge, and smaller fish escaped at higher rates ([Lewis et al. 2023](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/reservoir-fish-escapement-in-north-america-a-historical-review-an/)).

Hot Brook's outlet is a natural, turbulent riffle, closer to Elser's low-loss ponds. Scaled to Upper's ~925 acres, the low-loss rate gives roughly **1,000–5,500 fish a year**, mostly age-0 and age-1 sunfish and bass (inference). That flux should scale with the fish living near the outlet and with discharge, not with lake area. These dam counts are one-way, though. Adult moves through Hot Brook can go upstream as easily as down, as the Adirondack split of 24 up and 15 down shows.

### Upper and Lower are two linked populations, not one

The literature's working model is a set of local populations rescued now and then by strays. Across Wisconsin and Finland, how hard each lake is to persist in (habitat severity, area and productivity) predicted its species composition better than its isolation did ([Magnuson et al. 1998](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/handle/123456789/15898)). Piscivore assemblages persisted in low-oxygen Wisconsin lakes only "when a stream or connecting lake offered refuge" ([Tonn & Magnuson 1982](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/handle/123456789/15993)). In a Minnesota chain, larval crappie catch failed to predict year-class strength in two connected lakes because of "supplemental recruitment caused by emigration from other lakes" ([Parsons et al. 2004](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2005/other/050139.pdf)).

For Hot Brook this means the two lakes should share a species list but be free to differ in size structure, abundance and year-class strength. The angler's small, sparse perch could be a property of one lake. Recolonising a lake after a local loss should take years, as the Adirondack largemouth did, and should pause in drought years when the brook closes.

## Predators wait where a lake funnels its prey

### Outlet pools are documented hotspots, and Maine's ambushers are pickerel and smallmouth

Wherever migrating or drifting prey are squeezed through a narrow passage, piscivores gather and feed hard. At the outlet of Chilko Lake, British Columbia, **20 of 22 bull trout had food in their stomachs**, almost all of it outmigrating sockeye smolts, at up to **12.6 % of their body mass** ([Furey et al. 2015](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/extensive-feeding-sockeye-salmon-oncorhynchus-nerka-smolts-bull-trout-salvelinus)). Acoustic telemetry there marked the first river segment below the lake as "a high-risk landscape associated with poor survival", and smolts survived better when passing among many other smolts ([Furey thesis](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0307167)). In Washington's Cedar River, sculpin predation on sockeye fry was substantially higher in pools deeper than 2.5 m than in riffles ([Tabor & Peters 2016](https://bioone.org/journals/Northwestern-Naturalist/volume-97/issue-3/NWN15-09.1/Diet-of-the-Torrent-Sculpin-Cottus-rhotheus-in-the-Cedar/10.1898/NWN15-09.1.full)). At a Brazilian dam, the fish-ladder entrance held the most large predators, and tagged predators fed there for up to 90 days ([Agostinho et al. 2012](https://scielo.br/j/ni/a/FZrX3DpHSRyXnyZ4WNLjbwR/?lang=en)).

The closest evidence comes from Maine. At the Weldon Dam impoundment on the Penobscot River, tethered Atlantic salmon smolts were "exclusively predated by" **chain pickerel (43) and smallmouth bass (42)**. **23 % were taken within one hour.** Predation rose from **10 % to 33 % as the water warmed from 5 to 15 °C**, and smolts **within 40 m of shore** were at above-average risk ([Mensinger et al. 2025](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/smallmouth-bass-micropterus-dolomieu-and-chain-pickerel-esox-niger-identified-atlantic)). Older Maine work likewise named "dead water areas and pools in lower rivers" as where pickerel and bass ambush young salmon ([KRIS Sheepscot](http://www.krisweb.com/krissheepscot/krisdb/html/krisweb/analysis/hypoth_6_exotics_sheep.htm)). The angler's big bass in the deep pool at Upper's outlet fits all three parts of this pattern: prey funnelled out of a lake, the deepest available water, and a spot close to shore.

The meal can be large. Smallmouth in a Columbia River reservoir averaged a daily ration of **28.7 mg per gram of body mass**, or 2.9 % a day ([Vigg et al. 1991](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/rates-consumption-juvenile-salmonids-and-alternative-pray-fish-northern-squawfish)). A predator holding up to 12.6 % of its body mass after one prey pulse therefore carries about four days' ration (cross-species arithmetic). That is a bass that fills up when a school passes and then sits and digests, one an angler would describe as "waiting". The gap is plain, though. No northeastern warmwater study counted bass or pickerel at a lake-outlet pool against the open lake, so the size of the concentration is unknown.

Prey move in ways that blunt the funnel. In Bride Lake, Connecticut, **more than 80 % of about 320,000 juvenile alewives left in three pulses of one to two days**. The pulses were cued by rain, brief temperature drops and rising discharge, with fish moving around dawn early in the season and at night later ([Gahagan et al. 2010](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/eeb_articles/23)). Sockeye fry mortality to trout fell as water velocity and turbidity rose ([Ginetz](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/831/items/1.0101880)). Minnows crossed riffles **three times as often with predators in the pools** (7.2 % to 20.2 % per 30 minutes), and in larger groups ([Schaefer 2001](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/9696)). An outlet pool's attraction should therefore rise and fall with the flux of prey, not be a fixed property of the pool.

### Pickerel pass through and linger in summer, not spring

The one telemetry study of chain pickerel movement beyond a lake tracked **30 acoustic-tagged fish (31–62 cm)** for a year in George Lake, Nova Scotia. It recorded "extended summer migrations beyond George Lake" and winter inactivity, with distinct habitat use by individual fish. No counts or distances are published ([Sauriol et al. 2024](https://merseytobeatic.ca/sites/merseytobeatic.ca/files/agendafiles/Sauriol_Felixe.pdf)).

Surrogate species show the same structure: a sedentary majority and a larger-bodied mobile minority. Most grass pickerel in Ontario drains moved less than 500 m, but **16 % moved farther (up to 13.5 km), and the mobile fish were larger** ([Movement Ecology 2023](https://movementecologyjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40462-023-00420-2)). Northern pike in a small Colorado river stayed within about a river mile of their capture pool, moving pool to pool through riffles, most actively after dusk ([Colorado CWCB](https://dnrweblink.state.co.us/CWCB/0/doc/98734/Page40.aspx)). In winter, fewer than **2 %** of a Danish lake's pike and perch followed their prey into its streams ([Skov et al. 2008](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/inter-and-size-specific-patterns-of-fish-seasonal-migration-betwe/)).

Agencies place chain pickerel in "small stream pool habitats" with vegetation and wood ([WVDNR](https://wvdnr.gov/plants-animals/sportfish/chain-pickerel/)), lying motionless along vegetated margins or undercut banks ([Outdoor Alabama](https://www.outdooralabama.com/pickerel/chain-pickerel)).

That is the angler's "passing through and hanging out", and it is a **summer** behaviour. The earlier note modelled pickerel on northern pike's spring spawning runs through Riley Creek, Minnesota ([Chizinski et al. 2016](https://rpbcwd.org/files/0c215a59d/chizinskiEtAl2016_differentMigratoryStrategies_Pike_Carp.pdf)). The simulation copies it: 15 % of pickerel make a 28-day trip at ice-out (`MOVE3.pike`). No evidence supports such a run for chain pickerel, and Hot Brook's cobble brook lacks the flooded grass pike spawn in.

### Dace survive predators by holding water large fish do not hunt

Small-bodied fish and bass rarely share a lake. Central Ontario lakes with smallmouth held **2.3 fewer small-bodied species**, and four of six vulnerable species never coexisted with bass. The two that did used complex habitat by day and fed in the open at low light ([MacRae & Jackson 2001](https://harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202000s/MacRae%20and%20Jackson%202001.pdf)).

In streams, dace live beside pickerel, bass and trout through three behaviours. First, juvenile dace and creek chub "actively avoid locations … that contain predators", entering them mostly "during daylight or when structure is present" ([Fraser & Cerri 1982](https://agris.fao.org/search/zh/records/65de3a467c7033e84bea221b)), and predators drove dace into other compartments ([Fraser & Emmons 1984](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1139/f84-039)). Second, with bass present, juveniles were confined to shallow riffles and raceways ([Schlosser 1987](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfb19fb766d82b1802e4d7)). Third, the predators stay in the pools. The cost of shallow water comes from birds instead. All six tethered fish in water shallower than 18 cm were taken by herons or kingfishers within 24 hours, while 10 of 14 survived in deeper water ([Power 1987](https://ib.berkeley.edu/labs/power/publications/Power_1987_PredatorAvoidance.pdf)).

The simulation already gives the brook's dace (0.8 per m², about 1,400 fish) a strike cover of 0.9 in water under 0.6 m. It also holds fish of 10 cm or more in water of 0.6 m or deeper (`F3.RIFFLE_REFUGE`, `RIFFLE_D`, `RIFFLE_L` in `src/w3/fish3.js`). Yet pickerel, bass and fallfish still take **about 1,230 of the ~1,400 a year**. Blacknose dace live three to four years, and few reach age III ([Trial et al. 1983, dace HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-41.pdf)). That implies roughly **65–80 % adult mortality a year from all causes together**: fish, birds, disease and starvation (inference). **The sim's 88 % to fish alone exceeds that whole band before any other cause takes its share.**

The literature points to three missing pieces. Every lake fish may range into the brook, whereas real brook predators are a few pool residents plus a mobile minority of pickerel. Real stream bass and pickerel hold pools and slow margins and do not work riffle water. And the sim's dace do not avoid patches that hold large fish. A dace population has no rescue. Recruits from Upper's inlets or Lower's outlet would have to cross a lake full of predators, so the brook's dace must persist on their own (inference).

## Upper Hot Brook Lake feeds and warms the brook

### Lake plankton is stripped in the first 200 m, mostly by fish by day

A lake outlet is a conveyor of lake food, and the catch is front-loaded. In Polish outlets of Hot Brook's size (4–6 m wide, 0.2–0.4 m deep, 0.22–0.30 m/s), large cladocerans fell by **61–75 %** and copepods by **62–68 % over 200 m**. The fall was greatest **by day**, only 15–31 % at night and dawn. The authors attribute it mainly to fish predation by cyprinid fry, with filter-feeding invertebrates having a "rather small" impact ([PMC4938256](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4938256/)).

In 12 Colorado alpine outlets, **95 % of exported zooplankton biomass was gone within 500 m**, fastest in the first 10 m, and export was higher at night. **Fishless lakes exported five times more**, because lake fish suppress large zooplankton, and fish in the outlet streams lowered macroinvertebrate biomass ([Detmer et al. 2017](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/68860)). At about 41 % per 100 m, the brook strips roughly two-thirds of Upper's crustacean export over its length (derived). Brook fish should therefore eat lake zooplankton by day, then switch at dusk and through the night to invertebrate drift, which peaks after dusk.

### Mussels and filter feeders crowd just below the outlet

Net-spinning caddis reached **6,500 per m²** at a Montana lake outlet, against 1,427 at 200 m and 209 at 3.4 km ([Valett & Stanford 1987](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/food-quality-and-hydropsychid-caddisfly-density-in-a-lake-outlet-/)). Below Idaho outlets, filter feeders **peaked 40–160 m below the lake**, where the current reached about 0.3 m/s ([Robinson & Minshall 1990](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/gbn/vol50/iss4/3)). In six Maine lake–outlet pairs, filter-feeder biomass did not track the amount of suspended food. That points to current, attachment space and food quality as the limits ([Demi et al. 2012](https://bioone.org/journals/freshwater-science/volume-31/issue-3/11-124.1/Anadromous-alewives-in-linked-lakestream-ecosystems--do-trophic-interactions/10.1899/11-124.1.full)).

Unionid mussels are new to the project's notes, and they make lake outlets hotspots. A 35-year European study of a stream flowing through five lakes found **up to 290 mussels per m² (14.7 kg/m²) just below the lakes**, declining downstream as a power function. An estimated **1.9 million mussels in 3.2 km** filtered the equivalent of the stream's whole discharge about **2.5 times a day** ([2021 outlet-hotspot study](https://inis.iaea.org/records/8bv40-we758), seen as a search summary). Zebra mussels show the same source shape, falling from **more than 1,000 per m² at a lake outlet to about 10 per m² within 1 km** ([Horvath et al. 1996](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/zebra-mussel-dispersal-in-lake-stream-systems-source-sink-dynamic/)). The angler reports "lots of mussels" in the brook, which fits. No Maine measurement of *Elliptio* at a lake outlet was found.

### The brook runs at Upper's surface temperature

In southern Quebec, natural lakes warmed their outlet streams by **3.4 °C on average in August**, and distance to an upstream water body was among the main controls of stream temperature ([Auffray et al. 2023](https://espace.inrs.ca/id/eprint/15420/)). Water crosses Hot Brook's ~200 m in about 11–17 minutes at 0.2–0.3 m/s, too fast to change temperature much (derived). The brook is therefore Upper's surface water: about 24 °C at the late-July peak, with no downstream gradient at normal flow.

Across 100 lakes, area alone explained 83 % of the summer daily surface-temperature range. Below a change point of **3.2 km²**, small lakes swing **4–7 °C** a day, against about 1 °C for large ones ([Woolway et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4811584/)). Upper (3.7 km²) sits just above that change point, so a daily swing of about 1.5–3 °C in the brook is a reasonable estimate (inference). Below a lake in Washington, some deep pools were cooler than the rest of the stream ([Gendaszek et al. 2022](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20225063)), so Hot Brook's 1.1-m pool is the only cool spot, and only slightly. A Maine survey sheet calls one pond's outlet "warm and marshy, providing little brook trout habitat" ([MDIFW Mountain Pond](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/piscataquis/mountain_pond_t10r10.pdf)). The lake also damps and delays the brook's flow. A Maine lake outlet still ran at **2.4 times its mean flow in May and 1.05 times in June** ([USGS NWIS](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01012515,01035500&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)).

### The brook can carry roughly 20–145 kg of fish

Warmwater pool–riffle streams carry about **410–840 kg of fish per hectare**, with sunfish and bass **5–28 %** of the weight ([Big Walnut Creek, Indiana](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/ias/article/download/6856/6863/12978)). Catskill streams averaged **11.2 g/m²** ([Mulvihill et al. 2009](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2009/5047/includes/SIR2009-5047.pdf)). Over the brook's 1,725 m², that comes to roughly **20–145 kg of fish in all and 1–40 kg of sunfish and small bass** (derived). As 110–180 g redbreast, that is tens to at most a few hundred fish.

Depth sorts that capacity. In drying Ozark creeks of Hot Brook's width (5.4–8.2 m), **total fish density fell as maximum pool depth rose**. The authors read this as small fish avoiding the large predators in deep pools ([Dekar & Magoulick 2007](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf)). No study has compared fish density in a lake-connecting stream with its lakes.

## A dry year turns the brook into a trap

### The outlet stops at a sill, not gradually

A natural lake outlet is a threshold. In a Canadian lake chain, snowmelt lifted the lakes above their outlet elevations, then summer evaporation drew them below, and outflow ceased. Summer rain "was insufficient to overcome storage deficit to re-establish flow connectivity" ([Woo & Mielko 2007](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1727611)). Northeastern droughts are not rare. In 2016 the Saco River at Cornish, Maine, had its **lowest 7-day flow in a 99-year record** ([NRCC 2016](https://www.nrcc.cornell.edu/regional/drought/pubs/assessment_2016.pdf)), and in 2020 Maine DEP warned of dewatered stream segments and fish kills ([Maine DEP](https://maine.gov/dep/news/news.html?id=3157594)). The angler saw parts of Hot Brook nearly dry, and the project holds his photographs of a dry August (2025). The simulation fixes water level and brook depth, so it cannot produce this.

### Riffles close first, then the pool crowds, heats and loses oxygen

The riffles are the shallowest water, so passage stops there first. No biological passage depth exists for these species. Culvert guidance asks for at least **10–15 cm** for non-trout fish ([UW-Extension](https://www3.uwsp.edu/cnr-ap/UWEXLakes/Documents/ecology/shoreland/background/fish_friendly_culverts.pdf), seen as a search summary). Once surface flow vanishes into the cobble, the brook is a complete barrier. In northern Sweden, "subsurface streamflow through bouldery areas" was the commonest reason lakes predicted to have pike lacked them ([Spens et al. 2007](https://research.slu.se/sv/publications/network-connectivity-and-dispersal-barriers-using-geographical-in/)). Fish do not wait to be stranded. Adult Arctic grayling left a small connecting stream **as discharge fell** ([Heim et al. 2016](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70168356)).

Radio-tagged smallmouth in an Arkansas stream that dried into pools show what happens to bass ([Hafs 2007](https://www.bemidjistate.edu/directory/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2023/02/Hafs-A.W.-2007.-Smallmouth-bass-survival-movement-and-habitat-use-in-response-to-seasonally-discontinuous-surface-flow.-Ma.pdf); [Hafs et al. 2010](https://academic.oup.com/najfm/article/30/2/604/7846726)). Wetted area fell by more than 55 %. Fish moved away from drying reaches, 10 % of them more than 1 km, and mean movement fell from 270 m in May to 54 m by July. Summer survival was **67 % in the most-dried fork, against 79–84 %** in the others. Two fish were trapped when their pools isolated; their transmitters were later found on the dry pool beds.

As pools shrink, fish crowd into them. In Ozark creeks, fish density peaked partway through drying ([Dekar & Magoulick 2007](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf)). In a Kansas drought, a pool of under an acre held about **40,000 fish**, and numbers fell "to correspond to the carrying capacity of each pool". Clearer water there increased predation on young fish ([Deacon 1961](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34787/34787-h/34787-h.htm)). Smallmouth added to drying pools pushed small minnows and crayfish into the shallows, where land predators took the larger individuals ([Dekar & Magoulick 2013](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70150315)). Drying Arkansas pools reached **31–32 °C** ([Dekar & Magoulick 2007](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf)). Hot Brook's isolated pool would start from Upper's 24 °C, without the fresh lake water that kept it in step with the lake.

The lake's food subsidy stops too. Low flow cuts riffle insects but not pool insects, and filter feeders are hit hardest ([Walters & Post 2011](https://wyocoopunit.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Walters_and_Post_2011.pdf)). The prey pulse also stops. Juvenile alewives in Connecticut were "having difficulty leaving the pond they were born in" when the outlet stream dried ([Long Island Sound Study](https://lispartnership.org/?p=30640)). In a dry year, the pool's predators are therefore most crowded exactly when the schools that fed them stop coming.

### Fish return about two weeks after the water does

In Smiths Branch, a small Illinois warmwater stream that dried in 1953, **21 of the 29 regular species had moved back into most of the stream within two weeks of full flow**, and 25 by the end of the first summer. Longear sunfish took about two years, and two small species had still not returned four years later. Fish crossed "very shallow riffles during two brief periods of slight flow" ([Larimore et al. 1959](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1994573246)). Hot Brook has a permanent source lake at each end, so its recolonisation should run at the fast end of that range, from both directions.

### Beaver dams would change the picture

Beaver dams are flow-dependent barriers. Passage improves at high flow and for larger fish ([Needham et al. 2025](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/500916)). Juvenile coho passage over dam analogues fell from **76 % in early summer to 21 % in late summer** ([Pollock et al.](https://scottriver.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Do-beaver-dam-analogues-act-as-passage-barriers-to-juvenile-coho.pdf)). A Maine survey calls beaver dams in a pond outlet barriers "during periods of low flows", with fish "trapped when water is low" ([MDIFW Little Fish Pond](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/somerset/fish_pond_little.pdf)). Whether Hot Brook has had beaver dams is unknown.

### What to keep

Several parts of the brook model already match the evidence. **Dace density** of 0.8 per m² sits inside the 0.03–5.2 per m² measured in Catskill and Mohawk tributaries ([Mulvihill et al. 2009](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2009/5047/includes/SIR2009-5047.pdf); [George et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3133/sir20225121)), and the dace rightly stay brook-only, with no rescue from the lakes. The **fallfish nest cap** of about 150 spawners at once is a nest-site mechanism that nothing in the new notes contradicts. The **sucker run** and the brook's **plankton, filter-feeder and benthos supply** stand. **Depth sorting by size** in the brook, the rule that bigger fish hold deeper water, matches Power's pools, Aadland's depths by age and the angler's big bass in the pool. **Distance weighting** of crossing from the brook's mouth matches Mattamuskeet and Douglas Lake. And the **summer pool use by pumpkinseed (30 %) and redbreast (50 %)** living within 600 m keeps redbreast the majority, as the angler reports.

That last rule needs four additions. First, make it density-dependent. Gerking saw overcrowding push residents out: 37 of 39 fish were at home after two weeks, but only 23 of 33 after six. Second, bias it toward large fish, and check it against the 1–40 kg capacity. Third, add a pumpkinseed exit in September–October. Fourth, if the pool stays open and oxygenated, let some redbreast winter there, where the species forms "large aggregations … in low velocity, pool habitats" ([Aho et al. 1986](https://19january2017snapshot.epa.gov/www3/region1/npdes/merrimackstation/pdfs/ar/AR-783.pdf)).

### What remains unknown

The largest gaps sit at the heart of the question. **No movement study** has followed smallmouth, pickerel, white perch, redbreast, fallfish or dace through a brook under 1 km long joining two natural lakes. **No counts** exist of bass or pickerel in an outlet pool against the lake, or of minnow schools passing through a connecting stream, and fallfish have **no telemetry** at all. **No passage depth** has been measured for these species; the 10–15 cm figure is engineering guidance. And **Hot Brook itself is unmeasured**: its sill elevation, stage, discharge and dry-year riffle depths are unknown, as are its mussel density, whether beavers have ever dammed it, and whether its pool stays open and oxygenated under ice.

The angler can supply several of these quickly: whether the passing schools carry a black snout-to-eye stripe, how many bass hold the upper pool when they run, how shallow the riffles get in a dry August, and whether beavers have dammed the brook.

## Conclusion

The brook's fish are better described by **capacity and flux** than by a crossing rate. Three things decide who is in the brook at a given moment: **depth**, which decides which sizes can hold where; **each fish's own tactic** (resident, seasonal visitor or lake fish passing through), which the evidence says is individual and often lifelong; and **the flow of prey and water** through the brook, which decides when predators gather and when the passage closes.

Seen this way, the sim's two current brook puzzles are one mechanism. The angler's big bass waiting in the upper pool and the sim's vanishing dace both come from predators going where prey are funnelled. Real prey survive by holding water the predators will not enter and by passing in pulses that swamp them. The fix is therefore behavioural: who goes into the brook, and where they hunt. Tuning a cover number until the dace survive would not reproduce how real streams work.

The dry year is the one process the angler has seen that the sim cannot produce, and it is also the strongest test of the design. Suppose fish leave the riffles as flow falls, crowd the pool, lose the lake's food and prey pulse, suffer heat, low oxygen and predation, and pour back within weeks of the first autumn rain, all without being scripted. Then the brook will be doing what real lake-connecting streams do.
