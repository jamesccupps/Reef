# Hot Brook's land arrives mostly as water

Upper Hot Brook Lake covers 360 hectares inside 14.5 km of spruce–fir shore. Around a lake that size, the land reaches the water mostly through its hydrology, not across the waterline. Snowmelt and storms deliver the catchment's phosphorus, colour and sediment in a few weeks a year. Half of the January–June runoff in the surrounding basin has passed by **15–18 April**, small streams run at **3.3 times their mean flow in April and 0.2–0.3 times in late August**, and up to **86% of a forested catchment's dissolved organic carbon (DOC)** moves in events. Snow on the ice then decides the winter: Maine lakes pass **1–12% of surface light** under snow and ice, against the sim's fixed 40%, and that difference sets under-ice oxygen and winterkill in a pond 2.7 m deep on average. Food from the shore itself (land insects, leaves, pollen) is real but local. Spread over the whole of Upper Hot Brook, land insects come to under 1% of what the lake's own emerging insects carry out (derived). In the alder-roofed connecting stream, though, the same inputs are the base of the food web, as in a New Hampshire brook where more than 99% of the energy came from the land. The shore's largest effects on fish are structural and predatory. Undeveloped northern shores hold about **555 logs per km**, and removing three-quarters of one lake's logs nearly eliminated its yellow perch. Herons, kingfishers and raccoons make water shallower than about **20 cm** lethal by day for fish longer than 3–7 cm, which leaves the stream's 1.1 m pools as its only refuge. Turtles, frogs, birds and mammals cross the waterline on a calendar anchored on ice-out (about 23–28 April), and several of the sim's dates are wrong: northern painted turtle hatchlings mostly emerge the following spring, not in August. The sim should add land at its two ends (a rocky till bank on the lake, a fen margin on the pond), treat the stream's alder banks and the lakes' far shores as source terms, and give the air a weather state on the calendar clock. Every per-metre-of-shore flux must be divided by 10 or more, because the slice has 10–14 times more shoreline per square metre of water than the real lakes. The build order, by ecological effect, is: catchment water and loads, colour, snow on the ice, shore predators, shore wood, then insects, leaves, shade, wind, turtles and frogs.

## Rock, fen and alder frame Hot Brook's waterline

### Hot Brook's shores are mostly rocky till, with the wetlands at the far ends

Maine's fisheries agency (MDIFW) describes both Hot Brook lakes the same way: spruce–fir forest all round, a shoreline "mostly rocky" with some sand and mud, and a mostly muddy bottom. **Upper Hot Brook Lake has its wetlands on the south and west sides, a large sandy beach in the northeast and a row of camps along the south shore**, with an unimproved boat launch off Route 169 ([MDIFW, Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf)). **Lower Hot Brook Lake has its wetlands on the north and east sides and, at the 2004 revision, no camps.** Its only access is the rocky, hard-bottomed connecting stream, about 200 m long, which boaters must paddle, pole or walk through ([MDIFW, Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)). A commercial land listing says more than half of Lower remains undeveloped, which implies some building since 2004; it is unverified ([land listing](https://uclandforsale.com/properties/lot-25-woodland-ridge-rd-danforth-maine-04424/)). The Lakes of Maine database gives Upper a 14.5 km perimeter, 360 ha and a mean depth of 3.35 m, and Lower 10.8 km, 277 ha and 2.74 m ([Lakes of Maine, Upper](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1076); [Lakes of Maine, Lower](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1072)). Those figures give shoreline development factors of about 2.2 and 1.8 (derived): irregular glacial outlines with points and coves rather than round basins. Both lakes have **about 4 m of shoreline per 1,000 m² of water** (derived). That ratio decides how much any shore-borne flux can matter across a whole lake.

No Maine survey gives the share of lake perimeter in each shore type, bank heights or beach widths. National Lakes Assessment habitat data behind a 2023 Maine littoral-habitat study hold them, but were not accessible ([Deeds et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10642257/)). What does exist is qualitative but consistent. The Maine Natural Areas Program (MNAP) calls the sand-and-cobble Lakeshore Beach the most frequent lakeshore community on medium and large Maine lakes. Ice push, waves and drawdown make its open bands and berms, and the berms carry wave-cast wrack lines ([MNAP, Lakeshore Beach](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/lakeshorebeach.htm)). Wave and ice exposure combined with water-level swings strips shrubs and leaves a herb-rich open shore ([Keddy, Shorelines](https://drpaulkeddy.com/conservation/shorelines/)). Marsh and fen form only on sheltered, shallow, non-rocky margins ([Nova Scotia, lakeshore wetlands](https://novascotia.ca/natr/wildlife/habitats/nswetlands/lakeshore.asp)). Clear Maine lakes with rocky or sandy shallows carry only sparse pipewort, while coloured lakes carry bulrush and floating-leaved plants ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). Read alongside the MDIFW sheets, a Hot Brook-type lake is roughly 60–75% boulder and cobble over till, 15–30% fen, shrub and marsh in coves and at inlets and outlets, and 5–10% sand or gravel beach. That split is an inference, not a measurement.

Ice shapes the gentler of those shores. On lakes narrower than about 4 km, thermal expansion of the ice, not wind, drives most ice push. Lakes under about 0.8 km across are too small for much expansion ([Gatto, Ferrick & Calkins 2001](https://anrweb.vt.gov/PubDocs/DEC/WSMD/lakes/docs/lp_bomoicepush.pdf)). Both Hot Brook lakes, 3–4 km long, sit inside that window. At Gardner Lake, Connecticut, **a modern ice-push ridge stood 0.6–1.2 m high and 1.2–3.1 m wide**. Ice warming by about 1 °C an hour for six hours was enough to thrust it, and one winter reworked about 14 m³ of beach along 260 m of shore ([Pessl 1969, CRREL RR 259](https://erdc-library.erdc.dren.mil/jspui/bitstream/11681/5743/1/CRREL-Research-Report-259.pdf)). At Lake Bomoseen, Vermont, ice moved 0.6–2.1 m inland on damaged reaches whose bed slopes ran 0.03–0.3. Gentle shores, including gently sloping bedrock, let ice ride up, while a vertical bank resists it ([Gatto et al. 2001](https://anrweb.vt.gov/PubDocs/DEC/WSMD/lakes/docs/lp_bomoicepush.pdf)). The Maine Geological Survey has photographed a rampart at Bryant Pond ([MGS field photo](https://digitalmaine.com/mgs_geologic_field_photos/12293)). Ramparts 0.5–1.2 m high and 1–3 m wide at the winter waterline belong on gently sloping till and gravel. They do not belong on bedrock points or fen mats.

### Vegetation climbs from water-lilies to spruce–fir in under 3 m of elevation

MNAP's community descriptions give the zones from open water to forest. Water depth sets the aquatic zones, and elevation above the summer water sets the shrub zones.

No Maine source gives zone widths. The notes' estimates are 3–15 m of alder and 5–50 m of fen in a large cove; the widest fen mats may be quaking peat. A regional analogue is the Maine River Wetland Complex in Washington County. There, about 4,200 acres of peatland ring Pocomoonshine and Crawford lakes, and the sedge and sweetgale fens nearest the lakeshore are hydrologically connected to it ([MNAP focus area](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/focusarea/maine_river_wetland_complex.pdf)). Speckled alder links the shore to the water level. It grows to 9 m, sprouts into dense thickets and tolerates spring flooding. **When water stands at or above its root crown for 30 days or more, growth falls sharply and about a third of the plants die** ([USDA FEIS, *Alnus incana*](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/alninc/all.html)). A beaver dam or a long, high spring therefore kills shore alder, and a fluctuating shore stays open.

The connecting stream has its own bank community. MNAP's Alder Floodplain grows on seasonally flooded, ice-scoured riverbanks of mineral soil. It has **alder at more than 30% cover**, with red maple and red-osier dogwood, and bluejoint, ostrich fern and sensitive fern beneath, and its woody stems carry ice-scour damage early in the season ([MNAP, Alder Floodplain](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/alderfloodplain.htm)). Maine's regional channel curves put a 5–20 mi² brook at **5–11 m bankfull width and 0.3–0.5 m bankfull depth**. Bankfull flow is about three times the mean flow and is reached about 30 days a year ([Dudley 2004, USGS SIR 2004-5042](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2004/5042/pdf/sir2004-5042.pdf)). Hot Brook's banks, about 0.3–0.6 m above summer water, are inferred from those curves. So is a canopy about 60–90% closed: 2–5 m alder over a 3–8 m channel. Neither has been measured. Maine streams are short of wood. Surveys of coastal salmon rivers found wood loading "remarkably low", with riparian trees too small to become functional large wood ([EBTJV wood-addition application](https://easternbrooktrout.org/groups/whitewater-to-bluewater/funded-projects/ebtjv-projects/wood-additions-into-the-sheepscot-narraguagus-machias-watersheds-maine/application-for-wood-additions-into-the-sheepscot-narraguagus-machias-and-east-machias-watersheds-in-maine)). A search summary gives about 8 pieces per 100 m on the West Branch Sheepscot; that figure is unverified.

### Fallen trees are the lakeshore's most consequential structure

No Maine or New England lake has a wood survey; the numbers come from the northern Wisconsin lake district. **Undeveloped lakes averaged 555 logs per km of shoreline. On developed lakes the figure was 379 at forested sites and 57 at cabin sites**, and wood density rose with riparian tree density and fell with cabin density ([Christensen et al. 1996](https://urbanforestrysouth.org/resources/literature/impacts-of-lakeshore-residential-development-on-coarse-woody-debris-in-north-temperate-lakes-957)). Across 45 lakes, wood was extremely patchy: many sites had none, and others had 20 to 4,200 logs per km. The amount of downed wood on the adjacent land was the best predictor, wetland shores carried less, and wood lasts for centuries in the littoral zone ([Marburg, Turner & Kratz 2006](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_NatVarCoarseWoodinLakes.pdf)).

One whole-lake experiment shows what the wood does. In Little Rock Lake, logs and limbs were removed to 2 m depth, **cutting wood from 475 to 128 logs per km (−73%)**. Afterwards **yellow perch fell to extremely low densities**, and largemouth bass ate less fish and more terrestrial prey and grew more slowly ([Sass et al. 2006](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_WholeLakeWoodRemovalSass.pdf)). The same fallen trees carry other users. Painted turtles bask in groups on partly submerged logs ([NH Fish & Game](https://www.wildlife.nh.gov/wildlife-and-habitat/species-occurring-nh/eastern-painted-turtle)). Kingfishers fish from bare branches at the water's edge, with optimal perch density near 40 per km of shore ([Prose 1985, USFWS HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)). For Hot Brook, the Wisconsin figures translate to about 300–600 logs per km on Lower's forested shores, under about 100 on fen shores and 50–150 along Upper's camp shore (inferred). Most pieces would be whole spruce, fir, cedar or pine with the root wad on land, sloping into less than 2 m of water within about 10 m of shore.

### A natural outlet moves the shoreline by tens of centimetres a year

No stage record exists for an undammed Maine lake, because every USGS lake gauge in the state is on a regulated lake ([USGS NWIS site service](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/site/?format=rdb&stateCd=ME&siteType=LK&outputDataTypeCd=dv&seriesCatalogOutput=true)). The analogues agree with each other. Two kettle lakes in central New York with natural outlets fluctuated **about 1.5 ft (0.45 m) a year**, against 2.5–6 ft in lakes without outlets ([USGS WRIR 01-4166](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/2001/4166/wri014166.pdf)). Vermont regulators found Seymour Lake's natural level confined to a 14-inch band ([VT S.227](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2014/WorkGroups/Senate%20Natural%20Resources/Bills/S.227/S.227~Zachary%20Mauldin~As%20Introduced~1-29-2014.pdf)). Three non-drawdown Massachusetts lakes logged every 2 h for 3–4 years had summer levels from −30 to +102 cm (median +0.5 cm) about their reference pool ([Carmignani et al. 2021](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70228607)). Even Tropical Storm Irene changed lake depths by less than 1 m ([Klug et al. 2012](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/hawksites.newpaltz.edu/dist/1/8098/files/2012/10/Klug2012-EST-HurricaneIrenePaper.pdf)). A Hot Brook-type lake should therefore stand about +15–30 cm above its summer pool after the April freshet and fall to −10 to −30 cm in August–September. It rises again with November rain and stays nearly flat under the ice. Whether either Hot Brook lake has a control structure at its outlet was not confirmed.

Exposed width is the drop divided by the shore slope. **A 0.4 m fall bares about 20 m of a 2% sand flat, 8 m of a 5% beach, 2.7 m of a 15% cobble shore and 1 m of bedrock** (derived). On a fen, the mat rides with the water and little mud appears. The spring high has consequences elsewhere. Chain pickerel broadcast their eggs over flooded marsh vegetation right after ice-out ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)), so a weak freshet strands their spawning habitat. Loons nest within about 1.2 m of the water ([Wilson Bulletin 85:429](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v085n04/p0429-p0435.pdf)), and heavy rain now floods more loon nests than predators take in some years. New Hampshire lost a record 42 nests to flooding in one season ([Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/heavy-downpours-are-growing-threat-common-loons)).

### The shore's year runs from 120 days of snow to an October blaze

Volunteer records of red maple leaf-out in Maine gave mean dates between about 25 April and 13 May over 2011–2014 ([UMaine Signs of the Seasons](https://extension.umaine.edu/signs-of-the-seasons/red-maple-leaf-out/)). In the Maine Forest Service's Eastern Mid-Maine zone (Houlton to Calais, which takes in Danforth), **peak foliage fell between 30 September and 19 October** in 2020–2024 ([MFS past peak dates](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/projects/fall_foliage/report/past.shtml)). Daily NOAA records for 1991–2020 give the snow season. Houlton had at least an inch of snow on the ground on **about 120 days** a winter (9 complete seasons), with continuous cover typically from 25 December to 7 April. Caribou had 134 days, from 11 December to 12 April ([RCC-ACIS daily data](https://data.rcc-acis.org/StnData), computed). Pickerelweed and bulrush beds die back below the water in autumn, while cattail stands persist as brown stems ([MNAP Pickerelweed Marsh](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/pickerelweedmarsh.htm)). Lakes freeze in late November or early December, before the ground snow settles in. Land snow is gone two to three weeks before ice-out, leaving a spell of grey, snow-free ice.

## Snowmelt and storms deliver the catchment in a few weeks

### Precipitation is steady, but snow stores four months of it

Houlton, about 50 km north of Danforth, averages **998 mm of precipitation a year**. Every month gets 50–103 mm: February is driest, and June and October are wettest ([NOAA NCEI 1991–2020 monthly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&stations=USW00014609&format=csv&dataTypes=MLY-PRCP-NORMAL)). Measurable precipitation falls on 144 days, at least 2.5 mm on 84 days, at least 25 mm on only **6.9 days** and at least 51 mm on 0.8 days ([NOAA NCEI annual normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-annualseasonal-1991-2020&stations=USW00014607,USW00014609,USW00014610&format=csv&startDate=0001-01-01&endDate=9996-12-31)). NOAA Atlas 14 gives Danforth a 24-h storm of 57 mm every year, 65 mm every 2 years, 91 mm every 10 years and 132 mm every 100 years. A 10-year one-hour burst is 33 mm ([NOAA Atlas 14, Danforth](https://hdsc.nws.noaa.gov/cgi-bin/new/cgi_readH5.py?lat=45.66&lon=-67.87&type=pf&data=depth&units=metric&series=pds)). Caribou averages about 2 thunderstorm days in May, 5 in June and 7 in July ([NWS Caribou, May](https://weather.gov/car/MayClimateSummary); [June](https://www.weather.gov/car/Juneoutlook); [July](https://weather.gov/car/July2017ClimateOutlook)).

Winter turns that steady supply into storage. Caribou receives **about 300 cm of snow a year**. The median depth on the ground reaches 53 cm on 1 March, falls to 20 cm by 15 April and is zero by 1 May ([NOAA NCEI daily normals, Caribou](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-daily-1991-2020&stations=USW00014607&format=csv&startDate=0001-01-01&endDate=9996-12-31)). Mean snow water equivalent on 1 March exceeds 230 mm in northern Maine ([USGS WRIR 01-4258](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri20014258)). Houlton's monthly mean air temperature runs from −10.6 °C in January to 18.9 °C in July, averaging 4.8 °C ([NOAA NCEI normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-monthly-1991-2020&stations=USW00014609&format=csv&dataTypes=MLY-TAVG-NORMAL)). That gives about 900 freezing degree-days from December to March (derived). Years vary widely: winter 2025–26 was Houlton's driest on record ([NWS Caribou](https://www.weather.gov/car/Winter20252026SeasonalNarrativeforNorthernandEasternMaine)).

### Small Maine streams run at three times their mean in April and a fifth of it in late August

About 60% of the precipitation leaves as streamflow: 580–710 mm a year at three nearby gauges (derived from [USGS NWIS](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/dv/?format=rdb&sites=01030500,01030350,01017550&parameterCd=00060&statCd=00003)). The water comes in one pulse. The day by which half the January–June flow has passed has a median of **18 April on the Mattawamkeag River** (range 1 April to 1 May over 36 years) and **15 April on Wytopitlock Stream and Williams Brook** (same source, computed). Across the 11 snowmelt-dominated gauges of northern and mountainous Maine and New Hampshire, that date moved 1–2 weeks earlier, mostly in the last 30 years. It correlates with March–April air temperature (r = −0.72) ([Hodgkins & Dudley 2006](https://water.usgs.gov/climate_water/hodgkins_dudley_2006b.pdf)). Monthly mean flows, as multiples of the annual mean, show the shape ([USGS NWIS monthly statistics](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01030500&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)):

| Gauge | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mattawamkeag River (3,672 km², downstream of Hot Brook) | 0.61 | 0.49 | 0.95 | **3.31** | 1.99 | 0.75 | 0.42 | **0.30** | 0.32 | 0.60 | 1.13 | 1.13 |
| Wytopitlock Stream (127 km²) | 0.69 | 0.50 | 1.32 | **3.30** | 1.18 | 0.61 | 0.47 | **0.33** | 0.39 | 0.71 | 1.10 | 1.41 |

On an average day of the year, flow peaks on 15–19 April at **4.1–6.5 times** the annual mean and bottoms out on 25 August–2 September at **0.18–0.29 times**. On Wytopitlock, flow is below 0.018 times the mean 5% of the time, and droughts like 1995 drove small-stream monthly means close to zero ([USGS NWIS daily values](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/dv/?format=rdb&sites=01030350,01017550,01021480&parameterCd=00060&statCd=00003&startDT=2008-10-01&endDT=2025-09-30), computed). Storms add short pulses on top. From June to October these brooks see about 10–18 rises a year. **Flow climbs for 1–2 days to a median of 2.2–2.6 times the pre-storm level** (7–15 times at the 90th percentile), then recedes by a factor of 0.81–0.88 a day (same source, computed). Annual floods reach 6.5 times the mean flow on the Mattawamkeag and 19.6 times on 10-km² Williams Brook. About 60–65% of them come in April, and the rest mostly in May, December and March, when rain falls on snow ([USGS NWIS peak flows](https://nwis.waterdata.usgs.gov/nwis/peak?site_no=01030350&agency_cd=USGS&format=rdb), computed).

Hot Brook is not a free brook but a lake outlet, and its flow is Upper Hot Brook Lake's outflow. Lake storage damps and delays each pulse. Two short Maine lake-outlet records stay high into May and June: Clayton Stream ran 2.4 times its mean in May ([USGS NWIS monthly statistics](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01012515,01035500&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)). As a rough figure, a 25 mm storm on a catchment ten times the lake's area, with 30–50% quick runoff, raises the lake by about 10 cm. Outflow then peaks one to three days later and drains over a week or more. The catchment area is unknown, so that number is illustrative. The sound link between level and flow is a weir-type rating curve, Q ∝ (h − h_sill)^1.5. **As a drought lake falls toward its sill, the stream nearly stops** and its pools are isolated.

### Phosphorus and colour arrive in storms and the freshet

Maine's lake phosphorus plans score each land use with an export coefficient. In kilograms of total phosphorus per hectare per year, the Webber Pond plan assigns:

| Source | kg TP/ha/yr |
|---|---|
| Undeveloped forest | 0.04 (range 0.01–0.04, from Hubbard Brook work) |
| Passively managed forest | 0.02 |
| Wetlands | 0.10 (0.10–0.20) |
| Harvested forest | 0.40 |
| Shoreline residential lots | 0.25–2.70 |
| Private camp roads | 2.00 |
| Atmospheric deposition on open water | **0.16** (0.11–0.21) |

The same plan found that forest covered 47.6% of the direct watershed but supplied only 9.3% of the external load. Internal release from the sediment equalled the whole external load. The plan frames watershed P as running off "during storm events and springtime snow melting" ([Maine DEP, Webber Pond PCAP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/tmdl/2003/tmdlwebberrep.pdf)). Event timing is large. In a forested German headwater, sampling storms raised the annual P export estimate from 19–44 to as much as 83 g/ha ([TU Dresden](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/quantification-of-phosphorus-exports-from-a-small-forested-headwatercatchment-in-the-eastern-ore-mountains-germany(dfd324b2-86a6-439b-a029-5f5b0ed9c388).html)). In a New Hampshire tributary of Squam Lake, P spiked at each rain onset and stayed high for only a few hours around peak flow ([Plymouth State](https://plymouth.edu/sites/default/files/media/2023-12/phosphorus-transport-to-squam-lake-during-storm-events.pdf)). **At Maine's Bear Brook watershed, snowmelt raised total acid-soluble P with discharge while dissolved P stayed at or below about 3 µg/L** ([Water, Air & Soil Pollution: Focus](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/B%3AWAFO.0000028362.48966.17)), so the spring load is mostly particles. No Maine forest nitrogen coefficient was retrieved. Bear Brook still retained more than 80% of experimentally added N after 20 years ([EMAS 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10661-010-1527-z)), so forest N export is small.

DOC follows the same pattern. Hubbard Brook streams exported about 2 g C/m² a year in 1976–80 ([McDowell & Likens 1988](https://lternet.edu/biblio/origin-composition-and-flux-of-dissolved-organic-carbon-in-the-hubbard-brook-valley)), and nine catchments there exported 1.3–15.3 g C/m² a year over 1992–2022 ([Hubbard Brook](https://hubbardbrook.org/carbon-on-the-run-how-hidden-electrified-waters-speed-earths-forests-toward-the-sea/)). Wetter, peatier catchments sit at the high end. Stream DOC at Bear Brook in Maine ran 1–7 mg/L, "typically less than 3", and rose only slightly at high discharge ([Norton et al. 1999](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70022030)). Delivery is therefore set mainly by the volume of water. **Up to 86% of annual DOC export from forested catchments occurs during storms and snowmelt** ([Klug et al. 2012](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/hawksites.newpaltz.edu/dist/1/8098/files/2012/10/Klug2012-EST-HurricaneIrenePaper.pdf), citing Raymond & Saiers). In six Maine drinking-water lakes sampled around five severe storms, some showed a DOC spike that cleared within days, others a rise that persisted, and the largest lakes no change. The DOC came from leaf and needle litter washed in by runoff ([UMaine, Warner & Saros](https://smartwatermagazine.com/news/university-maine/understanding-effect-severe-rainstorms-freshwater-ecosystems)).

### Colour, not algae, sets the light

In 75 boreal lakes, PAR attenuation followed **log Kd = 0.24 log TP + 0.45 log DOC**. Coloured dissolved matter absorbed 56% of PAR on average, against 7% for phytoplankton pigments ([Thrane, Hessen & Andersen 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10021-014-9776-2)). Colour also sets the heat structure of small stratified lakes. Across lakes of increasing colour, the epilimnion thinned **from 2.5 m to 0.75 m**, with colour explaining 91% of the variance ([Houser 2006](https://doi.org/10.1139/f06-131)). Lower DOC would deepen epilimnia by 1–2 m in Shield lakes under 500 ha ([Fee et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1996.41.5.0912)), and coloured lakes keep steadier cold water in warm years ([Snucins & Gunn 2000](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2000.45.7.1639)). Colour also hides phosphorus. In 9 of 35 USGS study lakes in Maine, colour exceeded 30 Pt-Co units, and there "phosphorus is tied up with the color producing compounds" ([Cowing & Scott 1980, USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri8069)).

The colour is rising. Adirondack lakes gained about 0.5–0.7 mg/L of DOC per decade ([Rose & Stetler 2020](https://www.nalms.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/40-1-4.pdf)). All 29 high-elevation Maine lakes in a 30-year record had positive DOC trends ([UMaine Mitchell Center](https://umaine.edu/mitchellcenter/?p=14118)). Landsat estimates put Maine's mean lake clarity at 4.94 m in 1995 and 4.38 m in 2010 ([McCullough et al.](https://bioone.org/journals/Freshwater-Science/volume-32/issue-3/12-070.1/Landsat-imagery-reveals-declining-clarity-of-Maines-lakes-during-19952010/10.1899/12-070.1.full)). For the sim, a storm's turbidity should split into two parts: mineral murk that settles within days, and DOC colour that lasts weeks to months.

### Snow, not ice, sets the light under the ice

A Maine winter survey of 16 lakes around Bridgton in 2026 measured **ice 31–50 cm thick** and **light just under the ice at about 1–12% of the surface**. Light fell with snow depth (0 to about 20 cm), and snow was present on almost every visit. Chlorophyll often peaked within a few metres of the ice. **Five of the 16 lakes had bottom water at or below 2 mg/L of oxygen on at least one visit** ([LEA Winter Monitoring Report 2026](https://mainelakes.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/LEAWinterMonitoringReport2026_final.pdf)). Those lakes are in western Maine; Danforth's snowier winters should sit at the dark end of that range. A 46-lake synthesis gives the optical constants for a layered Beer–Lambert law ([Donini et al. 2026](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/485537)):

| Layer | Median albedo | Median attenuation (m⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Black (clear) ice | 0.55 | 0.79 |
| White (snow) ice | 0.60 | 4.35 |
| Snow | 0.94 | 8.96 |

With those medians, 40 cm of snow-free black ice passes about 33% of the light. Ten centimetres of snow on the same ice passes about 1.8%, and 30 cm of snow about 0.3% (derived). Lake Balaton agrees: snow-free ice passed about half the light, 4 cm of snow cut it to a tenth, and phytoplankton production fell from 20 to 4 mg C m⁻² h⁻¹ ([Herodek & Oláh 1973](https://real.mtak.hu/195744)). Snow also changes the ice. Once its load exceeds the ice's buoyant freeboard, (1,000 − 917) kg/m³ × ice thickness, the ice sinks and water floods the snow. That happens at about 25 mm of snow water on 30 cm of ice (derived). The slush freezes into white ice, which made up 10–43% of the ice column at a Finnish lake ([Leppäranta & Kosloff 2000](https://archive.geophysica.fi/pdf/geophysica_2000_36_1-2_233_lepparanta.pdf)). Each flooding event therefore brightens the water below, because white ice is far clearer than snow. Ice thickness follows Stefan's law, h = α√(freezing degree-days), with α from about 1.3–2.0 under deep snow to 2.7 when snow-free ([The Cryosphere discussion](https://tc.copernicus.org/preprints/tc-2020-125/tc-2020-125-AR1.pdf)). Houlton's ~900 °C·d gives 40–60 cm under snow (derived). Northern Maine reports agree: river ice up to 56 cm in February 2026 and about 61 cm on lower Moosehead Lake ([Maine RFAC 2026](https://www.maine.gov/mema/sites/maine.gov.mema/files/inline-files/2026%20RFAC%20Report.pdf); [Bangor Daily News](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2026/05/01/outdoors/outdoors-environment/moosehead-lake-ice-out-2026/)).

Oxygen is where this matters. Under the ice of shallow Arctic lakes 9–13 m deep, O₂ fell by **0.2–0.39 g/m² a day**, against 0.03–0.14 in 24-m Toolik Lake, because sediment area per unit volume is larger in shallow water. The rates fell to 20–50% of their initial values by late winter, and early-winter losses were smaller in low-snow years ([Schwefel et al. 2023](https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.1002%2Flno.12357)). Dividing by mean depth gives about **0.06–0.12 mg/L a day for Upper Hot Brook (3.35 m)**, 0.07–0.15 for a 2.7-m-mean pond and 0.02–0.04 for the sim's 9.3-m-mean lake (derived). Integrated over about 145 days of ice with rates declining, a pond with no under-ice photosynthesis loses roughly 6–15 mg/L, which spans the range from comfortable to anoxic (derived from an Arctic analogue). Snow decides which end a given winter lands on. In practical terms, keeping about 30% of a pond's ice clear of snow lets in enough light to prevent winterkill ([Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/winterkill-in-ponds)). Maine records winterkill only in very shallow ponds, as at Passaconway Pond (0.8 mg/L in February 1961, [MDIFW](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/york/passaconway_pond.pdf)). Hot Brook's white perch fishery, which requires oxygenated winter water ([MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)), places it at the favourable end in normal winters.

### Wind, thunderstorms and heat waves stir the water at short notice

Caribou's hourly normals put the monthly mean wind at **3.9 m/s in March and 2.5 m/s in August**. Spring afternoons reach about 4.6 m/s, July and August nights fall to about 1.7 m/s, and 17–19% of summer hours are calm ([NOAA NCEI hourly normals](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=normals-hourly-1991-2020&stations=USW00014607&format=csv&startDate=0001-01-01&endDate=9996-12-31)). A forest edge shelters the water surface for about **50 canopy heights downwind**, roughly 0.75–1 km for a 15–20 m forest ([Markfort et al. 2010](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/204593?ln=en)). Full wind stress needs a fetch of about 1 km ([Markfort et al., EGU 2014](https://opac.geologie.ac.at/ais312/dokumente/EGU/EGU2014-11486.pdf)). A 3–4 km Hot Brook lake is therefore well exposed, which helps keep it mixed, while a small beaver-sized pond would be almost entirely sheltered. In stratified lakes, stronger late-spring winds delayed hypolimnetic hypoxia, and calm, stable late summers slowed recovery ([NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/48461/noaa_48461_DS1.pdf)). In the sim's 14-m lake, a strong afternoon wind would tilt the thermocline into an internal seiche of about 2 h per km of basin length (derived from the two-layer formula), bringing cold, low-oxygen water up at the windward end.

Large storms reset small lakes quickly but briefly. Tropical Storm Irene cut thermal stability within hours in nine northeastern lakes. **All but one recovered to within 80% of pre-storm stability within a week**, but respiration fed by washed-in carbon outlasted the thermal disturbance and turned the lakes net-heterotrophic. Lakes in which storm rain on the lake and catchment exceeded half the lake's volume were disrupted far more ([Klug et al. 2012](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/hawksites.newpaltz.edu/dist/1/8098/files/2012/10/Klug2012-EST-HurricaneIrenePaper.pdf)). Heat runs the other way. Moose Pond in southern Maine reached **29.4 °C at the surface on 18 July 2024** and turned over completely on 12 October ([LEA, Moose Pond 2024](https://mainelakes.org/?p=91796)). Lake heat waves are defined as five or more days above the 90th-percentile surface temperature, and they are projected to grow hotter and longer ([Woolway et al. 2021](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/en/researchoutputs/lake-heatwaves-under-climate-change(9c36c873-7725-4942-8076-1adf883fa0e2)/export.html)). A deep lake cannot freeze until its whole column cools to about 4 °C, so **small, shallow basins ice over earlier than deep lakes**, and at ice-out shore leads open first ([Maine DEP, Seasonal Magic](https://www1.maine.gov/dep/water/lakes/lessons/seasonalmagic.pdf)).

Two smaller inputs complete the water budget. Groundwater ranges from 55% of inflow at Walden Pond to a minority at Lake Auburn, Maine, where streams supply about 62% ([USGS, Walden Pond](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri014137); [USGS, Lake Auburn](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2004/5106)). Brook trout use seeps and confluences to survive hot summers ([Briggs et al. 2018](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/6383/2018/hess-22-6383-2018.html)). The Hot Brook catchment's geology and groundwater share are unknown. No Maine lake evaporation rate was found.

## Shore trees feed the stream, and the lake exports more insects than it receives

### Land insects matter in the stream and within metres of a lakeshore

Forested streams receive land insects at rates that change their fish. A Swedish outlet stream 1–1.5 m wide under more than 70% canopy received **39.5 mg dry weight per m² a day** from May to October, about four times more than covered sections ([Erős et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3344874/)). Southeast Alaskan streams received about 80 mg/m² a day, and more under red alder than under conifers ([Allan et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1139/f03-019)). The flux peaks in July–August and falls to about zero in winter. Forested reaches receive 1.7 times what grassland reaches do ([Kawaguchi et al. 2003](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084%5B0701:TIIDTL%5D2.0.CO;2)), and **roofing over a stream to cut the input reduced fish biomass dramatically** (same source). Land insects can supply up to half the annual energy of drift-feeding fish, although they fall mostly in summer ([Baxter, Fausch & Saunders 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2004.01328.x)). In a Japanese forest stream they supplied 44% of the fish assemblage's annual energy, and 60–100% of the food of water-column fishes while the trees were in leaf ([Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)).

Lakes receive the same rain of insects along a thin edge. A model of a 12-ha German lake assumed **50.4 mg dry weight per metre of shoreline per day** (a figure seen only in a search summary of the record). Its authors concluded that fish-mediated terrestrial P matters only in lakes smaller than about 3 ha, "owing to the increasing perimeter-to-area ratio" ([Mehner et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2005.50.6.2022); its effect size is disputed by [Wurtsbaugh 2007](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/wats_facpub/117)). Development erases the input. Across 24 lakes, terrestrial insects were up to 100% of fish diet mass in undeveloped lakes and averaged 2% in developed ones ([Francis & Schindler 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0706.2009.17723.x)). Which fish eat them depends on feeding mode. A surface-feeding shiner's August diet was dominated by land insects even after their availability had fallen tenfold since April, while co-occurring fishes rarely took them ([Cloe & Garman 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02028848)). Shenandoah smallmouth bass took 0.1–2.6% and redbreast sunfish 1.9–8.0% of their annual diet from land insects, mostly in summer beetle pulses ([Murphy et al. 2005](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/murphy-325-335.pdf)).

Worked through, the German figure implies about 25 mg/m² a day for a 4-m stream with two forested banks, which agrees with the Swedish and Alaskan measurements (derived). For Hot Brook's alder-lined channel, 3–8 m wide, it implies about **12–33 mg/m² a day at the summer peak** (derived). Across the whole of Upper Hot Brook, spruce–fir banks at 0.4–0.6 of a deciduous forest's yield, and a June-to-October season worth about 65 days of peak input, give roughly 1.3–2 g per metre of shore a year. **Spread over 4 m of shore per 1,000 m² of lake, that is 5–8 mg/m² a year, under 1% of the lake's own insect emergence** (derived; assumptions as stated). On a lake this size, land insects are food only for fish working the near-shore band and the stream.

### Leaves fuel the stream and add a pulse of colour in October

A northern hardwood forest drops **about 570 g of litter per m² a year**: about half leaves, half in autumn, with storms setting the timing ([Gosz, Likens & Bormann 1972](https://doi.org/10.2307/1934293)). Leaf fall alone varied from 234 to 370 g/m² across 13 New Hampshire sites ([Yanai et al. 2012](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/variation-in-mass-and-nutrient-concentration-of-leaf-litter-acros/)). Under a closed canopy, a small stream runs almost entirely on that litter. **In Bear Brook, New Hampshire, more than 99% of energy input came from the land**, algae and vascular plants were absent, 47% of the input arrived as dissolved organic matter, and 66% was exported downstream ([Fisher & Likens 1973](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942301)). Breakdown rates differ mostly by species. Across 40 Appalachian measurements, leaf mass fell at a mean k of 0.0098 a day (range 0.0016–0.0316): red maple about 0.010 and white pine needles about 0.0055. Leaves moved about 1.6 m per transport event and 10–19 m a year ([Webster et al. 1999](https://ww2.coastal.edu/jjhutche/Pubs/webster%20et%20al,%201999,%2041-%20687-705.pdf)). In shaded Nova Scotia tributaries, red maple and speckled alder litter decayed at k = 0.013–0.032. Where shredding caddis congregated, bags lost 2–5% a day and were empty in 35–50 days ([Hydrobiologia 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-016-3001-6)). Undried leaves of six Shield riparian species lost 6–18% of their mass to leaching in two weeks ([France et al. 1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1002953629795)). Shredders turn roughly half of what they process into fine particles (a search summary gives 37–69%; [Freshwater Science 32(3)](https://bioone.org/journals/freshwater-science/volume-32/issue-3/12-090.1/The-effect-of-shredder-community-composition-on-the-production-and/10.1899/12-090.1.full)).

Lakes get far less per square metre. Most leaves land within about 10 m of shore ([Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)). Deposition falls exponentially with distance ([Llyn Frongoch](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00018365)), and leaves creeping along the ground add only 0.3–0.5 m a year, about 6% of the total ([France 1995](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/aspen_bib/1975)). Airborne litter can reach 10% of phytoplankton production in oligotrophic and very small lakes, scaled by the ratio of shoreline to lake area ([Gasith & Hasler 1976](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1976.21.2.0253)). In a Shield lake littoral, sugar maple lost mass at only k = 0.0039. Chironomids colonised the leaf packs within two days, and the authors judged the litter "habitat rather than a direct food source" ([Scott Lake](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1003537232319)). The leachate matters more than the leaves. **Forested Ontario shores delivered 17.8 g of leaf-leachate DOC per metre of shoreline a year, against 0.4 g after riparian clear-cutting**, and models predicted up to 9% lower plankton production without it ([France et al. 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01474657)). Spread over Upper Hot Brook, that is about 0.07 g C/m² a year (derived), small next to catchment runoff but concentrated in October and November.

### Pine pollen is a brief spring phosphorus pulse

At a 27-ha jack-pine lake in Ontario, floating collectors caught pollen delivering **0.34–1.7 mg P/m² a day for the 45 days after ice-out**, about 10 kg of P a year. Ambient, 3× and 10× pollen additions raised phytoplankton, herbivorous zooplankton and periphyton in littoral mesocosms ([Graham, Vinebrooke & Turner 2006](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2006.51.3.1524)). That is about 37 mg P/m² a year (derived), more than Maine DEP's 16 mg atmospheric coefficient, which plausibly already includes some pollen. White and red pine pollen holds about 3 mg P/g and can be up to a third of bulk P deposition ([Trent University thesis](https://batadora.trentu.ca/objects/etd-391)). Leaching and microbes release at least 80% of its carbon, 40% of its N and 50% of its P within days ([Aquatic Sciences 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00027-011-0198-3)). Hot Brook's white pine and spruce–fir shores are not jack pine, so the magnitude there is unknown.

### Shade decides the stream's light, not its temperature

Under a closed deciduous canopy, about 97% of a 2–5 m stream is shaded while the trees are in leaf, and algal production peaks instead in spring and autumn, when the canopy is open ([Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)). Under conifers in Sweden, PAR at the stream surface was only 23–26 µmol/m²/s ([Erős et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3344874/)). Wooded banks lower stream temperatures, more at the daily maximum than on average ([Environmental Evidence review 2012](https://environmentalevidencejournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/2047-2382-1-3)). In Maine, removing 80% of riparian vegetation on the East Branch Piscataquis raised maximum temperatures, while a 5% cut in shade had no effect ([KRIS Sheepscot](https://wvvw.krisweb.com/krissheepscot/krisdb/html/krisweb/watershed/riparian_lwd.htm)). A continental comparison found open reaches up to 8.4 °C hotter at the daily maximum. Maximum temperature rose only 0.34–2.9 °C from the top to the bottom of open reaches (an abstract seen through a search summary; [ESA 2012](https://eco.confex.com/eco/2012/webprogram/Paper35550.html)). Hot Brook carries lake water for about 200 m, so its temperature is set by Upper Hot Brook's surface. Canopy over such a short reach acts mainly on light and food. Only slack, sunlit pools, where water lingers, can heat appreciably above the inflow. No study quantifies shading of a lake's littoral by shore trees.

### Emerging insects carry more out than land insects bring in

A synthesis of 49 whole-lake estimates put the **median benthic secondary production at 2.3 g C/m² a year**. Insects were 70% of it, and 30% of the insects emerged ([Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)). Lakes produce less benthos than streams (1.46 against 6.67 g C/m² a year) but export a larger share (0.30 against 0.19), and they deliver about 2.5 times as much per metre of shoreline ([Gratton & Vander Zanden 2009](https://doi.org/10.1890/08-1546.1)). The same synthesis found litter and other land particles outweigh emergence in small lakes, and emergence outweighs them in large lakes ([Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)). At Mývatn, midge emergence ran 0.15–3.7 g dry/m² a year. Deposition peaked 20–25 m inland with 70% inside 100 m, midges were about 10% N and 1% P by dry mass, and **75–99% of emerging insects never returned to the water** ([Dreyer et al. 2015](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Dreyer_15_Ecology_quantifying-insect-deposition.pdf)). In three German lakes, emergence was dominated by chironomids and driven by water temperature, peaking in spring, summer or early autumn. Up to 1.8 g/m² a year emerged from the Lake Constance littoral ([Salvarina, Gravier & Rothhaupt 2017](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/a38a7017-821a-4130-97a6-56ebdbc696b2/full)). On land, emerged insects supply 25–100% of the energy of riparian birds, bats and spiders ([Baxter et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2004.01328.x)). Shoreline spiders drew 50% of their energy from aquatic producers, raccoons 48% and swallows 41% ([Kautza & Sullivan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1890/15-1095.1)).

In the stream the balance tips toward the land: hundreds of grams of leaves and grams of insects arrive per square metre, comparable to or larger than what the stream's own benthos produces.

## Turtles, frogs, birds and mammals cross the waterline on an ice-out calendar

### Painted turtles live in the water and breed on the bank

Maine's painted turtle is common and secure. Maine's wildlife agency gives its habitat as "permanent water with submerged vegetation and plentiful basking areas", and it hibernates in the muddy bottoms of lakes and ponds ([MDIFW, painted turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/painted-turtle.html)). It basks by hauling out onto logs, fallen trees, rocks and floating lily-tuber mats, often in groups. At Grand Lake, New Brunswick, the highest abundances are where such basking sites are plentiful ([COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)). Turtles bask most when air is much warmer than water: in spring, in sun, in the morning ([search summary citing Schwarzkopf & Brooks](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241721610_Seasonal_temperature_variation_in_the_painted_turtle_Chrysemys_picta)). In northern Ontario most bouts last under 60–100 minutes ([McKnight et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8366836/)). A secondary Adirondack account gives two or three basking-and-foraging cycles a day of about 2 h each, stopping on cloudy days ([Wild Adirondacks](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)). Turtles feed only once they can raise body temperature above 15–20 °C. Ontario densities run **2.6–15.7 per ha on the Canadian Shield and 7.5–10 in New Brunswick**, against 97–109 per ha in one small productive Algonquin pond. Adults survive 97–98% a year, mature at 9–11 years and live beyond 60. They overwinter in about 0.3 m of water on or in soft sediment and stay submerged for at least 143 days in central Ontario ([COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)).

Breeding takes them onto land. Females nest from late May to early July, peaking in mid-June, mostly in the afternoon and evening. They dig in open, sloping, often south-facing sand, loam or gravel, at a **median of 11 m from the water** (mean 78 m, maximum 1,233 m). Nova Scotia clutches average 8.6 eggs (5–11). Incubation takes 65–80 days, or 83–97 days in Algonquin. Nests at 26 °C or cooler produce males and nests at 29 °C or warmer produce females ([COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)). **In the north most hatchlings stay in the nest over winter.** In Canadian studies, 56–100% did so. In south-central New Hampshire only 7 of 42 hatchling observations were autumn emergences, between 10 August and 26 September. The rest came out in spring, and winter in the nest killed every hatchling in 1988–89 and about half in 1993–94 ([Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)). Hatchlings survive 24 h at −4 °C with half their body water frozen, but die frozen at −10.9 °C ([Storey et al. 1988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC282428)). Nest predation, mostly by raccoons and foxes, ranges from about 17% to more than 90%. In one Québec study 86% of nests were destroyed the night they were laid ([COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)). In New Hampshire, 22% of artificial nests were raided within a week, more often within 50 m of a pond ([Marchand et al. 2002](https://scholars.unh.edu/nhaes/202)). Turtles nesting 11 m from water therefore lay in the most dangerous strip.

### Snapping turtles nest far afield, and their young emerge in September

Snapping turtles are common across Maine. They lay in June in sandy soil, including gravel road shoulders, up to 3 km from their home water, and hibernate in lake-bottom sediment ([MDIFW, snapping turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/snapping-turtle.html)). A Maine biologist puts laying from late May into the first week of July, hatching about 85 days later and typical clutches at 25–45 eggs ([Sun Journal](https://new.sunjournal.com/?p=9255515), via a search summary). Unlike painted turtles, **northern snapping turtle hatchlings leave in autumn**. All 14 New Hampshire emergences fell between 5 and 24 September ([Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)), and only 1 of 129 Ontario clutches overwintered successfully ([Turtle Guardians](https://www.turtleguardians.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Remark-Hatchling-SNTU-Overwintering-in-Natal-Nest-2.pdf)). Northern densities are low: 1.5–2.4 adults per ha in Algonquin. Feeding begins once water exceeds 16 °C, about 14% of clutches produce hatchlings, adults survive 93–97% a year and maturity takes 11–19 years. Removing snapping turtles did not change duckling brood survival in South Dakota ([Tortoise Trust review](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)). Diet studies disagree. One Michigan stomach study reports about a third game fish and a third aquatic plants ([Michigan DNR RR 553](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR0501-RR0600/RR0553.pdf), via a search summary), while reviews stress slow, non-game fish and plants. Of Maine's other turtles, only the wood turtle fits Hot Brook, as a rare stream animal of slow, sandy reaches that hibernates under undercut banks ([MDIFW, wood turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/wood-turtle.html)). Spotted turtles live only in southern and central Maine, and Blanding's turtles only in York and Cumberland counties ([MDIFW, spotted turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/spotted-turtle.html); [MDIFW, Blanding's turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/blandings-turtle.html)).

### Frogs sit at the waterline and breed from May to August

The green frog is Maine's shore-edge frog. Its tadpoles overwinter in permanent water, and adults winter underwater or underground ([MDIFW, green frog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/green-frog.html)). It breeds from May to August in Maine and hibernates from October to March. Breeding males space themselves **1.0–1.5 m apart in dense emergent vegetation and 4–6 m apart on open shore**. Adults rarely go more than 1 m from water except on rainy nights. Northern tadpoles overwinter once and metamorphose the following June or July ([Virginia Herpetological Society, green frog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)). The bullfrog is more aquatic. It breeds from mid-June to July once air exceeds 20 °C, its tadpoles take about two years, and it hibernates in the mud of pond and stream bottoms ([MDIFW, bullfrog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/american-bullfrog.html)). Houlton's July mean air temperature is 18.9 °C, so in eastern Maine the bullfrog's cue is met only on warm spells. Males defend floating territories 2–5 m across, stay inactive until water nears 15 °C, and in Nova Scotia tolerated pond oxygen below 20 Torr for more than eight weeks ([VHS, bullfrog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)). Michigan bullfrogs and green frogs ate mostly insects: true bugs, adult dragonflies, flies and beetles, plus 5–15% frogs ([Werner, Wellborn & McPeek 1995](https://ecotox.oehha.ca.gov/citation/diet-composition-postmetamorphic-bullfrogs-and-green-frogs-implications-interspecific)). The mink frog, at the southern edge of its range in Maine, lives in dense shallow vegetation and breeds from late June to early August ([MDIFW, mink frog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/mink-frog.html)). Green frogs were significantly scarcer on developed Wisconsin lakes ([Grist, on Woodford & Meyer 2003](https://grist.org/article/ness-development/)).

The catchment's other amphibians mostly stay off the lakes. Wood frogs and spotted salamanders breed in fishless vernal pools, and fish make lakeshores and beaver flowages "biological sinks" for them. They migrate on the first warm, rainy nights of spring, from mid-March in southern Maine to mid-May in the north ([Calhoun & deMaynadier 2001](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/species_planning/reptiles/vernalpools/vernal_pools.pdf)). They move when night temperatures are above about 4–7 °C and the ground has thawed ([MDIFW blog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/why-did-amphibian-cross-road)). The red-spotted newt is the exception: an adult of lakes and permanent ponds with fish, which fish largely reject because of tetrodotoxin in its skin, while bullfrogs eat it ([PLOS ONE 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0027581)). The fish-eating common water snake has a patchy, disjunct Maine range, and its presence at Hot Brook is unconfirmed ([UMaine honors thesis](https://honors.umaine.edu/?p=9938)).

### Wading and plunging birds make shallow water lethal for larger fish

The great blue heron fishes in **water no deeper than about 50 cm**, wading in 15–25 cm to flush prey. It needs clear water and a firm bottom, and takes fish up to 25 cm: 95% of fish eaten in a Wisconsin study were 25 cm or shorter. Its main foraging periods are the four hours after sunrise and before sunset, and it leaves inland Maine for the winter ([Short & Cooper 1985, USFWS HSI](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/Herons/Heron_habitat.pdf)). Captive herons needed **about 300 g of fish a day** ([Glahn, Dorr & Tobin 2000](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/icwdm_usdanwrc/626)), about 60 kg over a seven-month Maine season (derived). Maine observers notice herons back around 15 March, and tagged birds reach colonies in the first or second week of April ([Maine HERON blog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/maine-heron-observation-network/monitoring-maines-great-blue-herons-three-years-and-counting)). That is before ice-out, so early herons work the open stream and pond margins.

The belted kingfisher catches fish **within about 15 cm of the surface, almost always in water shallower than 60 cm**. Its prey is usually under 10 cm (range 2.5–17.8 cm). Michigan lake territories averaged 0.8 km of shoreline and always included a sheltered bay. Kingfishers avoid muddy water, and wave chop matters to them almost as much as turbidity ([Prose 1985, USFWS HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)). Wintering birds in California needed about 70 kcal a day, spent 80% of their time hunting, succeeded on more than half of strikes from perches and about 20% from hovering, and did worst on disturbed water ([Forsell 1983](https://digitalcommons.humboldt.edu/etd/1592)). That is about 15–18 fish of 4 g a day, assuming about 1 kcal per gram of wet fish (derived).

Experiments show what these predators do to fish behaviour. Grazing fish longer than about 3 cm avoid water shallower than 20 cm, "where wading and diving predators feed most commonly and effectively". Of armoured catfish tethered where herons and kingfishers fished, **none of 6 survived 24 h in water under 18 cm, while 10 of 14 survived in deeper water**. In pens beside foraging herons, catfish were largely eliminated at 10 and 20 cm depth but mostly survived at 30 and 50 cm ([Power 1987](https://ib.berkeley.edu/labs/power/publications/Power_1987_PredatorAvoidance.pdf)). In 262 Tennessee stream pools, pool depth predicted the size of the largest fish, and fish about 100 mm long survived much worse in 10 cm pools than in 40 cm pools ([Harvey & Stewart 1991](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00634588)). Raccoons and herons quickly ate crayfish tethered in 5–15 cm of water but caused little mortality at 40–70 cm ([Englund & Krupa 2000](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/habitat-use-by-crayfish-in-stream-pools-influence-of-predators-de/)). The rule is "bigger fish deeper by day": small fish use the shallows as refuge from fish predators, larger ones leave them to the birds. **The stream's 0.35 m riffles (the sim's depth) lie inside heron and kingfisher reach, and its 1.1 m pools (the user's own measure of Hot Brook) lie beyond it**. That offers a second explanation, besides warm water, for sunfish crowding the stream's pools.

### Loons, ospreys, eagles, mergansers, otters and mink hunt deeper or roam

The common loon returns to Maine lakes right after ice-out and nests on the shoreline in May–June. Chicks hatch after about 27 days and are raised through July and August. Adults leave in September–October, and the birds winter on the coast ([MDIFW / Maine Audubon, *Living in Loon Territory*](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/livingwithloons.pdf)). A breeding territory averages about 40 ha in Maine ([Maine Audubon](https://maineaudubon.org/wp-content/uploads/1_Monitoring-Instructions_2024.pdf)). In 2019 the Belgrade Lakes held 23 territorial pairs but fledged only 0.35 young per pair ([Belgrade Lakes Association](https://www.belgradelakesassociation.org/Portals/belgrade-lakes/2019%20-%20BLA%20COLO%20Summary%20Report.pdf)). A Wisconsin pair raising two chicks removed **about 423 kg of food in 5.5 months**, often yellow perch ([Barr 1996](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Eau/explor/LotaV3/Site/fr/Main/Exploration/bibRecord.php?hk=001142)). Chicks ate 162–383 g of fish a day as they grew ([Fournier et al. 2002](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70024245)). One loon's dives averaged about 52 s ([Auk 84:123](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v084n01/p0123-p0123.pdf)). Taking off needs a run of 60–180 m on the water ([Loon Preservation Committee](https://loon.org/looncam-blogs/loon-cam-2019/getting-airborne-why-loons-dont-visit-your-birdbath/)).

The osprey takes larger fish from near the surface. Nests in Chesapeake Bay received 5.4 fish a day averaging 157 g ([J. Raptor Research 25:109](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v025n04/p00109-p00112.pdf)), and 82% of fishing efforts succeeded at a California lake ([Ueoka & Koplin 1973](https://sora-dev.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v007n02/p00032-p00038.pdf)). On the Maine coast the birds are present from early April to early September ([Journey North](https://journeynorth.org/livecam/osprey.html)). Bald eagles stay all year. **Inland Maine eagles ate 79% fish, favouring brown bullhead, white sucker and chain pickerel** ([Todd et al. 1982](https://hero.epa.gov/hero/index.cfm/reference/details/reference_id/3181496)), and they scavenge more when ice closes the water ([MDIFW, bald eagles](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/birds/bald-eagles.html)). Hooded mergansers breed on beaver ponds: where trapping stopped in south-central Maine, beaver colonies rose from 0.15 to 0.32 per km² and breeding mergansers reached 23–29 per 100 km² ([McCall et al. 1996](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/beaver-populations-and-their-relation-to-wetland-habitat-and-bree/)). Captive common mergansers ate 255–285 g of small trout a day ([Michigan DNR](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/IFR/ifrlibra/Research/abstracts/1700abs.htm)).

River otters in Maine eat mostly slow fish, "particularly … suckers and minnow", plus crayfish ([MDIFW, river otter](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/mammals/river-otter.html)). They feed most from dawn to mid-morning, use beaver lodges and log jams, and roam widely. An Idaho stream 16 km long supported 4–7 otters ([MDIFW otter assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/riverotter-speciesassessment.pdf)). An Arkansas otter spent 4,738 kJ a day, equal to **35–53 kg of prey a month**, with crayfish in 85% of summer scats and fish in 58% of winter ones ([Dekar, Magoulick & Beringer 2010](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70148714)). Otters dig through beaver dams to keep under-ice passages open between ponds ([University of Calgary thesis](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/23449)). Mink are nocturnal and active all year. Each night a mink works a core area of no more than about 300 m of shoreline, switching from fish in winter and spring to crayfish in summer and small mammals in autumn, at densities of 1.5 to more than 3 per km of good shore ([Allen 1984, USFWS HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-61.pdf)). Raccoons are about 97% nocturnal ([Michler 2016](https://www.projekt-waschbaer.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Michler_2016_Ph.D.Dissertation_Summary.pdf)). Beavers favour gradients under 6% and cut most wood within 30 m of the water ([Allen 1982, USFWS HSI](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-30.pdf)).

## Building the shore into a two-dimensional slice

### Three kinds of land fit a vertical slice

The sim is a vertical transect from the lake through the stream to the pond, so land can enter it in three ways. **First, in-plane banks at the two ends.** The lake's outer shore at the left edge and the pond's outer shore at the right are real geometry. They need a ground profile above the water (a `landY(x)` beside the existing `bedY(x)`), a substrate, vegetation zones, snow, nests, a waterline that moves with the level, and logs leaning into the water.

The lake bank should be Upper Hot Brook's "mostly rocky" shore. Boulders sit at the waterline, a 5–15% cobble beach rises behind them, and a 0.5–1.2 m ice-push rampart stands 1–3 m back, with sweetgale and alder behind it and spruce–fir beyond. One or two fallen spruce or pine lie with their root wads on the bank and their trunks sloping into less than 2 m of water. The pond bank should be Lower Hot Brook's northern and eastern wetland: a pickerelweed fringe in under 0.7 m of water, a sweetgale and leatherleaf mat within 0–0.3 m of the water, alder 1–3 m tall, then spruce, fir and cedar, with the pond's outlet leaving through a bulrush bed.

Land columns must be inert in the field grid, like the ~5,400 cells already below the bed. They must also stay out of `SLICE_M2`, which `config.js` ties to the world width, and out of the water cross-section behind `AREA_SCALE`. Otherwise adding land would silently rescale every density.

**Second, shores out of the plane.** Every column of the lake and pond faces a far shore hundreds of metres away. These shores act only as whole-lake areal sources: runoff, pollen, leachate and land insects. Every column of the stream has its two alder banks only metres away, out of the plane. They act as per-column canopy, insect fall-in and leaf litter at the real channel's rates, and are drawn as a backdrop.

**Third, a lumped catchment.** A land store holds the snowpack, soil water, forest-floor litter and the catchment's area and land-use mix. It turns weather into runoff and loads, and it is where the off-screen animals come from and go to: wood frogs and salamanders, wandering otters, eagles.

The water level should be a scalar per basin, not a remeshed grid. It sets the outflow through a rating curve, Q ∝ (h − h_sill)^1.5. It moves the drawn waterline across the in-plane bank. It also floods or strands a margin strip with its own small state: flooded area, emergent plants, pickerel eggs, loon-nest risk. The field grid can then stay fixed at summer-pool geometry.

### The air carries weather on the calendar clock and birds on the diel clock

The air above the water needs a state, not only a sky colour. It needs an air temperature (Houlton's monthly normals plus weather anomalies and a day cycle). It needs a wind: Caribou's monthly means, about 1.4 times the daily mean on summer afternoons and 0.65 times on summer nights, with a weaker winter cycle of about 1.15 and 0.9 (derived from the hourly normals), then reduced by sheltering where the fetch is short. It also needs a precipitation phase and rate (rain or snow by air temperature), a cloud cover and a snowfall. That state feeds every land–water mechanism: snowpack and melt, ice growth by freezing degree-days, turtle basking (the air–water gap), frog and salamander cues (air above 20 °C; rainy nights above 4–7 °C), emergence flight and steam fog. The sim runs two clocks, and each mechanism belongs on one of them. Hydrology, loads, phenology and snow belong on the calendar clock, where one real day is `BIOE.DAY`, about 4.93 s, so a storm's 1–2-day rise is 5–10 s and a recession half-life of 3.5–6 days is 17–29 s. Behaviour belongs on the 60-s diel clock, where an hour is 2.5 s, a turtle's basking bout is 2.5–5 s and a heron's dawn shift is 10 s. The existing four-state weather machine changes state every 10–26 s, which is 2–5 calendar days. It can stay as the visual layer if each state delivers precipitation in millimetres per calendar day, calibrated so the months match Houlton (50–103 mm) and days of 25 mm or more come about 7 times a year.

Agents in the air that affect the sim must use the seeded `core.rand`. They are the heron standing in the bank's shallows, the kingfisher on a snag 2–6 m up at the water's edge, the hovering osprey and the loon flying in and out, plus emerging insects leaving the band (an export) and land insects dropping in (an import). Fog, rain and snow particles, the pollen slick, rises, foliage colour and the snow on the banks are render-only. They use `Math.random`, and the sim never reads them.

### Divide shoreline fluxes by the slice's excess shore

The slice is a slab 27 m thick (`SLICE_M2` is 700 m² per 2,048 px), so the lake meets its in-plane shore along 27 m for every 700 m² of water: **0.039 m of shore per m²**. Upper Hot Brook has 0.0040, about **9.6 times less**. The pond has 0.056 against Lower Hot Brook's 0.0039, about **14 times less** (derived). Any per-metre-of-shore flux applied to the slab at its real rate would therefore overfeed the lake about tenfold. The rule should be to compute the lake-wide flux as F × (perimeter/area) of the real lake, which fixes the whole-lake budget. That flux is then spread with a kernel in normalised distance from the in-plane bank, so the near-shore gradient survives the stylised widths. The stream needs a different rule. Its slab is also 27 m across, against a real channel of 3–8 m (5–11 m at bankfull), so it should take the true areal rate, 2F/w, per square metre of drawn stream rather than spreading F over the slab.

The same logic applies to shore animals. The sim seeds 12 painted turtles and allows up to 41 in 0.13 ha of water (`scaleCount(TURTLE.SEED)`, `scaleCount(TURTLE.CAP)`): **90–310 per ha**, the density of the richest small ponds, not the 2.6–16 per ha of Shield and New Brunswick lakes. A Hot Brook-type slice holds one or two. Herps should be counted by in-plane shore length and shallow, vegetated area, not by `AREA_SCALE`.

### Close the budgets, save the state, and carve predators out of background mortality

Every new external term needs a ledger line beside the existing `inP`, `inN`, `outP` and `outN`: runoff P and N, atmospheric P, pollen, litter (C, N and P), leachate DOC and, if insects ever carry nutrients, the emergence export. Land stocks must be explicit pools: snowpack water, forest-floor litter, leaf cohorts in the water, and eggs in nests. Fish and insects are nutrient-neutral in the sim today. Land insects eaten and insects emerging therefore touch only the energy budget for now. Once insects carry N and P, the 70–99% of emergers that never return become a required sink of about 7–11 mg P/m² a year. Without it the closed budget silently keeps phosphorus that a real lake loses.

Several new pieces of state affect the future, so they must go into `Environment.toJSON`/`load` and `World.toJSON`/`parseSave`, with the save version bumped from v32 for the exact-resume test. They are the snowpack's depth, water equivalent and density; the ice layers (black, white and slush); the lake level per basin; the colour field; the litter cohorts; the turtle nests, with their eggs, degree-days and sex ratio; the land positions of frogs and turtles; the shore-predator agents; and the leaf state.

Background mortality needs adjusting as the predators arrive. `BIOE.M_OTHER` = 0.5 applies half of each species' published natural mortality as unexplained loss to "disease, parasites, herons, ospreys, otters, angling" (`world.js`). That published M already includes the shore predators. As herons, kingfishers, otters and mink become explicit, M_OTHER must fall so that total loss stays inside each species' published M of roughly 0.2–0.7 a year. A death audit by cause, size and depth should show it.

### A Hot Brook year with the shore in place

**Winter (yf 0.75–0.15).** Snow falls on the banks and on the ice at about 10:1. Ice thickens toward 40–60 cm, and each heavy snowfall depresses it, floods the snow and turns it to white ice. Under-ice light sits at 1–12% and opens to about 30% after a rain or thaw clears the snow. Diatoms grow only in the top metres. The pond's oxygen falls by 0.07–0.15 mg/L a day early in the winter and more slowly later, so a snowy, long winter, and only that, drives it toward 1–2 mg/L by March. On the sim's under-ice thresholds, smallmouth and suckers die first (0.8 mg/L), then white perch (0.5), then pumpkinseed, yellow perch and pickerel (0.35), while bullheads and shiners (0.2) survive. Frogs and turtles lie on the bottom in shallow water, painted hatchlings sit in nests in the frozen bank, and otters hunt under the ice from beaver lodges.

**Spring (yf 0.08–0.25).** The ground clears in early to mid-April. The freshet peaks on 15–19 April at 3–6 times mean flow, carrying particulate P and DOC, and raises the lake 15–30 cm, flooding the sedge and sweetgale margin just as the pickerel spawn. Ice-out comes about 23–28 April with shore leads first. On the falling limb of the freshet, as the water passes about 10 °C, white suckers run up the stream at night ([MDIFW Non-Sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). Loons arrive the same week, herons are already hunting, painted turtles haul out on sunny mornings, and salamanders and wood frogs cross the banks on rainy nights. Pollen falls for 45 days.

**Summer (yf 0.25–0.50).** Leaf-out in mid-May cuts the stream's light to 3–10% and ends its spring algal peak. Insects emerge in late May and June. Turtles nest on the open bank in mid-June, and raccoons find many of the nests. Bullfrogs call on warm spells. Land insects fall in from July to August, mostly onto the stream and the near-shore band. Flow sinks to 0.2–0.3 times the mean by late August and the lake to 10–30 cm below its pool, baring cobble and sand. The riffles become a shallow trap where herons and kingfishers take every fish over 5–7 cm that strays out of the 1.1 m pools. Thunderstorms (5–7 days a month in June–July) bring 1–2-day pulses, brief mixing and post-storm oxygen dips. Heat waves push shallow water toward 28–30 °C.

**Autumn (yf 0.50–0.75).** Snapping turtle hatchlings reach the water in September. Painted turtles and then frogs settle to the bottom from mid-September to October. Colour peaks in the first half of October. Leaf fall, centred on mid-October, loads the stream with litter that leaches 5–20% of its mass as DOC within two weeks and feeds shredders and chironomids through the winter. November rain and the end of transpiration bring a second high-flow season. The deep lake turns over late, while the shallow basins freeze first, around 1 December.

Each mechanism needs a gate check and a validation target before its parameters are trusted. The hydrograph should reproduce the April peak, the late-August minimum and the 1–2-day storm rise. Snow on the ice should give 1–12% light under the ice in a normal winter, kill fish in the pond only in heavy-snow winters, and never kill them in a basin 3.35 m deep on average. The predators should keep total predation inside published M, and a death audit by cause should show fish over 5–7 cm leaving water shallower than 20 cm by day. The same audit must confirm that the riffles do not silently zero the stream's recruitment. Painted turtle hatchlings should appear in April–May, not August.

## Conclusion

The intuitive picture of a lakeshore, with insects dropping from overhanging trees and leaves drifting onto the water, describes the stream and the first few metres of the lake, not the lake as a whole. At Hot Brook's scale, the land's leverage runs through water: through when the snow melts and how hard it rains, through how much colour the runoff carries, and through how much snow sits on the ice. Those are also the cheapest mechanisms to get right, because Maine's gauges and weather stations measure them better than any shoreline flux has been measured. The shore matters most as structure and as a hunting ground, and both effects work through the existing fish brains: logs as cover and spawning substrate, and a depth–size–light risk field the `fear` input can read. Built that way, angler-realistic habitat use can emerge rather than be scripted: big fish deep by day, perch on the wood, sunfish in the stream's only water deeper than a heron can wade.

The stylised geometry is the main trap. A slice with ten times the real shore per square metre and a littoral 3–25 times steeper than Hot Brook's will either drown the lake in shore subsidies or leave its shore predators nowhere to stand. Scaling by the real perimeter-to-area ratio and gentling the bank profile fix both. The largest unknowns can be closed cheaply. They are the Hot Brook catchment's area and land-use mix (a GIS lookup), the lakes' colour and whether the outlet is dammed (one visit or a DEP query), the under-ice oxygen decline of a shallow Maine lake (one winter's profiles), and the depth and number of fish in the stream's pools at noon and at dusk (a mask and a notebook). The last of these would test, in one afternoon, whether the sunfish are there for the warmth, for the depth, or for both.
