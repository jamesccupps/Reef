# Hot Brook rewrites the reef: shallow, native, colder

Re-basing Synapse Reef on Hot Brook changes the lake before it changes the fish. **Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake are 280–370-hectare saucers only 5.5 and 4.0 m deep at their deepest (means 3.3 and 2.7 m).** They are unstratified and mud-bottomed, 23 °C at the surface in August, with 41–59% of their soundings in the 0–3 m band the sim lacks, no cold-water habitat in the lakes or their tributaries, and ice from about December to the last week of April. Maine's fisheries agency lists nine fish in both lakes: smallmouth bass, white perch and chain pickerel as the principal fisheries, plus yellow perch, brown bullhead, white sucker, fallfish, blacknose dace and banded killifish. **Only two of the sim's eight species, yellow perch and brown bullhead, belong there.** Bluegill, common carp, northern pike and fathead minnow are absent or illegal introductions in Maine, and the rainbow trout has no habitat. The recommended set replaces carp with white perch, pike with chain pickerel, largemouth with smallmouth bass, bluegill with pumpkinseed, trout with white sucker and fathead with golden shiner, at roughly **60–150 kg/ha of fish rather than 180–260**, with the two perches the largest share and piscivores 10–20%. The re-base will not by itself cure the sim's real failure, sunfish and perch that do not replace themselves, because Maine's 208-day winter below 10 °C and its lower productivity push recruitment down. What the sim lacks is the compensation real centrarchids and percids show after the larval stage: juvenile survival that doubles when density halves, size-dependent first-winter survival, cannibalism, and maturity that comes earlier and smaller when growth is slow (northern pumpkinseed mature at **6–8 cm total length**, not the sim's 11 cm). The user's sighting of sunfish in Hot Brook's warm pools is unconfirmed rather than contradicted. No state list includes pumpkinseed, but the same lists omit golden shiner, common shiner and American eel, which occur in hundreds of Maine lakes, and pumpkinseed seek water of 27–29 °C, warmer than the lake ever gets.

## Hot Brook is two 3-metre saucers, not a stratified kettle

### The basins have no deep water, and most of their bottom lies in the missing 0–3 m band

The Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Wildlife (MDIFW) survey sheets give Upper Hot Brook Lake **912 acres (369 ha) and a maximum depth of 18 ft (5.5 m)**, and Lower Hot Brook Lake **713 acres (289 ha) and 13 ft (4.0 m)** ([MDIFW, Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf); [MDIFW, Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)). The Lakes of Maine database, seen only through search summaries, gives slightly smaller areas (890 and 684 acres) and mean depths of 11 ft (3.35 m) and 9 ft (2.74 m) ([Lakes of Maine, Upper](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1076); [Lakes of Maine, Lower](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1072)). A tally of the printed soundings agrees with those means and shows where the water is. In Upper, 41% of 83 soundings are 3 m or shallower, 78% are 4 m or shallower and 94% are 5 m or shallower. Its 16–18 ft water is confined to a northern basin, and the southern basin is a 10–14 ft flat with two islands. In Lower, 59% of 63 soundings are 3 m or shallower and none exceeds 4 m. Transect soundings under-sample the shore, so the true shallow share is higher. Both sheets describe a bottom that is "mostly muddy" and shores that are rocky with some sand, including a large beach on Upper's northeast shore. The margins are spruce–fir forest with wetlands. Both lakes are shallow, do "not stratify during summer months", and drain tributaries that hold no habitat for cold-water fish.

The ratio of mean to maximum depth shows how unusual these basins are. Across the USGS study of 43 Maine lakes that ratio has a median of **0.35** (range 0.21–0.55), with a median mean depth of 6.7 m and a median maximum of 18.3 m ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). Upper and Lower Hot Brook sit at **0.61 and 0.69**: flat floors with short margins. The sim's slice already has a Hot Brook-like ratio of 0.64, made of a flat 3.6 m shelf, a drop-off and a 14.4 m deep basin averaging 9.3 m. But it is about 2.7 times too deep, and its shallowest water is the depth at which Hot Brook's floor begins. The missing band is where Maine's fish breed. Smallmouth bass nest "in four to ten feet depth of water" (1.2–3 m) ([Ledlie, *The Black Bass in Maine*](https://amff.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Black_Bass_in_Maine_pp9-11.pdf)). Pumpkinseed nest in water under 1 m ([DFO pumpkinseed synopsis](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/Library/337845.pdf)), and yellow perch lay their egg strands at 1.0–3.7 m ([USFWS HSI, yellow perch](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-55.pdf)). Chain pickerel broadcast eggs in flooded marsh vegetation ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)), and New Brunswick redbreast sunfish nest in 43 cm of water ([COSEWIC, redbreast sunfish](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/redbreast-sunfish/chapter-6.html)).

Scale follows from area. At 280–370 ha, the sim's 0.07 ha slice is between a four-thousandth and a five-thousandth of either lake. It is therefore a transect through an open lake, not a closed pond, and fish moving between the slice and the rest of the lake is the realistic default. A faithful 4–5.5 m basin also fills only about a third of the current 14.6 m frame, so a Hot Brook geometry implies re-scaling the render or the slice area, together with every density that depends on them. If the landscape is to keep a deep, stratified basin, the realistic model is in the same town. **East Grand Lake (16,070 acres, 128 ft deep) read 70 °F at the surface and 48 °F at 110 ft in September 1952.** It has a very rocky shore with rocky reefs and annual drawdowns of 6–7 ft. Its cold-water community of landlocked salmon, lake trout, smelt, burbot and alewives lives alongside bass, both perches, pickerel and redbreast sunfish ([MDIFW, East Grand Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/aroostook/grand_lake_east.pdf)). That is a different lake with different fish, not a Hot Brook basin.

### Shallow Maine lakes mix all summer, so the bed stays oxic

Maine's stratification threshold is about 8–10 m of maximum depth. In the 43-lake study every lake shallower than about 9 m was unstratified, among them Portage (7.6 m), Pushaw (8.5 m) and Long Pond (5.5 m). Molasses Pond did not stratify despite a 14.3 m hole, because its mean depth was only 5.2 m. Lakes 11–15 m deep held hypolimnia of only 4–5% of their volume, small enough to go anoxic in late summer ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). Very shallow ponds read almost the same top and bottom: 78 and 77 °F at 5 ft in Great Pond, 77 and 74 °F at 6 ft in Sherman's Mill Pond ([MDIFW, Great Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/cumberland/great_pond.pdf); [MDIFW, Sherman's Mill Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/knox/shermans_mill_pond.pdf)). A Hot Brook basin should therefore be polymictic, with 0–3 °C between top and bottom, no thermocline and **no summer anoxia anywhere on the bed**. That reverses one of the sim's defining behaviours. Its deep bed now goes anoxic all summer, its larvae die in thin anoxic layers, and it has no profundal benthos. In a Hot Brook basin the whole muddy floor stays oxygenated and productive.

Where Maine lakes do stratify, late-summer depletion is common. Among the more productive stratified lakes in the USGS study, only one kept more than 2 mg/L of oxygen in its hypolimnion and 67% fell below 0.5 mg/L. Only seven lakes kept more than 5 mg/L, enough for salmon, lake trout and brook trout ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). The Maine Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) says funnel-shaped basins deplete naturally, and that depletion now starts earlier and lasts longer ([Maine DEP 2024 Integrated Report](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/305b/2024/2024_ME_IntegratedRpt-REPORT-DRAFT.pdf)). Turnover in a stratified lake injects phosphorus. In Brewer Lake, epilimnetic total phosphorus (TP) rose from 0.008 to 0.014 mg/L between mid-September and November, and an *Anabaena* bloom followed, a pattern "observed on other Maine lakes" ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). That autumn bloom belongs to a deep basin, not to Hot Brook.

Winter is the risk season for shallow lakes, but Maine documents winterkill only in very shallow, weedy ponds. Passaconway Pond (4 ft deep) fell to 0.8 ppm of oxygen in February 1961 and winterkills "during the more severe winters". Great Pond (5 ft) is most at risk in "years of heavy snowfall and thick ice cover", and Sherman's Mill Pond (6 ft) kills "especially during years when water flows are low" ([MDIFW, Passaconway Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/york/passaconway_pond.pdf); [MDIFW, Great Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/cumberland/great_pond.pdf); [MDIFW, Sherman's Mill Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/knox/shermans_mill_pond.pdf)). Unstratified Pushaw Lake (8.5 m) showed depressed oxygen under the ice but no kill ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). Nothing records a winterkill at Hot Brook, and its white perch fishery is indirect evidence against one. MDIFW says white perch persist in lakes about 25 ft deep "if the deeper water is not depleted of oxygen during the winter months" ([MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)), yet both Hot Brook lakes, at 13–18 ft, carry white perch as a principal fishery. The sim should run a slow under-ice decline over the mud to a low but non-lethal minimum, and reserve winterkill for 1–2 m ponds or heavy-snow winters.

### Ice holds the lakes from about December to the last week of April

The nearest long ice record is West Grand Lake in Washington County. Its mean ice-out was **April 28 over 1878–2008 (standard deviation 8.8 days, extremes April 2 and May 19) and April 24 over 1979–2008**. Aroostook's Eagle and Portage lakes average May 7 ([USGS OFR 2010-1214](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2010/1214/pdf/ofr2010-1214.pdf)). Ice-out moved about 9 days earlier in northern New England between 1850 and 2000 ([USGS FS 2005-3002](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/old.2005/3002/)). Freeze-up has no long Maine record. It usually comes in November or December ([Bangor Daily News](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2025/12/26/outdoors/early-season-ice-unpredictable/), via a search summary), so roughly 135–150 days of ice is an inference, not a measurement. Summer peaks are better known. August survey sheets across Maine read 69–78 °F (21–26 °C) ([MDIFW, Cold Stream Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/somerset/cold_stream_pond.pdf); [MDIFW, Passaconway Pond](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/york/passaconway_pond.pdf)), and Hot Brook read 74 °F (23.3 °C) in August 1949. Recent heat waves pushed lake surfaces to 28–30 °C ([Range Ponds 2024 assessment](https://www.rangeponds.org/s/Range-Ponds-2024-Water-Quality-Assessment-1.pdf)). The best single measure of a Maine growing season is the number of days below 10 °C: **208 ± 4 at Spednic Lake** on the Maine–New Brunswick border ([Dudley & Trial 2014, USGS SIR 2014-5164](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2014/5164/pdf/sir2014-5164.pdf)).

The sim's calendar is right in summer and wrong at both ends. Its surface temperature follows 12.5 + 13·sin(…) °C, peaking at 25.5 °C on August 1; ice forms below 1.2 °C and melts above 3.5 °C (`config.js`: `SEASON`, `ENV`, `ICE`). Worked through, those settings put ice-on between about December 14 and January 1 and ice-out between March 19 and April 2, depending on the weather offset. That is roughly 80–110 days of ice and **about 160 days below 10 °C**, so spring arrives three to five weeks early. A plain sine cannot fix this. Fitted to ice-out at the end of April and ice-on in early December, it reaches 15 °C only around June 10, two weeks after Spednic's modelled median start of smallmouth spawning on May 28 (derived). Real Maine lakes warm fast once the ice goes, so the seasonal curve needs a steep spring limb and a slower autumn one.

### Colour, more than algae, sets the light in eastern Maine

Maine lakes are mostly mesotrophic, soft and moderately coloured. DEP's 1,928 assessed lakes are **58% mesotrophic, 35% eutrophic and 7% oligotrophic by number**, or 72%, 16% and 12% by area ([Maine DEP 2024 Integrated Report](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/305b/2024/2024_ME_IntegratedRpt-REPORT-DRAFT.pdf)). Mean true colour is 20.7 SPU (range 0–197) and mean epilimnetic TP 11.2 µg/L ([Lakes of Maine report guide](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/data/Explanation%20of%20Individual%20Lake%20Water%20Quality%20Report%202017.pdf)). Statewide Secchi depth estimated from Landsat fell from 4.94 to 4.38 m between 1995 and 2010 ([UMaine summary of McCullough et al. 2013](https://elh.umaine.edu/?p=3101)). Phosphorus is the limiting nutrient ([Lake Stewards of Maine](https://www.lakestewardsofmaine.org/volunteer-programs-tools/water-quality-monitoring/understanding-water-quality-indicators/)). The water is soft: 12 µS/cm and about 3 mg/L alkalinity just under the ice, pH 6.6–7.2 in the summer epilimnion ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)).

No colour, Secchi, TP or chlorophyll value exists for either Hot Brook lake. Their spruce–fir and wetland margins point to a coloured mesotrophic lake. The closest analogue is Aroostook's Madawaska Lake: 38 SPU, Secchi 3.3 m, TP 13 µg/L and chlorophyll 6.9 µg/L ([Lakes of Maine, Madawaska Lake](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/data/texts/1802.htm)). That gives working defaults of 30–60 SPU, Secchi 2.5–4 m, TP 10–20 µg/L and chlorophyll 3–8 µg/L. In lakes above 30 SPU "phosphorus is tied up with the color producing compounds", so TP and Secchi overstate productivity ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). Dissolved colour, not phytoplankton, should therefore dominate light attenuation in a Hot Brook basin.

Plants follow the light. An Italian regression of plant colonisation depth on Secchi depth, Zc = 1.39·SD + 1.37 ([Azzella et al. 2014](https://jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/jlimnol.2014.932/639/3830)), puts rooted plants down to 4–5.5 m in a lake with 2–3 m Secchi: the entire Hot Brook floor, wherever the mud allows. Highly coloured Maine lakes carry bulrush (*Scirpus*) and floating-leaved plants such as *Nuphar* and *Pontederia*; clear lakes with rocky or sandy shallows carry only sparse pipewort (*Eriocaulon*) ([USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)). The phytoplankton has spring and autumn diatom peaks with a midsummer minimum; in Forest Lake, diatoms made up 86% of cell area in June and 14% in July (same source). The zooplankton is a soft-water mix of *Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, *Holopedium*, calanoid and cyclopoid copepods, and the rotifers *Conochilus* and *Asplanchna* ([Blair-Glantz et al. 2009](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2009NE/webprogram/Paper155679.html)). In calcium-poor lakes the jelly-coated *Holopedium* is replacing *Daphnia* ([PEARL, Queen's University](https://www.queensu.ca/pearl/media/jellification/jellification_pics.php)). The native virile crayfish made up 94% of the crayfish caught at 1,061 Maine stream sites ([Marquis et al. 2009](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2009NE/webprogram/Paper155692.html)).

## Only yellow perch and bullheads make the move to Maine

Maine has **69 freshwater fish species, 47 of them native**; the Downeast region has 47 species, 41 of them native ([MNAP, *Freshwater Species and Ecosystems in Maine*, ch. 3](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_3.pdf)). Its only native centrarchids are pumpkinseed and redbreast sunfish. Every black bass, black crappie, northern pike and muskellunge in the state is introduced ([MDIFW warmwater fish poster](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/warmwaterfishposter.pdf)), and so is the fathead minnow ([MDIFW Non-Sport and Commercial Management Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). Bluegill is a recent illegal arrival, confined mostly to the Sheepscot drainage. There it "greatly outnumber[s]" pumpkinseed and hybridises with them: one Savade Pond net caught 30 bluegill, 8 black crappie and 2 pumpkinseed ([MDIFW, *An Undetected Fish Invasion*](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/undetected-fish-invasion)). Smallmouth bass were stocked into the Saco, Androscoggin, Kennebec, Penobscot and St. Croix drainages in 1868–1881 and are native nowhere in New England ([Smithsonian NEMESIS](https://invasions.si.edu/nemesis/species_summary/550562)). MDIFW states flatly that "Bass are not native to Maine" ([MDIFW Black Bass Technical Work Group](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/BassTechnicalWorkGroupNov2019.pdf)). Chain pickerel were native only to southwestern Maine and were transplanted across the south, centre and east in the nineteenth century; Washington County lakes ran commercial pickerel fisheries into the 1950s ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)). White perch's pre-settlement range in Maine "is not known" ([MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)). The state's natural-areas programme describes "a relentless homogenization of fish assemblages" ([MNAP ch. 3](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_3.pdf)).

Regional frequencies say which fish a typical New England lake holds. In a random sample of 203 northeastern lakes, pumpkinseed occurred in **74%**, yellow perch in 72%, largemouth bass in 55% and chain pickerel in 45%. Bluegill occurred in 32%, smallmouth bass in 31%, white perch in 19% and redbreast sunfish in 14% ([Whittier et al. 1999](https://nysm.nysed.gov/bibcite/reference/21118)). Golden shiner was in 71% of lakes ([Whittier et al. 2000](https://nysm.nysed.gov/bibcite/reference/21097)), and brown bullhead, white sucker and banded killifish in 69%, 56% and 26% ([Whittier et al. 2001](https://nysm.nysed.gov/bibcite/reference/21057)). Within Maine, white sucker lives in 1,206 lakes, golden shiner in 851, yellow perch in 818, American eel in 683, common shiner in 462, fallfish in 461, banded killifish in 363 and fathead minnow in only 96 ([MDIFW Non-Sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). Chain pickerel occupy 781 waters and white perch 514 ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf); [MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)).

Against that background, Hot Brook holds a typical eastern Maine warm-water community. Both lakes list smallmouth bass, white perch and chain pickerel as principal fisheries, plus yellow perch, brown bullhead ("hornpout"), banded killifish, blacknose dace, fallfish and white sucker. They are managed for larger-than-average bass, with a special 25-fish bag limit on white perch ([MDIFW, Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf)). Smallmouth bass are certainly introduced and pickerel almost certainly so. White perch's status this far up the Penobscot–Mattawamkeag–Baskahegan system is unresolved, and yellow perch, bullhead, sucker, fallfish, dace and killifish are probably native. The total target rests on analogues, because no per-hectare standing crop for any eastern Maine warm-water lake was found. The one Maine rotenone study, Rupp and DeRoche (1965), covers three small trout lakes and could not be extracted ([Behnke archive scan](https://arc.lib.montana.edu/robert-behnke/objects/2491-36-10.pdf)). Michigan's compilation of 64 lakes gives the frame. Whole lakes ran from 10 to 360 lb/acre; "normal" lakes averaged **88 lb/acre (about 99 kg/ha)** and perch-only lakes 46 lb/acre (52 kg/ha). Soft-water northern lakes, the closest chemistry to Maine's, held only 10–62 lb/acre (11–70 kg/ha). Marsh Lake, for example, at an alkalinity of 6 ppm, held 52 lb/acre, of which 5 were smallmouth bass and 47 white sucker ([Schneider 1973](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1794.pdf)). The sim's 180–260 kg/ha matches Michigan's slow-growing bluegill lakes (182 lb/acre, about 204 kg/ha) rather than soft water. A target of **60–150 kg/ha, about 100 kg/ha or 7 kg in the slice**, splits the difference between soft-water chemistry and Hot Brook's warm, fully littoral basins.

Within that total, Schneider's balanced lakes put piscivores at **10–21% of fish biomass**, a prey-to-predator ratio of about 4:1 to 9:1 (computed from his tables). Northern smallmouth densities give the per-fish scale. Wisconsin's Mildred Lake held about 20 adults per hectare and Clear Lake 8–9.5 per hectare over 225 mm ([Wisconsin DNR, Mildred Lake](https://apps.dnr.wi.gov/doclink/lakes_fish-plant_reports/oneidamildredlake2005.pdf); [Clear Lake study](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/63bc1cce-f6af-4b30-8cc7-11f73f884ca4)), and Cosgrove Lake only 1.3 ([Cosgrove Lake 2015 summary](https://www.exploreflorencecounty.com/i/f/Cosgrove%20Lake%202015%20Summary.pdf)). Five to twenty adults per hectare is **0.35–1.4 adults in the slice**: one visiting bass, not a resident school.

Maine's own descriptions say the community is limited by food rather than by predators. Yellow perch in Maine are "commonly small in size … adults probably average closer to 8 inches" ([MDIFW Non-Sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). White perch "form very large populations that occasionally dominate", the usual problem is "too many small perch", and stunting can be "so extreme that a 6-year-old perch may be only 4 inches long" ([MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)). Bass are "prolific so populations can be crowded" ([MDIFW smallmouth bass](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/fisheries/species-information/smallmouth-bass.html)). Upstream Baskahegan Lake has "a large bass population … but growth rates are not good due to the large size of the population and insufficient forage" (from a search summary of the [MDIFW Baskahegan Lake sheet](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/baskahegan_lake.pdf)). Pickerel keep "moderate to high density populations wherever [they occur]" as long as the lake has shallow, weedy habitat ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)). Nova Scotia lakes invaded by pickerel yielded **0.047 fish per trap-hour, against 1.04 in pickerel-free lakes** ([Mitchell, LeBlanc & Heggelin](https://www.ap.smu.ca/~lcampbel/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf)). A Maine re-base should therefore produce crowded, slow-growing perches. The sim's current pathology, sunfish and perch dwindling toward zero, is the opposite of Maine's.

Fish-eating wildlife takes a small, size-selective share. A loon family eats about 360 kg over a 120-day breeding season (an often-quoted alternative is about 900 kg), mostly prey of 10–70 g ([Journey North](https://archive.journeynorth.org/tm/loon/FoodChain.html)). Southern Maine held about 3,174 adult loons in 2025, more than double the 1983 count ([WBUR/AP](https://www.wbur.org/news/2025/12/13/more-loons-are-filling-maines-lakes-with-their-ghost-like-calls/), via a search summary). One pair with two chicks on Upper Hot Brook would remove about **1–2.5 kg/ha a year, or 70–175 g a year from the slice**: a handful of fish (derived). An osprey nest receives about 5.4 fish a day averaging 157 g ([J. Raptor Research 25:109](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v025n04/p00109-p00112.pdf)), roughly 0.2 kg/ha a year on a lake this size (derived). Bald eagles take mostly bullheads and suckers ([Mitchell et al., citing Dunstan & Harper and Todd et al.](https://www.ap.smu.ca/~lcampbel/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf)). Otters shift from 14% fish in summer scats to 84–88% in winter ([Missouri Conservationist](https://mdc.mo.gov/magazines/conservationist/2005-06/otter-appetites)).

The trout slot has nothing to hold in Hot Brook. MDIFW finds no cold-water habitat in either lake or any tributary, and Baskahegan Stream downstream "supports a good seasonal trout fishery" while brook trout "are seldom caught in the lake" (search summary of the [Baskahegan Lake sheet](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/baskahegan_lake.pdf)). If brook trout return later as stream visitors, the official FB4 set exists (Hartman & Cox 2008) ([FB4 parameter file](https://github.com/jim-breck/FB4/blob/master/Parameters_official.csv)). Lab growth slows above 16 °C, stress rises at 21–22 °C and positive growth ends at 23.4 °C ([Chadwick & McCormick 2017](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70209864)). Maine age-3 trout average 338 mm in lakes and 198 mm in streams ([MDIFW, *Brook Trout: Biology and Management*](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/Biology_and_Management.pdf)).

## The 200-metre connector is a warm riffle that floods in April and trickles in August

### Hot Brook is short, rocky and fed by warm lake water

The lakes are joined by "a short (1/8 mile) non-navigable connecting stream", about 200 m long. It "is rocky and hard bottomed [but] does not contain any suitable habitat for coldwater fish", and a boater "must be prepared to paddle, pole, or walk a boat through parts of the waterway" ([MDIFW, Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)). Lower Hot Brook drains to Baskahegan Stream, which reaches the Penobscot through the Mattawamkeag River ([Wikipedia, Danforth CDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danforth_%28CDP%29,_Maine), via a search summary). No survey, temperature record, discharge or drainage area exists for Hot Brook itself, so its dimensions come from Maine's regional channel curves. Bankfull width is 7.67·DA^0.52 ft, bankfull depth 0.594·DA^0.34 ft and bankfull discharge 5.19·DA^1.05 cfs, for a drainage area DA in square miles. Bankfull flow is about three times the mean annual flow and is equalled or exceeded about 8% of the time, roughly 30 days a year ([Dudley 2004, USGS SIR 2004-5042](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2004/5042/pdf/sir2004-5042.pdf)). A 5–20 square-mile drainage gives a channel **5.4–11.1 m wide and 0.31–0.50 m deep at bankfull**, carrying 0.8–3.4 m³/s. A rocky riffle that must be waded in places fits the low end of that range.

The flow follows snowmelt. In the Mattawamkeag River, Hot Brook's downstream basin (1934–2026, about 640 mm of runoff a year), mean monthly flow as a multiple of the annual mean runs as follows ([USGS NWIS 01030500](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01030500&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)):

| Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.61 | 0.49 | 0.95 | **3.31** | 1.99 | 0.75 | 0.42 | **0.30** | 0.32 | 0.60 | 1.13 | 1.13 |

Small Washington County brooks show the same pattern, damped where ponds and wetlands store water. Libby Brook (6.4 square miles) runs about 1.8 times its mean in April and about half its mean from July to September ([USGS NWIS 01021470](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01021470&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)).

The connector's temperature is set by the lake it drains. As the outflow of an unstratified lake it should track the lake surface, about 22–26 °C in August. That is an inference, but Maine survey sheets repeatedly describe outlets as warm and spring-fed inlets as cold ([MDIFW, Trout Pond](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/penobscot/trout_pond_lowell.pdf), via a search summary). Exposure to the sun adds heat on top of that: forest harvest that removed shade raised western Maine stream temperatures by 1.0–4.4 °C ([AFS 2015 abstract](https://afs.confex.com/afs/2015/webprogram/Paper20627.html), via a search summary). In Maine's best-studied lake-to-lake connector, the Rapid River, 96% of brook trout and 72% of landlocked salmon moved more than 1 km to thermal refuges once river water topped 25 °C, while **68% of smallmouth bass made no long moves and stayed in the warm river** ([Jackson & Zydlewski 2009](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70171380)). Lake outlets are also rich feeding grounds. At one outlet, filter-feeding caddisflies ran 6,500 per m² at the outlet, 1,427 at 200 m and 209 at 3,400 m downstream ([Glacier National Park outlet study](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/food-quality-and-hydropsychid-caddisfly-density-in-a-lake-outlet-), via a search summary), so Hot Brook's entire length lies in the enriched zone below a lake. Barriers decide which fish pass: a mile of falls on the Big Musquash Stream outlet has kept bass out for more than a century ([Wired2Fish](https://www.wired2fish.com/news/emergency-rule-enacted-to-eliminate-bass-from-maine-lake)). Beaver are the other gatekeeper. South-central Maine held 0.15–0.32 colonies per km², and colony density doubled within a few years where trapping stopped, adding 36% to open water ([McCall et al. 1996](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/beaver-populations-and-their-relation-to-wetland-habitat-and-bree/)).

### Spring runs and summer nests set the stream's calendar

MDIFW describes minnow spawning migrations as "limited to short movements upstream or beyond the shoals of a lake" ([MDIFW Non-Sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). The connector's big event is the white sucker run. Suckers "run in streams during the early spring to spawn in the gravelly areas of shallow, swift-flowing streams. Generally, spawning takes place at night", and a Maine trap-net study found white suckers were **96% of the catch during the spawning run** (same source). Runs begin near 10 °C ([DFO white sucker](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/species-especes/profiles-profils/white-sucker-meunier-noir-eng.html)). Spawning sites lie in water under 30 cm deep flowing at 14–90 cm/s ([USFS white sucker assessment](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/boise/AWAE/projects/fish_cattle/White%20sucker.pdf), via a search summary). White perch spawn in lake shallows at 59–60 °F, but "several streams provide perch fishing although the perch may not be resident populations, but rather migrants from nearby lakes and ponds" ([MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)). ### Where Maine's records and the sunfish sighting disagree

The user has watched sunfish gather in warm pools of the stream between the lakes in summer. The state's records do not show it. Neither MDIFW sheet lists a sunfish of any kind, in the August 1949 survey or in the 1965 and 2004 revisions. No stream survey, temperature record or fish record exists for Hot Brook itself ([MDIFW, Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf); [MDIFW, Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)). Three things weaken the inference that sunfish are absent. First, the sheets are summary forms built around a sport-fish survey: they carry a generic "minnows" entry, and both print the same templated "75 feet – 71 °F" reading for lakes only 13 and 18 ft deep. Second, they omit fish that are among the commonest in Maine lakes: golden shiner (851 lakes), common shiner (462) and American eel (683) ([MDIFW Non-Sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)). Absence from these lists is weak evidence of absence from the lakes. Third, pumpkinseed is the most widespread fish in northeastern lakes (74%) and native to Maine ([Whittier et al. 1999](https://nysm.nysed.gov/bibcite/reference/21118)). Redbreast sunfish, Maine's other native *Lepomis*, is listed next door in East Grand Lake ([MDIFW, East Grand Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/aroostook/grand_lake_east.pdf)).

The behaviour itself is undocumented anywhere. **No source found describes sunfish congregating in warm stream pools, in Maine or elsewhere.** The nearest evidence shows only that pumpkinseed move. In a Polish lake, PIT-tagged pumpkinseed shifted a mean of 43 m (maximum 156 m), and 49% of relocations happened in autumn ([Zięba et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/10.3389/conf.fmars.2015.03.00123/2930/XV_European_Congress_of_Ichthyology/all_events/event_abstract)). A European tagging project, known only from a search summary with uncertain attribution, traced pumpkinseed from lakes into connected streams as far as 2.5 km downstream at about 30 m a day ([EU CORDIS project 501750](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/501750/reporting/it)). In the Detroit River, pumpkinseed presence rose with water temperature where bluegill presence did not ([University of Windsor](https://scholar.uwindsor.ca/glierpub/325)). Thermal preference supplies a mechanism. Pumpkinseed have a final temperature preferendum of **27.7 °C** ([Hasnain et al. 2013, via the ORNL compilation](https://hydrosource.ornl.gov/?p=732)). Their behavioural optimum in Lake Monona was 27–29 °C by day and 28.5–32 °C at night ([Yoder 2012, MBI thermal database](https://www.vermontlaw.edu/sites/default/files/Assets/enrlc/cases/vermont-yankee/2012-05-25%20MBI%20Report%20Appendix.pdf)). Both are several degrees above the 23 °C Hot Brook surface of August 1949. A riffle carrying lake water at lake temperature offers no extra warmth. A shallow, sunlit, slack pool can heat a few degrees above its inflow, as shade removal does to Maine streams, and a fish seeking 27–29 °C would find it.

That hypothesis is assembled from sourced pieces; nobody has observed it. It predicts the gathering peaks on calm, sunny July and August afternoons and thins in cool or high-flow spells. The verdict is that **Maine's records neither confirm nor refute the observation**, and the observation is the best local evidence available. The sim should treat a *Lepomis* as present, parameterise it as pumpkinseed because pumpkinseed is far better documented, and carry redbreast as the named alternative. Pool use should emerge from each fish's thermal preference and the stream's own heat budget rather than be scripted. One summer visit with a mask or a minnow trap would settle both the species and how warm the pools run.

## Northern fish grow slower, mature smaller and winter longer

### Five of the eight recommended species have no bioenergetics model of their own

The official Fish Bioenergetics 4.0 (FB4) table has sets for **yellow perch (Kitchell et al. 1977), smallmouth bass (Whitledge et al. 2003; Shuter & Post 1990) and brown bullhead (Hartman et al. 2017)**. It has none for pumpkinseed, redbreast sunfish, chain pickerel, white perch, white sucker or golden shiner ([FB4 parameter file](https://github.com/jim-breck/FB4/blob/master/Parameters_official.csv); [fb4package species database](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/fb4package/vignettes/fb4-species-database.html)). Each gap has a defensible surrogate, and each surrogate has a known bias.

Pumpkinseed must borrow the bluegill set. Bluegill respiration runs **1.21–1.69 times** the routine oxygen consumption measured on 30 g pumpkinseed at 5–25 °C (Roberts 1964; derived from the [FishBase oxygen list](https://www.fishbase.se/physiology/OxygenDataList.php?ID=3372&GenusName=Lepomis&SpeciesName=gibbosus&fc=302&StockCode=3568)). A pumpkinseed on the bluegill set therefore pays 20–70% extra maintenance. One study with unknown acclimation is too thin to justify a correction, but enough to run respiration × 0.6–0.8 as a sensitivity test. Michigan pond modellers had to raise the bluegill consumption intercept by 50% to reproduce observed growth ([Breck 1997](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1995.pdf)). Both biases point the same way, toward the slow yearling growth the sim now shows.

Smallmouth bass need two sets. At maximum ration the Whitledge set gives positive net energy for a 100 g fish at 15–30 °C, but it is negative for a 5 g fish at every temperature (−168 J/g/d at 20 °C). Solving the set for the break-even size puts it at about 10 g at 20 °C and 20–25 g at 15 and 25 °C (derived from the [FB4 parameter file](https://github.com/jim-breck/FB4/blob/master/Parameters_official.csv)). Whitledge therefore belongs only above about **30 g (about 13 cm)**; below that, the largemouth (Rice et al. 1983) set the sim already runs is the stand-in. The Shuter and Post row as stored in FB4 is negative at maximum ration at every size and temperature tested, and should not be used.

Chain pickerel must borrow the northern pike set (Bevelhimer et al. 1985). A 2008 revision for juvenile esocids found that model overestimates consumption by 39–52% ([TAFS 137(6)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1577/T07-229.1)), and a tiger muskellunge test found the overestimate worst in winter and at low ration ([Chipps et al. 2000](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1577/1548-8659%282000%29129%3C0186:GAFCBT%3E2.0.CO;2)). Scaling the pike set's maximum consumption by 0.65–0.75 corrects for that (derived). One defect resolves itself in the swap. The Bevelhimer curve peaks at **24.6 °C** because it was fitted to small pike, which made it wrong for a 65 cm pike. Chain pickerel adults are pike-juvenile sized: Maine ice anglers kept fish averaging 43 cm and 1.2 lb ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)). Their final preferendum is 24–25.5 °C ([ORNL compilation](https://hydrosource.ornl.gov/?p=732)). The small-pike curve fits the pickerel almost exactly.

White perch's closest surrogate is its congener the striped bass, which has an age-staged FB4 set (Hartman & Brandt 1995) ([FB4 parameter file](https://github.com/jim-breck/FB4/blob/master/Parameters_official.csv)). Young-of-year white perch reached a maximum consumption of about 0.42 g/g/d at 25.1 °C ([Maryland Sea Grant, Behr 1993](https://www.mdsg.umd.edu/reu/students/peter-behr-university-miami)). Hypoxia at or below 40% saturation raised their routine metabolism up to fourfold, cut growth threefold and halved consumption ([Hanks & Secor 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00227-010-1608-0)). White sucker has no analogue; a Kitchell-type curve shaped like yellow perch's (optimum about 22–23 °C, maximum about 28 °C) matches its 17–24 °C preferred range ([Ontario Freshwater Fishes, white sucker](https://www.ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=19)). Its energy density can be taken from a Colorado measurement of about 5,585 J/g ([measured energy densities](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Measured-energy-densities-of-A-Arctic-Char-B-Brown-Trout-C-kokanee-D-Lake_fig2_318870863)). Both choices are uncalibrated. Golden shiner can take FB4's Quebec "dace" set (Trudel & Boisclair 1994: optimum 26 °C, maximum 29 °C, energy density 5,522 J/g). Its respiration should be checked against a measured standard rate of 131 mg O₂/kg/h for 5.3 g shiners ([Beecham et al. 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1577/A07-079.1)); the test temperature is unstated, and that rate is roughly half the fathead set's.

The consumption curves explain winter behaviour. Evaluated for a 100 g fish (derived from the FB4 rows), perch and pike keep 1–2% of body weight a day at 4 °C, while the basses fall to 0.3–0.5%:

| Maximum consumption, g/g/d, 100 g fish | 4 °C | 10 °C | 20 °C | 25 °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yellow perch (adult) | 0.018 | 0.032 | 0.067 | 0.067 |

That matches the field. Perch are "active all winter under ice" ([Scott & Crossman, part 8](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/Library/52369_Pt8.pdf)). Maine ice anglers caught 1.18 pickerel a day against 0.49 in summer ([MDIFW chain pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)). Maine smallmouth stop feeding and growing below 50 °F ([MDIFW Black Bass Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/blackbass.pdf)).

Three comparisons with the sim's current numbers matter most. The sim's sunfish matures at 11 cm with a Michigan bluegill growth curve (9.7 cm at age 2). A northern pumpkinseed is only 5.8–7.5 cm TL at age 2 (derived with the Fernando conversion, TL = 2.761 + 1.202·SL mm), but it matures at about that size. The swap lowers the size bar by 3–5 cm, which equals one to two years of northern pumpkinseed growth: Ontario fish add 14–35 mm of standard length a year at ages 1–3 ([Copp et al. 2004](https://www.reabic.net/publ/Copp_et%20al_2004_Lepomis%20gibbosus.pdf); derived). The sim's pike M of 0.30 is about half the pickerel's 0.60–0.66, reflecting the pickerel's shorter life and faster turnover. The sim's under-ice oxygen thresholds are too forgiving for centrarchids and esocids. Its rule scales each 25 °C lethal value by e^(0.055·(T − 25)), which at 2 °C gives 0.21 mg/L for the sunfish, 0.31 for the bass and 0.14 for the pike. Cooper and Washburn's natural thresholds are 0.6 for bluegill and largemouth and 0.3–0.4 for pumpkinseed, perch and pike (derived comparison).

Gape and body depth decide which of these fish can eat which. A 10 cm pumpkinseed is about 37 mm deep, and by the Fernando gape equations a bass needs to be about 32 cm (smallmouth) or 38 cm (largemouth) long to swallow it head-first ([Fernando et al. 2018](https://eaglehill.us/SENAonline/articles/SENA-17-2/20-Fernando.shtml); derived). Yellow perch, by contrast, is the fish most often identified in Ontario smallmouth stomachs ([Scott & Crossman, part 7](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/Library/52369_Pt7.pdf)).

### Maine's winter is the smallmouth's real limit

Maine smallmouth bass face "approximately 200+ day" periods below 50 °F, which sets the species' northern limit in the state, and late re-nesting leaves young "too small to survive" ([MDIFW Black Bass Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/blackbass.pdf)). USGS implemented Shuter et al.'s (1980) first-year model for Spednic Lake, and its equations transfer directly ([Dudley & Trial 2014](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2014/5164/pdf/sir2014-5164.pdf)):

| Stage | Rule |
|---|---|
| Spawning trigger | daily temperature above 15 °C and at most 27 °C, after 10–150 degree-days above 10 °C |
| Egg-to-fry mortality | set by the minimum daily temperature Tmin over the 15 days to swim-up: 1 below 10 °C, 3.0 − 0.2·Tmin at 10–15 °C, 0 at 15–27 °C |
| Swim-up and growth | swim-up at 9.1 mm; then 0.12·T − 1.7 mm a day at 14.2–25.5 °C, and 1.4 mm a day up to 31.5 °C |
| First-winter survival | every fish shorter than Cmin = 0.1938·WD + 7.917 mm dies and every fish longer than Cmax = 0.3375·WD + 22.083 mm survives, where WD is the number of days below 10 °C; mortality falls linearly in between |

With Spednic's 208 winter days the thresholds are 48 and 92 mm. Measured young-of-year of 71–76 mm then lose about **42%** over their first winter (derived). The sim's 160-day winter would put the thresholds at 39 and 76 mm, and the same fish would lose about **7%** (derived). Moving the sim to a Maine calendar therefore multiplies first-winter loss for a typical young bass about sixfold, which is why the calendar change must wait for the compensation described next. Spednic's year classes failed after the cold, wet summers of 1986 and 1996.

## Compensation lives in the juvenile year, not the larval days

The sim's young-of-year die at density-independent rates: a larval rate of 0.08 per day divided by length in cm, plus a juvenile rate of 0.035 per day at 20 °C. In its latest runs about 120,000–140,000 hatched sunfish become about 300 by October, 30 by March and 10 recruits, whether the spawners are many or few. Real lakes keep the same density independence at the larval stage. In Oneida Lake, New York, daily mortality of larval yellow perch was 0.042 and unrelated to density (P > 0.3), and larval growth was not density-dependent either. Growth in the open-water juvenile stage was strongly density-dependent (R² 0.507), and a winter mortality index fell as October length rose (R² 0.382). The authors called this "delayed compensation … through the first winter and second spring" ([Irwin et al. 2009](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/138/1/99/7888582)). The sim's larval rate integrates to a total mortality of about 1.35 over the larval stage, close to Oneida's 1.22 (derived). The larval stage is right; the compensation that should follow it is missing.

A model calibrated to Oneida's long record shows the size of that compensation ([Rose et al. 2001, Table 1](https://aquaticecology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2012/07/Rose_etal_FishFisheries.pdf)):

| 18 mm juveniles per ha | Survival, 18 mm to age 2 | Age-2 recruits per ha | Mature at age 4 | Spawners per ha |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60,594 | 0.41% | 205 | 24% | 29.1 |
| 30,329 | 0.85% | 210 | 41% | 35.4 |
| 15,325 | 1.56% | 221 | 54% | 44.2 |

**Survival roughly doubles each time density halves, so recruitment stays within about 8% across a fourfold range of juveniles.** Overall survival from egg to age 2 is only about 0.019%, close to the sim's own level. The sim's fault is not that survival is low but that it never rises when fish are scarce. Compensation in the model came from four sources: release from walleye predation, faster growth of scarcer juveniles (whose longer bodies escape predators), faster adult growth that doubled the share mature at age 4, and more spawners.

Centrarchids compensate at least as strongly. In four Michigan lakes, copper sulphate killed bluegill eggs and fry on the nests for the whole spawning season. The next autumn's age-0 catch was only marginally lower than in control lakes, and the investigators concluded the poisoning killed few if any more bluegill than would have died anyway. Across 38 Midwestern ponds, bluegill standing stocks of 112–168 kg/ha produced the most age-0 fish by fall, with fewer at lower and higher stocks. In Lake Opinicon, 94% of predation on bluegill nests from the water column was by other bluegill. At very high adult density in Blueberry Pond, only about 2% of adults attempted to spawn, and the same fish spawned normally in sparse ponds ([Wisconsin DNR bluegill review 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf)). For smallmouth, percent survival of young fell as their abundance rose in three field datasets, and only 12–47% of mature males in Lake Opeongo spawned in a given year ([Chu 2001](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/4beb3aaa-bf92-48ed-ab14-11f2ba6ac752/content)). An individual-based smallmouth model with cannibalism produced Beverton–Holt recruitment at low density and overcompensating Ricker recruitment at high density ([Dong & DeAngelis 1998](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/consequences-cannibalism-and-competition-food-a-smallmouth-bass-population-individual)). For Florida largemouth bass, a density-independent model had no support against the Ricker model ([Shaw & Allen 2016](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de5cf70f3e94b9e5ce58ec)).

Formal stock–recruitment parameters for these families are scarce. Across 49 bony fishes the maximum annual reproductive rate at low density ranges from 0.4 to 13.5 replacement spawners per spawner, with walleye at 2.48 and pike at 1.66, and no *Perca*, *Lepomis* or *Micropterus* entries ([Froese & Luna 2004](https://www.fishbase.de/rfroese/NoFecundRel.pdf)). Ricker refits by life-history group give mean steepness of 0.70 for periodic and 0.57 for equilibrium strategists, with log-recruitment variability of 0.55–0.71 ([Rose et al. 2001](https://aquaticecology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2012/07/Rose_etal_FishFisheries.pdf)). A defensible prior for sunfish and perch is an annual rate of 1.5–5. With adult survival of 0.5–0.6, that implies a Beverton–Holt steepness of about 0.5–0.75 (derived with the standard Myers relations).

The model form follows from the mechanism. If juvenile mortality rises linearly with cohort density, M(N) = M0 + M1·N, over a stage of length T, integrating gives Beverton–Holt recruitment R = a·E/(1 + b·E), with a = e^(−M0·T) and b = (M1/M0)·(1 − e^(−M0·T)). Writing M = M0·(1 + N/N50) is the same thing: per-capita mortality doubles at density N50. Ricker curves arise instead when mortality depends on the initial cohort or on cannibals. Density-dependent growth alone can regulate a population. It was significant in 9 of 16 long series and acts on asymptotic length rather than the growth coefficient ([Lorenzen & Enberg 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1690856)), but it matters only through survival and maturity. One trap to avoid: a predator with a saturating (type II) response makes juvenile mortality *depensatory*, as walleye did to Oneida perch in the 1960s ([Irwin et al. 2009](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/138/1/99/7888582)). Predators will not rescue a sparse sunfish population unless they switch away from scarce prey.

The first winter adds a size filter that density sharpens, because crowded cohorts grow less. Age-0 bluegill of 20–30 mm lost more than 55% over 150 days at 4–9 °C *even when fed*, and mortality was strongly size-selective against 50–60 mm fish held alongside them ([Shoup & Wahl 2011](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article-abstract/140/5/1298/7877534)). An 18-lake study proposed 60 mm as the minimum length for a yellow perch to survive a Great Lakes-basin winter ([University of Windsor thesis](https://scholar.uwindsor.ca/etd/1485), via a search summary), and Lake Saint-Pierre perch survived well above 66–73 mm ([Boussion 2025](https://constellation.uqac.ca/10286/1/Boussion_Imanol_2025_m%C3%A9moire.pdf), via a search summary). Those thresholds are not universal. Nebraska perch showed size-selective winter loss in both directions ([Kaemingk, University of Nebraska](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/natrespapers/681), via a search summary), and stunted Lake Jesse perch in Nova Scotia persist with autumn young near 36 mm ([Scott & Crossman, part 8](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/Library/52369_Pt8.pdf)), so the filter should be a soft sigmoid rather than a cliff. In a Canadian Shield lake, open-water age-0 pumpkinseed approached 35 mm by September, and the survival threshold rises with winter length ([Environmental Biology of Fishes 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10641-026-01836-6), via a search summary). In a northern-range lake, largemouth bass entering winter below about 50–60 mm were hit hardest ([Post, Kitchell & Hodgson 1998](https://curate.nd.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Interactions_among_adult_demography_spawning_date_growth_rate_predation_over_winter_mortality_and_recruitment_in_largemouth_bass_in_a_northern_lake/24723597)).

Maturity is the second buffer, and it is highly plastic. Most Ontario pumpkinseed mature in their third year, and all are mature by ages 2–5 ([Copp et al. 2004](https://www.reabic.net/publ/Copp_et%20al_2004_Lepomis%20gibbosus.pdf)). In two winterkill ponds beside Lake Opinicon they matured **1–2 years earlier and more than 20 mm smaller** than in the lake ([QUBS, Fox & Keast 1991](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/effect-of-overwinter-mortality-in-reproductive-life-history-characteristics-of-pumpkinseed-lepomis-gibbosus-populations)). Crowding pulls in both directions. High density went with earlier maturity and more gonad investment across 27 Ontario populations ([QUBS](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/growth-density-and-interspecific-influences-on-pumpkinseed-sunfish-life-histories), via a search summary). Yet in a stunted lake fewer mature age-2 females spawned, and they spawned late ([Danylchuk & Fox 1994](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00004929)). Female yellow perch across 13 Great Lakes stocks reached 50% maturity at 1.9–5.2 years and 144–233 mm, and males at 0.8–3.0 years and 128–190 mm. Faster-growing stocks matured younger but larger ([Feiner et al. 2017](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/62247/noaa_62247_DS1.pdf)), and stunted Nova Scotia perch mature at age 2 near 95 mm ([Munro & MacMillan 2012](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/nsis/article/download/nsisv47-1munro/3151)). Parental male bluegill in Minnesota reached 50% maturity at 135–177 mm and 4.8–6.8 years ([Jacobson 2005](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_ReducedBGBagEvaluation.pdf)), while about 20% of males mature early as cuckolders ([Wisconsin DNR review](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf)). A **probabilistic maturation reaction norm**, with maturity probability rising with length at an age-dependent midpoint, gives the sim the Oneida buffer for free: when fish are scarce and grow fast, more mature at a given age.

## Twelve changes, ranked by what each unlocks

The order follows dependency. The first two changes give the populations the self-correction real ones have. Without them, every Maine-specific change that lowers recruitment (a longer winter, lower productivity, a lower biomass target) will drive the lake toward its refuge floors faster. The next five define the system: geometry and species. The rest calibrate it, and the stream comes last because it needs a multi-basin world and its headline behaviour has the least evidence. A few sourced values should be kept out of the sim. Shuter and Post's smallmouth row as stored in FB4 cannot produce growth. FishBase's white perch length–weight relation is two to four times too light against Maine weights. Moore's 1942 caged-fish oxygen thresholds were rejected by Doudoroff and Shumway, and an unsourced 36.7 °C "lethal" value for chain pickerel circulates in the Ontario database. Several sources also disagree with each other. MDIFW's Non-Sport Plan marks golden shiner, banded killifish and bridled shiner as not native, against MNAP's count of both Maine killifishes as native. White sucker maturity runs 2–4 years in Ontario but 5–8 years in Maine. Golden shiner's maximum age is 5 years per MDIFW and 9 per FishBase. Pumpkinseed fecundity is "up to 1000" in FishBase's summary against 2,451–10,633 in Ontario. Where Maine and Ontario disagree, the Maine figure should win for the lake and the Ontario figure should serve as the plasticity range.

## Conclusion

The Hot Brook anchor inverts what the sim spent most of its physics on. Stratification, the summer-anoxic deep bed and the post-turnover cyanobacteria bloom belong to the deep East Grand-type lake next door, not to Hot Brook. Hot Brook is the littoral zone the sim never had, extended across the whole basin, with an oxygenated muddy floor, plants potentially everywhere, and nests in the top 3 m. Several current defects dissolve in the swap rather than needing fixes. The small-pike thermal curve that misfit a 65 cm pike fits a 43 cm pickerel. The apex-density problem shrinks with a predator a quarter the size. The carp anomaly disappears. A sunfish that matures at 6–8 cm closes a year of the generation gap that is starving the sim's recruitment. Maine's real pathology is the reverse of the sim's: lakes crowded with stunted white and yellow perch, limited by food rather than predators. A Maine-faithful sim is therefore one whose populations regulate themselves through slow growth and plastic maturity, which is exactly the compensation it lacks today.

The largest uncertainties sit where a modest field or archive effort would remove them. They are the Hot Brook lakes' own water chemistry and ice dates, a Maine standing-crop figure (Rupp and DeRoche 1965), the species and temperature of the sunfish in Hot Brook's pools, and real bioenergetics for white perch, white sucker and chain pickerel. The model should treat those surrogate-driven species, together with the bluegill-borrowing pumpkinseed, as the first place to look when long runs drift. It should also treat the stream's sunfish as a test of emergence: if fish that prefer 27–29 °C find warm pools on their own, the sim has earned the observation rather than scripted it.
