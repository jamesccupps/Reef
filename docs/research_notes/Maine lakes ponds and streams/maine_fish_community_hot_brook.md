# Maine fish communities: Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lakes, Hot Brook, and eastern Maine warm/coolwater lakes

Scope: the fish species set, native and introduced status, abundances, lake–stream movements, and fish-eating wildlife for a realistic Maine (eastern and northern first) version of the lake simulation. The focus water is Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake (Danforth, T8 R4 NBPP, Washington County; Bancroft Twp., Aroostook County).

Unit conversions used throughout: 1 acre = 0.4047 ha; 1 lb/acre = 1.121 kg/ha; °C = (°F − 32)·5/9.

## Q1. Which species make up Maine warm/coolwater lake and pond communities, and which are native vs introduced (and where)?

### Takeaway
Maine has about 69 freshwater fish species, about 47 of them native. The warm/coolwater lake community is built from these groups:
- **Panfish:** yellow perch, white perch, and pumpkinseed, with redbreast sunfish in some waters.
- **Predators:** chain pickerel and smallmouth bass (largemouth bass in places).
- **Bottom and littoral fish:** brown bullhead and white sucker.
- **Minnows:** golden shiner, fallfish, common shiner, creek chub, dace, and banded killifish.

Every black bass (smallmouth, largemouth), black crappie, bluegill, northern pike, muskellunge, and fathead minnow population in Maine is **introduced**. Chain pickerel and white perch are native to the state but only to part of it, and were widely transplanted. Bluegill is a recent illegal introduction confined mostly to the Sheepscot drainage in mid-coast Maine. The Midwestern sim set therefore has no faithful counterpart for bluegill, carp, northern pike, or fathead minnow in an eastern Maine lake.

#### Chain pickerel: native range and spread
- The MDIFW pickerel assessment says chain pickerel "was apparently native to only the southwestern portion of Maine". Widespread transplants then spread it across much of the southern half of the state. — [MDIFW, *Chain Pickerel Assessment*, Brokaw 2001, updated Lucas 2008 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)
- **Early transfers:**
  - In the early 1800s pickerel were moved from the Penobscot to the St. Croix.
  - In 1817 or 1818 they were moved into Annabessacook Lake.
  - Such introductions were "routinely accomplished in large sections of southern, central, and eastern Maine during the nineteenth century"; northern Maine "apparently escaped such transfers."
  - Commercial pickerel fisheries ran in several Washington County lakes from the early 1900s into the 1950s. The plan says this is partly why pickerel are so widespread in Washington County today.

  — [MDIFW pickerel assessment [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)
- **Statewide distribution (Table 1 of the assessment):**
  - 781 waters, 514,205 acres; principal fishery in 586 waters, 331,654 acres.
  - Region C (Downeast): 151 lakes, 137,311 acres.
  - Region F (Penobscot): 107 lakes, 137,037 acres.
  - Regions E and G (north): fewer than 20 waters each; G has 12 lakes, 1,945 acres.
  - Region C's printed principal-fishery acreage (5,440 acres for 95 lakes) looks like a typo.

  — [MDIFW pickerel assessment [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)
- Pickerel ponds were sometimes chemically reclaimed for trout. Pickerel have since re-established populations in "a number of smaller ponds previously managed for brook trout." — [same [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)

#### White perch: native range and spread
- White perch's pre-settlement distribution in Maine "is not known". They were "quite likely" present in all major river drainages up to the first barrier to migration. Settlers then introduced them widely, and dams that flooded falls let them colonise new waters. They are absent from the St. John headwaters above Grand Falls and the upper Androscoggin. — [MDIFW, *White Perch Management Plan*, M. R. Smith, Region F, May 2002 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- MNAP describes white perch as natively coastal (estuaries, rivers, and lakes with coastal access) but "introduced to many inland lakes over the past 100+ years". — [MNAP ch. 3 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_3.pdf)
- **Distribution (White Perch Plan Table 2):**
  - 514 waters, 571,236 acres; principal fishery in 437 waters, 391,316 acres. The text elsewhere says 522 waters, 445 of them principal.
  - Region C: 103 waters, 108,706 acres. Region F: 94 waters, 150,834 acres. Region G: 7 waters, 1,630 acres.
  - All populations are wild; Maine has no white perch hatchery.

  — [MDIFW White Perch Plan [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)

#### Smallmouth and largemouth bass: introduction history
- Black bass first came to Maine in 1869. A Livingston Stone fry contract failed. Fish then came from three sources:
  - Newburgh, NY, into Duck Pond (Falmouth);
  - Connecticut, into ponds in Dedham and Newport;
  - Newburgh fish bought by G. S. Page, into Cochnewagon Pond and Cobbosseecontee Lake.

  The 1867 Fish Commission report warned bass "should be carefully excluded from all trout waters." — [Ledlie, *The Black Bass in Maine*, AMFF *American Fly Fisher* [primary]](https://amff.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Black_Bass_in_Maine_pp9-11.pdf)
- Smallmouth bass were introduced in 1868–1881 into lakes of the Saco, Androscoggin, Kennebec, Penobscot and **Saint Croix** drainages (Everhart 1966; Warner 2005). Their native range is the Great Lakes, Hudson Bay and upper Mississippi basins, so all of New England is outside it. Maine fish were later stocked into New Brunswick and Nova Scotia (1905–1953). — [Smithsonian NEMESIS, *Micropterus dolomieu* [primary]](https://invasions.si.edu/nemesis/species_summary/550562)
- Kendall Warner (2005) counted 51 southern and central Maine waters that received authorised bass introductions between 1868 and 1881. — [Central Maine newspaper, citing Warner 2005 [search summary]](https://www.centralmaine.com/?p=1800598)
- MDIFW says smallmouth bass are "now found throughout southern, central, and eastern Maine". Illegal introductions into northern waters threaten native species. In the north MDIFW does not manage bass and allows unlimited harvest. — [MDIFW smallmouth bass page [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/fisheries/species-information/smallmouth-bass.html); [MDIFW Black Bass Technical Work Group, Nov 2019 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/BassTechnicalWorkGroupNov2019.pdf)
- The Bass Technical Work Group states that "Bass are not native to Maine" and that neither species is raised in IFW hatcheries. — [same [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/BassTechnicalWorkGroupNov2019.pdf)
- **Largemouth in the St. Croix:** in 2012 largemouth bass were confirmed in Grand Falls Flowage (St. Croix drainage, Washington County), an illegal introduction. MDIFW encouraged anglers to take largemouth of all sizes from Grand Falls Flowage, Lewy Lake, Long Lake, Big Lake and the St. Croix below Vanceboro. Officials said more than 18,000 acres of smallmouth fishery was at risk. — [Bangor Daily News, 29 Jun 2012 [primary]](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2012/06/29/news/difw-encourages-down-east-take-of-largemouth-bass/)

#### Bluegill (introduced, local)
- Bluegill are not native to Maine; the MDIFW blog calls them "highly invasive".
- **Where found:**
  - 2017: Savade Pond (62 acres, Windsor, headwaters of the West Branch Sheepscot). The net caught 30 bluegill, 8 black crappie and 2 pumpkinseed; the 2 pumpkinseed were the only native fish netted.
  - The following summer: abundant in Turner Mills Pond, Long Pond (Windsor) and Sheepscot Pond (Palermo). Sheepscot Pond had dozens of bluegill of all ages and no pumpkinseed found.
- Bluegill "greatly outnumber" pumpkinseed in that watershed and hybridise with them. The likely cause was an unpermitted stocking.

— [MDIFW blog, *An Undetected Fish Invasion*, W. Ashe, 19 May 2020 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/undetected-fish-invasion)

- **Native ("indigenous") status in the plan's Table 2:**
  - Yes: creek chub, lake chub, dace, fallfish, common shiner, blacknose shiner, white and longnose sucker, eel.
  - No: fathead minnow, emerald shiner, mummichog, and also golden shiner, banded killifish and bridled shiner.
  - The last three "No" entries conflict with MNAP, which counts both Maine Fundulidae as native. They also conflict with general knowledge that golden shiner is native to Maine (unverified here). Treat these three as uncertain; this may be a column error in the plan.
- **White sucker vs longnose sucker by county (Table 4):**
  - Washington County: 103 white-sucker lakes (124,113 acres), but only 1 longnose-sucker lake.
  - Aroostook: 125 white-sucker lakes (85,206 acres). Penobscot: 104 lakes (90,540 acres).
  - Longnose sucker is concentrated in Piscataquis (40 lakes, 168,749 acres).
- **Life history, white sucker:**
  - Matures at 5–8 yr; maximum age 15+.
  - Spawns in early spring in "gravel bottomed, fast flowing streams, may spawn on lake shores".
  - Often 4–5 lb in lakes.
  - A Unity College study found white suckers made up 96% of trap-net catch during the spring spawning run.
- **Life history, golden shiner:** matures at 2–3 yr at 3–4 in; maximum age 5; a few reportedly reach 12 in. It scatters adhesive eggs over submerged vegetation in late spring and summer. The plan calls it the "primary forage for bass, pickerel".
- **Life history, fallfish:** matures at 3–4 yr; usually 5–8 in, up to 16 in. Males build communal stone nests up to 6 ft across and 2 ft high. The plan says it "competes with young gamefish".
- **Life history, common shiner:** spawns "in running water over a clean, gravel bottom … when water temperatures rise into the low 60s" (≈16–17 °C).
- **Life history, banded killifish:** spawns "throughout the summer" over weeds; mature at 2–3 in.
- **Life history, yellow perch:** spawns "in early spring when water temperatures are in the mid 40s" (≈7 °C). Maine fish "seldom exceed 12 inches … adults probably average closer to 8 inches."

#### Salmonids and diadromous fish
- **Landlocked salmon:** native to only four basins — West Grand Lake (St. Croix), Green Lake (Union), Sebec Lake (Penobscot) and Sebago Lake (Presumpscot). Stocking has spread them since 1868. — [MDIFW blog, *How do IFW hatcheries help salmon spawn* [search summary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/how-do-ifw-hatcheries-help-salmon-spawn)
- **Brook trout:** MDIFW estimates almost 600 lakes hold wild brook trout (never stocked) and 28 lakes hold wild lake trout. Most are in western and northern Maine, with a few Downeast. — [MNAP ch. 3 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_3.pdf)
- **Diadromous species:** American eel is the only catadromous species. Alewife, blueback herring, shad, rainbow smelt, sea lamprey and Atlantic salmon are anadromous. Landlocked smelt, alewife and salmon populations exist. — [MNAP ch. 3 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_3.pdf)
- **Northern Maine:**
  - Brown trout, rainbow trout and splake are non-native.
  - Muskellunge entered from a Quebec introduction (late 1970s) down the St. John River.
  - Smallmouth bass are in the St. John below Madawaska and in parts of the Aroostook River, from illegal stockings.

  — [The County (Aroostook), *Non-native fish of northern Maine*, Apr 2019 [primary]](https://thecounty.me/?p=210276)

### Inferences
- **A faithful eastern-Maine shallow-lake set** maps onto the sim's 8 slots as follows. This is my mapping, not a published one.

  | Sim slot (current species) | Maine replacement | Basis |
  |---|---|---|
  | minnow (fathead) | **golden shiner** for a generic Maine lake (851 lakes; 71% of NE lakes), or **banded killifish + fallfish + blacknose dace** for Hot Brook specifically | Hot Brook species list |
  | sunfish (bluegill) | **pumpkinseed**, the native Lepomis (74% of NE lakes) | Pumpkinseed is not on the Hot Brook species sheet; see Q2 |
  | perch | **yellow perch** (keep) | — |
  | carp | **white perch**, as the dominant schooling planktivore/benthivore and the lake's main "too-many-small-fish" species | MDIFW plan |
  | catfish (brown bullhead) | **brown bullhead** (keep; native) | — |
  | (benthic omnivore) | **white sucker**, the detritivore/benthivore and the main stream spawner | No Maine source found for common carp in eastern Maine lakes |
  | bass (largemouth) | **smallmouth bass** (introduced; principal fishery) | — |
  | pike | **chain pickerel** | — |
  | trout | no lake counterpart in the Hot Brook lakes (no coldwater habitat) | Could become brook trout as a stream visitor from Baskahegan Stream, or be dropped |

- **For a statewide "typical Maine pond" instead of Hot Brook:** golden shiner and pumpkinseed are the most defensible forage-fish choices.
- **Native vs introduced in the Hot Brook lakes** (my reasoning from the sources above):
  - **Smallmouth bass:** certainly introduced, before the 1949 survey.
  - **Chain pickerel:** probably a 19th–20th-century introduction, since it is native only to southwestern Maine.
  - **White perch:** native status uncertain. The lakes sit far up the Penobscot–Mattawamkeag–Baskahegan system.
  - **Probably native:** yellow perch, brown bullhead, white sucker, fallfish, blacknose dace, banded killifish.

## Q2. Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake and Hot Brook: physical data, species, management

### Takeaway
The two lakes form a pair of large, shallow, unstratified, mud-bottomed warmwater lakes in spruce-fir forest. They drain north via Hot Brook to Baskahegan Stream, then the Mattawamkeag River and the Penobscot.

| | Upper | Lower |
|---|---|---|
| Area | ≈360–369 ha | ≈277–289 ha |
| Maximum depth | 5.5 m | 4.0 m |
| Mean depth | ≈3.3 m | ≈2.7 m |

MDIFW lists the same nine named species in both lakes:
- principal fisheries: smallmouth bass, white perch, chain pickerel;
- also yellow perch, brown bullhead, banded killifish, blacknose dace, fallfish, white sucker.

There is no coldwater fish habitat in the lakes or their tributaries. The lakes are managed for larger-than-average bass, and white perch have a special 25-fish bag limit. The stream between the lakes is only about 200 m (1/8 mile) long, rocky and hard-bottomed. No stream survey, temperature record, or documented fish use of it was found.

### Cited Findings

#### Upper Hot Brook Lake — MDIFW lake survey sheet (surveyed Aug 1949; revised 1965, 2004; project F-28-P) [primary] — [source](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf)
- **Location:** Danforth and T8 R4 NBPP, Washington County. The map also labels Bancroft Twp., Aroostook County. USGS quads Jimmey Mountain and Stetson Mountain.
- **Area:** 912 acres (≈369 ha). **Maximum depth:** 18 ft (5.5 m).
- **August temperatures:** surface 74 °F (23.3 °C). The sheet also prints "75 feet – 71 °F" (21.7 °C). The depth cannot be right (the lake is 18 ft deep), so it is probably a template error; read it as about 71 °F near the bottom.
- **Fishes:** smallmouth bass, white perch, yellow perch, chain pickerel, bullhead (hornpout), banded killifish. Under "Minnows": blacknose dace, fallfish, white sucker.
- **Principal fisheries:** smallmouth bass, white perch, chain pickerel.
- **Description:**
  - Mostly surrounded by spruce-fir forest, with wetlands on the south and west sides.
  - Shoreline mostly rocky with some sand, plus a large sandy beach in the northeast; "the lake bottom is mostly muddy."
  - Camp development along the south shore.
- **Character:** "a typical warm water lake with excellent spawning and adult habitat for bass, perch, and pickerel. The lake is shallow and does not stratify during summer months."
- **Tributaries:** "several small tributaries … none have any suitable habitat for coldwater fish." The outlet flows into Lower Hot Brook Lake and then into Baskahegan Stream.
- **Management:** "managed for a larger than average size bass fishery and has special regulations"; special 25-fish bag limit on white perch; open to fishing until Nov 30. Unimproved boat launch at the south end, off Route 169.
- **Bathymetric map (my tally of the printed soundings):**
  - 83 soundings with mean 10.7 ft (3.25 m).
  - 18% of soundings are 15 ft or deeper, all in the northern basin (to 18 ft).
  - 19% are 6 ft or shallower; 40% are 10 ft or shallower.
  - The southern basin is a broad 10–14 ft flat with two islands.
- **Lakes of Maine (MIDAS 1076):** 890 acres (360 ha), perimeter 9 mi (14.5 km), mean depth 11 ft (3.35 m), max 18 ft. Warmwater fishery; no known invasive aquatic plant. Lower Hot Brook is listed 2.07 mi to the north. — [Lakes of Maine, Upper Hot Brook Lake [search summary; page 403 to fetch]](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1076)

#### Lower Hot Brook Lake — MDIFW lake survey sheet (same dates) [primary] — [source](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)
- **Location:** Danforth and T8 R4 NBPP (Washington County), and Bancroft (Aroostook County).
- **Area:** 713 acres (≈289 ha). **Maximum depth:** 13 ft (4.0 m).
- **Temperatures:** printed identically to the upper lake (74 °F surface; "75 feet – 71 °F"), probably copied over.
- **Fish list and principal fisheries:** identical to the upper lake.
- **Description:** spruce-fir forest, with wetlands on the north and east sides. Shoreline mostly rocky with some sand and mud; "lake bottom is mostly muddy." "There are no camps on this lake."
- **Character:** shallow, does not stratify, "excellent spawning and adult habitat for bass, perch, and pickerel."
- **The connecting stream:** "The major tributary is the inlet from the upper lake and although this stream is rocky and hard bottomed it does not contain any suitable habitat for coldwater fish." The outlet flows into Baskahegan Stream.
- **Access:** only through the inlet stream from the upper lake. A boat cannot be motored through; "a boat operator must be prepared to paddle, pole, or walk a boat through parts of the waterway."
- **Management:** same as the upper lake (larger-than-average bass, 25-fish white perch limit, season to Nov 30).
- **Bathymetric map (my tally of the printed soundings):** 63 soundings with mean 8.9 ft (2.71 m). None reach 15 ft; 25% are 6 ft or shallower and 58% are 10 ft or shallower. The inlet is at the south end and the outlet at the northeast.
- **Lakes of Maine (MIDAS 1072):** 684 acres (277 ha), perimeter 6.7 mi (10.8 km), mean depth 9 ft (2.74 m), max 13 ft. — [Lakes of Maine, Lower Hot Brook Lake [search summary]](https://www.lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=1072)

#### Hot Brook (the stream)
- The lakes are joined by "a short (1/8 mile) non-navigable connecting stream" (≈200 m). — [MDIFW Upper and Lower survey sheets [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)
- The GNIS-type point for "Hot Brook" is at 45.68145°N, 67.93390°W, elevation 367 ft (112 m), on the USGS Jimmey Mountain 1:24k quad. Danforth is about 3.5 mi away and Irish Settlement about 2.0 mi. — [TopoQuest, Hot Brook [primary]](https://topoquest.com/place/maine/stream/hot-brook/568338)

### Inferences
- **Morphometry cross-check:** my sounding-transect means (10.7 ft upper, 8.9 ft lower) agree with Lakes of Maine's mean depths (11 ft, 9 ft).
- **Littoral share:** by the usual light-limited definition, effectively 100% of both lakes is littoral or potential macrophyte habitat if Secchi depth exceeds about 2 m. Lower has no point deeper than 4 m; Upper has only about 18% of soundings beyond 4.6 m (15 ft). This is a strong contrast with a stratified Midwestern kettle lake.
- **No hypolimnion:** there is no thermal refuge for coldwater fish and no summer-anoxic bottom layer to dodge. The whole water column reaches about 22–24 °C in August (from the 1949 sheet).
- **Hot Brook's layout:** the GNIS point (112 m elevation) is probably Hot Brook's mouth at Baskahegan Stream. Hot Brook would then be the outlet of Lower Hot Brook Lake flowing to Baskahegan Stream, plus the 200 m thoroughfare between the lakes. The East and West Branches are probably tributaries of the upper lake. This is not verified from a map.
- **Elevation drop:** the lakes are probably about 125–135 m elevation (inferred only from nearby land lots at 420–440 ft). That implies a modest drop of perhaps 10–20 m to the brook mouth; this is unsourced.
- **The connector as habitat:** as the outflow of a warm, unstratified lake, its summer temperature should track lake surface water (about 22–24 °C in August). MDIFW says it is not coldwater habitat. Its "rocky and hard bottomed" bed is the kind of habitat white sucker, fallfish and common shiner use for spawning (see Q4).
- **The 25-fish white perch limit:** the Hot Brook lakes are probably two of the "seven lakes in eastern Maine" in the 2002 plan. The species sheets were revised in 2004 and list this limit.
- **Sunfish:** **pumpkinseed is absent from the MDIFW Hot Brook species lists** (and so are golden shiner, common shiner and American eel). Either these species were not caught in the 1949, 1965 and 2004 surveys, or they are absent. The user's premise of sunfish congregating in Hot Brook pools in summer cannot be confirmed from MDIFW records. If the sim needs a Lepomis, it is an assumption for the Hot Brook lakes specifically, although pumpkinseed is plausible regionally.

- **Ice-in and ice-out dates, and winter dissolved oxygen** were not found. Winter oxygen matters because the lakes are shallow and mud-bottomed, which risks winterkill.
- **No recent MDIFW netting or electrofishing data** (catch rates, length frequencies, bass density) were found for either lake. The current wording of the bass special regulation was not found.
- **Hot Brook itself:** its length, gradient, pool/riffle structure, summer temperatures and any fish survey (MDIFW stream survey, culvert or fish-passage records) were not found.
- **Which MDIFW region the lakes are in** (C Downeast or F Penobscot) was not established.
- Native status of white perch and pickerel in this specific drainage is undetermined.
- No loon, eagle or osprey nesting records specific to these lakes were found.

## Q3. Typical abundances: standing stock, relative abundance, predator:prey ratios, pumpkinseed vs bluegill, perch stunting, pickerel and smallmouth densities

### Takeaway
No whole-lake biomass (kg/ha) estimate for any Hot Brook-type eastern Maine lake was found online. The best quantitative analogs are Michigan's compiled standing crops (Schneider 1973):
- whole-lake **10–360 lb/acre (11–404 kg/ha)**;
- "normal" lakes average **88 lb/acre (≈99 kg/ha)**;
- perch-only lakes **46 lb/acre (≈52 kg/ha)**;
- soft-water northern lakes often only **10–62 lb/acre (11–70 kg/ha)**.

In "normal" Michigan lakes, piscivores (bass plus pike/walleye) made up **≈11–21% of fish biomass**. For Maine:
- MDIFW describes white perch, yellow perch and pickerel as abundant to over-abundant and prone to stunting. Yellow perch adults average about 8 in (20 cm), and in stunted white perch populations a 6-year-old may be only 4 in (10 cm).
- Pumpkinseed, not bluegill, is the native and dominant Lepomis of northern New England.
- No per-hectare density was found for chain pickerel or Maine smallmouth bass. Northern Wisconsin smallmouth estimates are about 1–20 adults/ha.

DeRoche 1965, "Standing crops of fishes in three small lakes compared with C14 estimates of net primary productivity", *TAFS* 94:9–25. It covers three potential trout lakes treated with rotenone, by authors at the Maine Dept. of Inland Fisheries & Game. The scan was too large to open, so its numbers were not extracted. — [Montana State Behnke archive scan](https://arc.lib.montana.edu/robert-behnke/objects/2491-36-10.pdf)

#### Pumpkinseed vs bluegill in northern New England
- Pumpkinseed was the most widespread species in the 203-lake EMAP Northeast sample (74% of lakes). Bluegill was in 32% and was judged non-native in most of those lakes. — [Whittier et al. 1999 [abstract]](https://nysm.nysed.gov/bibcite/reference/21118)
- In Maine, bluegill is an illegal, recent introduction (Sheepscot drainage). Where established it "greatly outnumber[s]" pumpkinseed and hybridises with it; in the Savade Pond net the catch was 30 bluegill to 2 pumpkinseed. — [MDIFW blog 2020 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/undetected-fish-invasion)

#### Yellow perch: abundance and stunting in Maine
- Yellow perch occur in 818 lakes (736,745 acres). "Because of the relatively low fertility of Maine waters, short growing seasons, and inter-specific competition; yellow perch in Maine are commonly small in size … adults probably average closer to 8 inches." Maine "has spent more time and effort at eliminating yellow perch populations than … managing them." — [MDIFW Non-Sport plan 2002 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)

#### White perch: dominance and stunting
- "Where conditions are favorable, white perch form very large populations that occasionally dominate the waters they inhabit … Usually the problem is one of too many small perch."
- Stunting can be "so extreme that a 6-year-old perch may be only 4 inches long". Depleted food "also affects the growth of … salmon, trout, and bass."
- Fecundity is 20,000–300,000 eggs per female (about 22,000 at 6 in). Life span is 5–7 yr.
- MDIFW estimated 50% of desirable-size perch could be harvested without harm, but "standing crop cannot be estimated at this time because of inadequate data." Statewide harvest was 964,889 fish per year.

— [MDIFW White Perch Plan 2002 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)

#### Chain pickerel: density proxies, growth, diet
Source for this subsection unless noted: [MDIFW Chain Pickerel Assessment [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf).

- "The species is generally able to maintain moderate to high density populations wherever it occurs. As long as a lake contains relatively shallow, weedy habitat, the pickerel is likely to do well." Pickerel are least abundant in oligotrophic lakes with scarce weedy shallows.
- **Harvest benchmark (1985 plan):** about **0.35 lb/acre/yr (≈0.39 kg/ha/yr)** harvested; mean angled fish 16.2 in and 1.1 lb.
- **Winter catch rates by lake type:**
  - Warmwater-only "Type A" lakes: 1.0–2.0 pickerel per angler.
  - Mixed lakes: 0.15–0.90.
  - Salmonid lakes: 0.02–0.10.
- **Life history:**
  - Matures at age 3–4 and 13–16 in (33–41 cm).
  - Female:male ratio about 1.3:1.
  - 10,000–12,000 eggs per lb of female.
  - Spawns "shortly after ice-out" in flooded vegetation; eggs hatch in 1–2 weeks.
  - Young of the year reach 4–5 in (10–13 cm) by Sept–Oct.
  - Diet turns strongly piscivorous after 7–9 in (18–23 cm).
  - Grows 2.5–3.5 in per year.
- **Diet:** yellow perch, white perch, sunfish, minnows and smelt, plus crayfish, frogs and dragonfly nymphs.
- **Predation on salmonids:**
  - Brandy Pond (1989): 15 of 54 pickerel (28%) had eaten newly stocked brown trout; the 54 averaged 16.9 in and 1.1 lb.
  - Beddington Lake: 21% of pickerel over 10 in contained Atlantic salmon smolts.
  - Late 1960s: pickerel were "the most common predator on newly stocked salmon".
- **Interaction with largemouth bass:** pickerel have declined in small waters where largemouth became established, because largemouth prey on young-of-year pickerel in weed patches.
- **Introduced pickerel in Nova Scotia** (15 lakes, mean area 15.8 ha, mean depth 2.7 m):
  - Total fish CPUE was **0.047 fish/trap-hour in pickerel lakes vs 1.04 in pickerel-free lakes**, about two orders of magnitude lower.
  - Pickerel lakes had simplified communities and truncated size distributions.
  - Pickerel stomachs held mostly insects (58.8%) and few fish.
  - Pickerel were introduced there in 1945 and are now in 95 locations.
  - — [Mitchell, LeBlanc & Heggelin, *Impact of introduced chain pickerel on lake fish communities in Nova Scotia* [primary]](https://www.ap.smu.ca/~lcampbel/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf)

#### Smallmouth bass density (no Maine figure found; northern analogs)
- Mildred Lake, Wisconsin: 1,569 ± 420 adults over 8 in, which is **8.2/acre (≈20/ha)**. — [Wisconsin DNR, Mildred Lake 2005 [search summary]](https://apps.dnr.wi.gov/doclink/lakes_fish-plant_reports/oneidamildredlake2005.pdf)

- **Maine qualitative evidence:**
  - Bass in Maine are "prolific so populations can be crowded"; Maine bass grow slowly, and a 20-in bass may be about 20 years old. — [MDIFW smallmouth page [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/fisheries/species-information/smallmouth-bass.html)

- **Hot Brook total fish biomass:** a working target of about **60–150 kg/ha** is defensible, but it is not measured.

## Q4. Seasonal movements between lakes and connecting streams

### Takeaway
Spring sequence in Maine, by trigger:
- **Ice-out:** chain pickerel spawn first, in flooded marsh vegetation.
- **Mid-40s °F (≈7 °C):** yellow perch spawn (ribbons over vegetation and woody debris).
- **About 10 °C, with high flow:** white suckers run up gravelly, fast inlets and outlets, spawning at night. Maine sucker trap-net runs are 96% white sucker.
- **59–60 °F (15 °C):** white perch spawn in shallows; some move into streams.
- **Low 60s °F (16–17 °C):** common shiner and fallfish spawn on stream gravel.
- **Summer:** banded killifish spawn all summer in weedy shallows.

In summer, brook trout leave warm main channels for cold seeps and tributary mouths, where predators gather. Pumpkinseed are fairly site-faithful, but a European study tracked them moving up to 2.5 km from a lake down an outflow stream. No source documents sunfish using warm stream pools specifically, or any movement in Hot Brook itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Chain pickerel:** "Spawning occurs shortly after ice-out when adults move in to swampy areas containing flooded vegetation." No nest; eggs broadcast; hatch in 1–2 weeks. — [MDIFW Pickerel Assessment [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf)
- **Yellow perch:** spawn "in early spring when water temperatures are in the mid 40s", laying gelatinous ribbons over vegetation or submerged branches. — [MDIFW Non-Sport plan [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- **White and longnose suckers:**
  - Suckers "run in streams during the early spring to spawn in the gravelly areas of shallow, swift-flowing streams. Generally, spawning takes place at night."
  - Longnose suckers run "usually earlier in the spring than white suckers."
  - White suckers "may spawn on lake shores."
  - Commercial sucker harvest (lobster bait, especially gravid females) is concentrated on the spring run. 96% of trap-net catch in the run was white sucker (Unity College study).
  - — [MDIFW Non-Sport plan [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- **White sucker trigger (non-Maine sources):**
  - Runs begin at about 10 °C (Ontario).
  - Run strength depends on stream discharge (Barton 1980).
  - Spawning happens at 57–68 °F (14–20 °C) after the run starts (Iowa).
  - Spawning sites are "typically small streams or the inlets and outlets of lakes" (USFS).
  - — [Upper Thames River Conservation Authority](https://thamesriver.on.ca/spawning-white-suckers-in-medway-creek-a-springtime-spectacle/); [Iowa DNR white sucker](https://programs.iowadnr.gov/lakemanagement/fishiowa/fishdetails/WHS); [USFS white sucker account [search summaries]](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/boise/AWAE/projects/fish_cattle/White%20sucker.pdf)
- **White perch:**
  - "The white perch spawns mainly in the spring at water temperatures around 59-60 degrees F … in shallow water over almost any bottom type"; eggs hatch in 4–5 days.
  - "Several streams provide perch fishing although the perch may not be resident populations, but rather migrants from nearby lakes and ponds."
  - — [MDIFW White Perch Plan [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- **Minnows:**
  - "All are spring or summer spawners, and spawning migrations are limited to short movements upstream or beyond the shoals of a lake."
  - Common shiner spawns in running water over gravel in the low 60s °F.
  - Fallfish build stone nests.
  - Blacknose and longnose dace spawn on gravel in fast water.
  - Banded killifish spawn all summer among weeds.
  - Golden shiner spawns over submerged vegetation in quiet water.
  - — [MDIFW Non-Sport plan [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- **Smallmouth bass:** "They spawn in May, in four to ten feet depth of water, excavating shallow basins, where they remain protecting their spawn and young" (1867 Massachusetts account in the Maine Fish Commission report). — [Ledlie, *Black Bass in Maine* [primary]](https://amff.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Black_Bass_in_Maine_pp9-11.pdf)
  - Spring and summer adults use rocky shallows and drop-offs in 5–15 ft. — [MDIFW smallmouth page [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/fisheries/species-information/smallmouth-bass.html)
  - MDIFW sets bass weigh-in tournament closures by county "during nesting period". — [MDIFW Bass TWG [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/BassTechnicalWorkGroupNov2019.pdf)
- **Brook trout thermal refuges (Maine):**
  - In heat, brook trout move to "deeper pools that are cooler and better oxygenated" and to "small, colder tributaries".
  - One example: a trout in a "cool 50-degree streamside seep" (10 °C) next to a main stream "in the mid 70's" (≈23–24 °C), "approaching the fish's thermal limits." — [MDIFW blog, *Fish don't enjoy heatwaves either* [search summary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/fish-dont-enjoy-heatwaves-either)
  - Refuges "often congregate fish in high numbers … near spring holes or tributaries," and attract predators. Underwater video at a central-Maine confluence showed trout leaving when a snapping turtle arrived and returning after it left. — [MDIFW blog, *Brook Trout Beat the Heat*, 23 Jul 2015 [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/brook-trout-beat-heat)
  - **Warming projections:** in-stream temperatures in a Kezar Lake (Maine) tributary are projected to rise 1.0–2.2 °C. Western Maine forest-harvest studies observed stream warming of 1.0–4.4 °C. — [AFS 2015 abstract [search summary]](https://afs.confex.com/afs/2015/webprogram/Paper20627.html)
  - **Seep physics:** groundwater seeps keep brook trout habitat stable. Shallow flow paths (about 5 m or less below the surface) are the most warming-sensitive. — [Briggs et al. 2018, HESS 22:6383 [search summary]](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/22/6383/2018/)
- **Pumpkinseed movement:**
  - **Poland, Emerald Lake (PIT tags):** 359 fish tagged and 43 recaptured. 39% of recaptures were at the previous location. Mean displacement was 43 m (maximum 156 m). Relocations were 49% in autumn, 36% in summer and 15% in spring. Fish preferred shallow reed and *Typha* shelves. — [Zięba et al. 2015, XV European Congress of Ichthyology abstract [primary]](https://www.frontiersin.org/10.3389/conf.fmars.2015.03.00123/2930/XV_European_Congress_of_Ichthyology/all_events/event_abstract)
  - **European lake-to-stream tagging project:** pumpkinseed moved from lakes into connected streams, with outlet type affecting movement. The farthest movement was 2.5 km downstream at about 30 m/day. Fish aged 3+ moved farthest, with the longest trips in spring. — [EU CORDIS project 501750 report [search summary; attribution uncertain]](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/501750/reporting/it)
  - **Detroit River:** pumpkinseed presence was positively correlated with water temperature; bluegill presence was not. — [Univ. Windsor, sympatric sunfish in a large river [search summary]](https://scholar.uwindsor.ca/glierpub/325)

### Inferences
- **Hot Brook spring calendar.** Hot Brook (the rocky 200 m thoroughfare and the outlet to Baskahegan Stream) is the obvious spring spawning-run corridor. A plausible sequence:

  | Timing | Who moves | Where |
  |---|---|---|
  | Late April–May, about 10 °C, high flow, at night | White suckers | into Hot Brook gravel |
  | At ice-out | Chain pickerel | into flooded wetland margins (lake edges, not the brook) |
  | Early spring, about 7 °C | Yellow perch | spawn on brush and vegetation |
  | Late May–June, about 15 °C | White perch | shallows, possibly the brook |
  | June, about 16–18 °C | Fallfish and common shiner | brook gravel nests |
  | May–June | Smallmouth bass | nest in the lakes on the rocky/sandy shoreline at 1–3 m |

  Banded killifish and minnows spawn through summer in weedy lake shallows.
- **No cold refuge for trout.** Both lakes are warm and unstratified, and MDIFW says neither the lakes nor their tributaries have coldwater habitat. The brook therefore offers no summer cold refuge, and **brook trout should not be modelled in the Hot Brook lakes**. At most, they could be occasional stream visitors from Baskahegan Stream's seasonal trout fishery.
- **Late-summer and fall movement.** If pumpkinseed are present, a late-summer and fall dispersal pulse, partly out of the lake into the outflow, is consistent with the European data (autumn relocation peaks, downstream movement of up to 2.5 km). It is not documented for Maine.

### Gaps
- No Maine-specific run timing (calendar dates) for white sucker, yellow perch or white perch was found. No data were found for any stream in the Baskahegan/Mattawamkeag system.
- No source documents sunfish or pumpkinseed congregating in warm stream pools in summer, in Maine or elsewhere.
- No fall or winter lake–stream movement data were found for Maine warmwater species.
- Ice-out dates for the Danforth area were not found. They would set the spring trigger in the sim.

## Q5. Fish-eating birds and mammals on Maine lakes, and their impact

### Takeaway
The main fish predators on Maine lakes are:
- **Common loon:** about 3,174 adults and 568 chicks in southern Maine in 2025, and rising.
- **Osprey and bald eagle.**
- **Great blue heron, common merganser and belted kingfisher.**
- **River otter and mink.**

Order-of-magnitude intakes:
- A loon family (2 adults and 2 chicks) eats roughly 360–900 kg of fish over a ~120-day breeding season, mostly 10–70 g perch and sunfish taken in water under 5 m deep.
- An osprey nest receives about 5 fish per day averaging about 157 g (≈0.85 kg/day).
- Mergansers and herons eat roughly 0.45–0.7 kg of fish per day.
- Bald eagles feed about 90% on fish, mostly bullheads and suckers.

Otter diets shift from crayfish and other prey in summer to mostly fish in winter. Fish-eating birds and mammals concentrate at thermal refuges and dense prey patches.

### Cited Findings
- **Loon numbers (Maine Audubon annual count):**
  - The southern-Maine estimate for 2025 (south of 45°N) is about **3,174 adult loons and 568 chicks**. That is more than double the 1983 count (1,417 adults, 176 chicks), and adults are up 13% in 10 years.
  - Northern lakes, which are not part of the estimate, had 545 adults and 42 chicks on 121 surveyed lakes in 2025.
  - The count is taken on the third Saturday in July, 7:00–7:30 am.
  - — [WBUR / AP, 13 Dec 2025 [search summary]](https://www.wbur.org/news/2025/12/13/more-loons-are-filling-maines-lakes-with-their-ghost-like-calls/); [Bangor Daily News 2011 (chicks 150–400/yr historically) [search summary]](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2011/07/13/news/wet-spring-expected-to-contribute-to-low-loon-count/)
- **Loon energetics (Journey North):**
  - An adult weighs about 5,000 g. Near-grown birds eat about 22% of body weight per day; small chicks about 40%.
  - Over a 120-day breeding season a family eats **≈360 kg (792 lb)**. An oft-quoted alternative estimate is about 2,000 lb (≈900 kg).
  - Typical prey is 10–70 g, occasionally up to 300 g. A 10 g bass is about 4 in long; a 70 g bass about 7 in.
  - — [Journey North, *Loon food chain* [primary]](https://archive.journeynorth.org/tm/loon/FoodChain.html)
- **Loon diet:**
  - Breeding-season prey in order of importance: yellow perch, pumpkinseed, bluegill, salmonids, suckers.
  - Loons favour perch and sunfish because of their "erratic swimming behavior", and feed mostly in water under 5 m.
  - They switch to crayfish, snails, leeches and insects when fish are scarce or the water is turbid. Chicks eat small fish, insects and crayfish.
  - — [Wyoming Game & Fish loon literature summary [search summary]](https://wgfd.wyo.gov/media/1355/download); [Cornell All About Birds [search summary]](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/Common_Loon/lifehistory)
  - Caveat: the search summary merged several pages, so which of these two pages carries the prey ranking and the 5 m feeding depth is uncertain. The prey list cites Barr (1973) among its sources.
- **Osprey:**
  - Chesapeake Bay nests averaged 5.4 fish/day, 4–43 cm long, mean 156.9 g. That multiplies to about 850 g/day per nest (my arithmetic).
  - Finnish nests: 5.2 ± 1.0 fish/day.
  - Glass & Watts (2009): about 1,250 g/day per breeding household (male's share 400 g); 300–350 g/day in winter.
  - — [J. Raptor Res. 25(4):109–112 [search summary]](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v025n04/p00109-p00112.pdf); [Ornis Fennica [search summary]](https://ornisfennica.journal.fi/article/download/132953/81499); [Inland Bays osprey presentation citing Glass & Watts 2009 [search summary]](https://inlandbays.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Osprey-Reproduction-Population-Diet-and-Stressors.pptx.pdf)
- **Bald eagle:**
  - Bald eagles feed about 90% on fish, most commonly bullheads and suckers. Pickerel reach up to 20% of the diet where present (Dunstan & Harper 1975; Todd et al. 1982). — cited in [Mitchell et al., Nova Scotia pickerel paper [primary]](https://www.ap.smu.ca/~lcampbel/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf)
  - The Maine-specific study is Todd, Young, Owen & Gramlich 1982, "Food habits of Bald Eagles in Maine," *J. Wildl. Manage.* 46:636–645. Its tables were not obtained. — [Avian Diet Database citation](https://speciesconnect.com/?p=272544)
- **Common merganser:** about **454 g/day** per bird in winter (Oklahoma reservoir); gizzard shad were 84% of food by weight there. — [Miller 1973, Proc. SE Assoc. Game Fish Comm. [search summary]](https://seafwa.org/journal/1973/predation-warm-water-reservoirs-wintering-common-mergansers)
- **Great blue heron:** about 1.5 lb/day (≈680 g) on streams and 1.0 lb/day (≈454 g) on lakes, as trout-only intake. Wintering mergansers in the same study ate about 0.9 lb/day (≈410 g) of trout. — [Alexander 1976, Mich. DNR Fish. Res. Rep. 1839 [search summary]](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1839.pdf)
- **River otter:**
  - In the Missouri Ozarks, fish were in only 14% of summer scats but 84–88% of winter scats. Prey included sunfish, suckers, minnows and bass. — [Missouri Conservationist, *Otter Appetites* [search summary]](https://mdc.mo.gov/magazines/conservationist/2005-06/otter-appetites)
  - Minnow communities are thought to "buffer otter predation from more socially valued sportfish" (Lagler & Ostenson 1942; Ryder 1955). — cited in [Mitchell et al. [primary]](https://www.ap.smu.ca/~lcampbel/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf)
- **Predators at refuges:** "Fish grouped in large numbers often attract predators" such as birds, turtles and people. A snapping turtle displaced brook trout from a refuge in central Maine. — [MDIFW, *Brook Trout Beat the Heat* [primary]](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/brook-trout-beat-heat)
- **Rotenone recovery:** poisoning studies note that crayfish, turtles, birds and predatory fish can remove large numbers of dying or dead fish. — [Schneider 1973 [primary]](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1794.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Loon pressure on Upper Hot Brook (≈365 ha).** One breeding pair with 2 chicks eating 360–900 kg per season removes **≈1–2.5 kg/ha/yr** of 10–70 g perch, white perch and sunfish. That is roughly 1–4% of a 60–150 kg/ha fish stock, concentrated on small prey-sized fish. Two pairs would double it. Loon territory density on these lakes is unknown.
- **Osprey pressure.** One osprey nest at about 0.85 kg/day over a roughly 100-day nesting season removes about 85 kg, which is ≈0.2 kg/ha/yr on a 365 ha lake. Ospreys select larger fish (up to about 0.5 kg; suckers, bullheads, perch).
- **Effect on the sim's existing loon.** The sim's single diving loon (`BIRD` config) should take mostly 10–70 g perch and sunfish in water under 5 m. In Hot Brook-type lakes that is essentially the whole lake.
- **Winter otter.** Otter predation should rise in winter, when fish dominate the diet.
- **Eagle prey choice.** Eagles should key on bullheads and suckers, fitting the white sucker and brown bullhead slots.

### Gaps
- No Maine-specific osprey, heron, merganser, kingfisher, otter or mink diet percentages or consumption rates were obtained.
- No loon or osprey territory density for eastern Maine lakes was found, and no records for the Hot Brook lakes.
- No published estimate was found of the fraction of lake fish production removed by birds in Maine.
- The web-search budget ran out before mink, kingfisher and cormorant searches. These are the next leads, along with Todd et al. 1982 (Maine eagles) and Barr 1996 (loon feeding biology).
