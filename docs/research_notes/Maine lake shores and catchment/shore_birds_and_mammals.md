# Shore predators of Maine lakes, ponds and small streams: birds and mammals that eat fish, frogs, crayfish or turtles

Scope: quantitative behaviour, intake and presence values for the fish-eating birds and mammals of a small Maine lake, a 4 m pond and a rocky stream with ~1 m pools (Hot Brook, Danforth/Weston). Region flags: **[ME]** Maine, **[NE]** other New England, **[NA-x]** elsewhere in North America (state given), **[EU]** Europe. "Popular" marks non-peer-reviewed sources (agency leaflets are not marked popular). Unit conversions and arithmetic in the Inferences are mine.

Research method note: several key numbers come from the USFWS Habitat Suitability Index (HSI) model reports (Short & Cooper 1985 heron; Prose 1985 kingfisher; Allen 1984 mink; Allen 1982 beaver), which are reviews of the older primary literature. I read their OCR text directly; the primary studies they cite are named in each finding.

## 1. Common loon (Gavia immer): calendar, territory, nesting, takeoff, diving, prey and food intake

### Takeoff and calendar summary
In Maine, loons arrive right after ice-out (April) and nest on the shoreline (May–June). Eggs hatch after ~27 days, and chicks are raised July–August, fly by Sept–Oct and leave for the coast by Nov. A pair holds ~40–72 ha of water. On the Belgrade Lakes this works out to about 1 pair per 95–280 ha. A pair raising two chicks removes roughly 423 kg of food in ~5.5 months, about 2.5 kg/day per family. Dives usually last 40–60 s. Takeoff needs a ~30–180 m run.

### Cited Findings
**Calendar [ME]**
- April: loons "return to Maine's lakes right after ice out" and spend about a month establishing territories and pair bonds. May–June: nests of dead vegetation and mud "right on the shoreline", often on small islands with fewer predators. July–August: eggs hatch after about 27 days, and the family moves to a nearby "nursery" area. Sept–Oct: most chicks feed themselves and can fly; parents leave to gather in large "rafts", and juveniles raft later in the fall. Nov–March: on the coast. Juveniles wait "about seven years" before returning to fresh water to breed. — [Maine IF&W / Maine Audubon, *Living in Loon Territory*](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/livingwithloons.pdf)
- The same leaflet names skunks and raccoons as loon (nest) predators and says boat wakes flood shoreline nests and swamp small chicks [ME]. — [Maine IF&W](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/livingwithloons.pdf)

**Territory size and density**
- [ME] A breeding territory averages about 100 acres (~40 ha) and is larger or smaller depending on food, competition and habitat quality. — [Maine Audubon monitoring instructions 2024](https://maineaudubon.org/wp-content/uploads/1_Monitoring-Instructions_2024.pdf)
- [NA-WI] Territories averaged about 72 ha, and food supply may limit territory size. — [Barr 1996, *Hydrobiologia* 321:119–144 (abstract record)](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Eau/explor/LotaV3/Site/fr/Main/Exploration/bibRecord.php?hk=001142)
- [ME] Belgrade Lakes, 2019: 23 territorial pairs, 14 nested, 9 nests succeeded, 15 chicks hatched (the summary says 13), and 8 survived past 6 weeks. That is 0.35 fledged young per territorial pair, a nesting frequency of 0.61 and chick survival of 0.53. Great Pond had 12 territorial pairs (7 nested, 4 chicks hatched, 2 fledged; 0.17/pair). Long Pond had 11 pairs (7 nested, 11 hatched, 6 fledged; 0.55/pair). Raft nests 2/2 succeeded vs natural nests 7/13 (54%). Recorded failure causes include "mammalian predation". — [Belgrade Lakes Assoc. 2019 Common Loon summary](https://www.belgradelakesassociation.org/Portals/belgrade-lakes/2019%20-%20BLA%20COLO%20Summary%20Report.pdf)
- [ME] Natural-nest success in later Belgrade years: 2/9 (22%) in 2020, 9/18 (50%) in 2023, 6/15 (40%) in 2024. Rafts did 2/3 in 2024. — [BLA 2020](https://www.belgradelakesassociation.org/Portals/belgrade-lakes/PDFs/General/2020%20-%20BLA%20COLO%20Summary%20Report.pdf); [BLA 2023](https://www.belgradelakesassociation.org/Portals/belgrade-lakes/2023%20-%20BLA%20COLO%20Summary%20Report_docx.pdf); [BLA 2024](https://www.belgradelakesassociation.org/Portals/belgrade-lakes/Files/2024%20-%20BLA%20COLO%20Summary%20Report.pdf)
- [ME] Lake areas: Great Pond is 8,239 acres (IF&W survey; LakesOfMaine gives 8,533). Long Pond is 2,557 acres (LakesOfMaine and the loon-count record; the IF&W survey map gives 2,714) with ~31 miles of shoreline. — [IF&W Great Pond map](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/kennebec/great_pond.pdf); [LakesOfMaine Long Pond](https://lakesofmaine.org/lake-overview.html?m=5272); [IF&W Long Pond map](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/kennebec/long_pond_belgrade.pdf)
- [ME] Statewide (southern-half count): 3,147 adults and 420 chicks estimated in 2024 from >1,600 volunteers on 407 lakes, about double the first count in 1983. 2025: 3,174 adults and 568 chicks. — [WABI 2024](https://www.wabi.tv/2024/12/06/maines-loon-population-soars-2023); [AP via Boston Globe 2025](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/12/13/metro/more-loons-are-filling-maines-lakes-with-their-ghost-like-calls/)

**Nesting**
- [NA, 1973 Wilson Bull.] In 26 nests, rim-to-water distance was 0 ft for 15 nests, 0.1–1.0 ft for 6, 1.1–2.0 ft for 2, 2.1–3.0 ft for 1 and 3.1–4.0 ft for 2. None was more than ~1.2 m from water. 4 of 26 sat on muskeg (bog mat); the rest were on sand, clay or boulders. — [Wilson Bulletin 85(4):429–435](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v085n04/p0429-p0435.pdf)
- Incubation is 26–31 days and fledging (flight) 11–13 weeks [NA-NY] — [NY DEC](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/animals-fish-plants/common-loon). Incubation averages 28 days (27–30) [NA-SK] — [Blue Jay](https://bluejayjournal.ca/index.php/bluejay/article/download/5758/5747/5796). Chicks are independent feeders at ~8 weeks but cannot fly until 11–12 weeks — [McIntyre, J. Field Ornithol. 54(3)](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jfo/v054n03/p0247-p0253.pdf)
- [NA-WI] Chicks learn to feed over their first 8 weeks, and juveniles catch all their own food by ~11 weeks. — [Barr 1996](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Eau/explor/LotaV3/Site/fr/Main/Exploration/bibRecord.php?hk=001142)
- Egg predators listed: corvids, herring gulls, raccoons, striped skunks, fishers and mink. A nest camera at Lake Umbagog [ME/NH border] recorded an immature bald eagle eating a loon egg, a first in the peer-reviewed literature. Bald eagles also take adult loons and chicks. — [science.gov / worldwidescience topic aggregator (abstracts)](https://worldwidescience.org/topicpages/c/capturing+common+loons.html); [Concord Monitor "Granite Geek" 2019 [NE-NH], popular](https://granitegeek.concordmonitor.com/2019/11/22/bald-eagles-attack-loons-but-thats-not-why-loons-are-struggling/)
- [NE-NH] A record 42 nests flooded in one recent New Hampshire year, and an unusual number of Maine loons skipped nesting. Heavy rain is now a larger nest threat than predators in some years. — [Audubon](https://audubon.org/news/heavy-downpours-are-growing-threat-common-loons)

**Takeoff**
- [NE-NH] A takeoff run is "usually anywhere between 200 and 600 feet" (~60–180 m). Stronger wind shortens it. The often-quoted "quarter mile" is a conflation with the lake length needed. — [Loon Preservation Committee](https://loon.org/looncam-blogs/loon-cam-2019/getting-airborne-why-loons-dont-visit-your-birdbath/)
- [NA-MN] Minnesota DNR says 100–600 ft (~30–180 m). — [AOL/news report of MN DNR, popular](https://www.aol.com/articles/iconic-minnesota-bird-needs-football-123200970.html)

**Diving**
- One loon averaged ~52 s submerged (range 26–61 s). Palmer's handbook review of 40 dives gives 8.5–60 s. — [Auk 84(1):123 (1967 note)](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v084n01/p0123-p0123.pdf)
- [NA-SC, winter, reservoir] Solitary loons averaged 71.3 ± 7.7 s per dive (n = 46, range 15–219 s). Social birds made shorter, shallower dives on baitfish. — [Waterbirds 29(2):169 (2006)](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/waterbirds/volume-29/issue-2/1524-4695(2006)29[169:WCADBI]2.0.CO;2/Water-Clarity-and-Diving-Behavior-in-Wintering-Common-Loons/10.1675/1524-4695(2006)29[169:WCADBI]2.0.CO;2.full)
- [NA, winter, marine] Geolocator loons from MN/WI/MI breeding lakes dived as deep as 50 m (Gulf of Mexico), mostly on benthic prey. Dive duration, bottom time and post-dive surface interval all increased with depth. — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70255634)
- Most dives are shallow and under a minute; deep dives can last up to ~5 min. — [Florida FWC](https://myfwc.com/wildlifehabitats/profiles/birds/waterbirds/common-loon/)

**Prey and intake**
- [NA-WI] Loons are facultative predators: they prefer streamlined, erratically swimming fish but mostly take whatever is easiest to catch, "often yellow perch". A pair rearing two chicks could remove **~423 kg of food over ~5.5 months** on territory. Loons do not appear to harm sport fisheries. — [Barr 1996](https://wicri-demo.istex.fr/Wicri/Eau/explor/LotaV3/Site/fr/Main/Exploration/bibRecord.php?hk=001142)
- [NA-WI] Chick daily energy expenditure (doubly labelled water): at 10 d, 425 g chick and 686 kJ/d; at 21 d, 1,052 g and 768 kJ/d; at 35 d, 1,963 g and 1,935 kJ/d. Mass gain was 51, 54 and 33 g/d. **Fish intake was 162–383 g wet mass per chick per day** depending on age. — [Fournier et al. 2002, Auk 119:1121 (USGS)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70024245)
- [NA-AB] Chicks on fishless lakes relied on leeches and other invertebrates. On a lake with minnows, >70% of chick prey was fish. Adults spent >90% of their time on the nesting lake and >50% foraging. — [Hydrobiologia 2007 (Alberta)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-006-0059-6)
- Typical fish are ~10–70 g each, up to ~300 g. Near-grown loons eat ~22% of body mass per day and small chicks ~40%. A pair plus chicks eats ~360 kg over a 120-day season by Journey North's own calculation. — [Journey North (popular, educational)](https://archive.journeynorth.org/tm/loon/FoodChain.html)

### Inferences
- **Density for the sim:** Belgrade gives ~1 pair per 278 ha on Great Pond (3,334 ha) and ~1 per 94–100 ha on Long Pond (1,035–1,098 ha, a long, convoluted shore). Pairs scale with shoreline coves and nesting sites, not open area. A Maine lake of "a few hundred ha" therefore holds ~1–3 pairs. The sim's 2-D slice is one territory, so `BIRD.MAX = 1` (one bird or pair) is realistic.
- **Per-bird intake:** Barr's 423 kg over ~167 days is ~2.5 kg/day per family. Subtracting two chicks at ~0.16–0.38 kg/day for ~12 weeks (~40–65 kg total) leaves ~1.1 kg/day per adult, ≈20–25% of a ~4–5 kg adult. That matches the ~22% figure. Expect each adult to take ~15–100 fish/day at 10–70 g each, plus ~5–15 fish/day per chick delivered by the parents.
- **Calendar check:** the sim's year-fraction 0 = Mar 1, so `BIRD.SEASON [0.14, 0.76]` runs ~Apr 21 – ~Nov 15. That matches ice-out ~Apr 23 and loons leaving before ice-on (~Dec 1). Chicks should appear ~late June to mid-July (ice-out + ~1 month + 27 d incubation) and fly ~11–12 weeks later (late Sept).
- **Nest placement:** 0–1.2 m from the water's edge, on islands, bog mats or hummocks. A nest needs a shoreline cell at the waterline and is lost to water-level rises above ~0.3 m.
- **Dive timing:** real mean dives last ~40–60 s, and post-dive surface rests lengthen with depth. Prey comes from <10 m in breeding lakes, but no breeding-lake depth study was found. The sim's 9–18 s dives are compressed; the ratio of dive time to surface time is what should be preserved.

### Gaps
- No measured adult daily intake (g/day) for wild breeding loons was found; the ~1.1 kg/day above is derived.
- No breeding-lake dive-depth distribution was found; all depth data are from wintering birds.
- No surface-interval (rest between dives) values for breeding loons.
- The Barr 1996 full text (prey-size distribution and species percentages) was not accessible; only the abstract.
- I could not open the BRI (Biodiversity Research Institute) Maine loon reports.

## 2. Great blue heron (Ardea herodias): depth, time of day, prey, intake, Maine presence

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Herons wade in water ≤50 cm deep (often 15–25 cm) and take fish ≤25 cm, mostly ≤20 cm, plus frogs and crayfish. A heron needs ~300 g of fish per day. They forage most at dawn and dusk and are absent from inland Maine in winter. They return in late March to early April and breed May–August.

### Cited Findings
- Herons catch fish "in shallow water, usually ≤50 cm deep" (Bent 1926; Meyerriecks 1960; Bayer 1978). Their usual foraging succeeds only in shallow (≤0.5 m), clear water with a firm substrate and small fish (≤25 cm). Fish up to ~20 cm dominated the diet at SW Lake Erie (Hoffman 1978) [NA-OH]. 95% of fish eaten in a Wisconsin study were ≤25 cm (Kirkpatrick 1940) [NA-WI]. Deep-water diving is "unusual behavior". Diet includes fish (preferred), frogs, tadpoles, newts, snakes, rodents, crayfish, insects and carrion. Herons forage solitarily and defend feeding territories, or in flocks where prey is dense. — [Short & Cooper 1985, USFWS HSI Model: Great Blue Heron, Biol. Rep. 82(10.99)](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/Herons/Heron_habitat.pdf) ([USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/fwsobs82_10_99))
- The same report says feeding sites need to be free of human disturbance for "the 4 hours following sunrise or preceding sunset", or else have a ~100 m buffer from habitation or ~50 m from slow traffic. It reads these as the main foraging periods. Foraging flights from the colony are mostly ≤4–5 km (Minnesota, Mississippi R.), up to 20.4 km, with a maximum of 29 km (Ohio). — [Short & Cooper 1985](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/Herons/Heron_habitat.pdf)
- The winter range runs from the "southern New England coast" southward (AOU 1983, quoted in the HSI), so there are no inland Maine herons in winter. — [Short & Cooper 1985](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/Herons/Heron_habitat.pdf)
- When wading to flush prey, herons work water ~15–25 cm deep. Their main food is small fish. — [Hinterland Who's Who (Canadian Wildlife Service)](https://www.hww.ca/wildlife/birds/great-blue-heron/). Prey is usually ~half the bill length. — [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/blue/learn/nature/great-blue-heron.htm)
- **Intake:** captive wild-caught herons needed **~300 g of live catfish per day** to maintain body mass. They caught healthy catfish poorly but preferred disabled fish and bluegills. — [Glahn, Dorr & Tobin 2000, N. Am. J. Aquaculture 62:149–156](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/icwdm_usdanwrc/626)
- Adults weigh ~2.4 kg. — [UWI species sheet](https://sta.uwi.edu/fst/lifesciences/sites/default/files/lifesciences/images/Ardea%20herodias%20-%20Great%20Blue%20Heron.pdf)
- **Maine presence [ME]:** people notice herons back in Maine around March 15. GPS-tagged birds left their wintering grounds ~March 22 and reached Maine colonies in the 1st–2nd week of April. — [Maine IF&W HERON blog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/maine-heron-observation-network/monitoring-maines-great-blue-herons-three-years-and-counting); [HERON enters year 15](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/maine-heron-observation-network/HERON-enters-year-15)
- [ME] Colonies are monitored May–August. In 2022, 55 of 105 surveyed colonies were active, with ≥469 nesting pairs. Coastal pairs fell from 1,208 (1983) to 430 (2009). The great blue heron is a Maine Species of Special Concern. — [Maine IF&W HERON fact sheet](http://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/03-HERONFactSheetrev5.pdf); [HERON blog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/maine-heron-observation-network/HERON-enters-year-15)
- [NA-WA] Strike success was higher in deeper water (False Bay); no cm values in the abstract. — [UW thesis 2014](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/handle/1773/27280)

### Inferences
- **Sim parameters:** forage only where water is <0.5 m (best 0.1–0.3 m). Take fish ≤20–25 cm and mostly 5–15 cm, plus frogs, tadpoles and crayfish. Intake is ~300 g/day ≈ 10–30 small fish (10–30 g). Activity peaks in the ~4 h after dawn and before dusk. Present from ~early April (when lake margins or the stream are open) to ~October/November freeze-up. Usually one bird per few hundred metres of shallow shore, and it defends its patch.
- Herons arrive before lake ice-out (~Apr 23), so early-season birds should use the stream and pond margins first.

### Gaps
- No Maine-specific fall departure date was found; typical is Oct–Nov, but I have no source.
- No wild daily intake figure; 300 g is from captive birds.
- I found no measured per-hour heron capture rates or numbers of herons per km of lake shore.
- The Birds of the World account (Vennesland & Butler) is paywalled.

## 3. Belted kingfisher (Megaceryle alcyon): perches, plunge depth, prey, intake, presence

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Kingfishers take fish 4–14 cm long (mostly <7.6–10 cm) from the top ~15 cm of water, almost always where the water is <60 cm deep. They also take crayfish, frogs and tadpoles. A kingfisher eats ~70 kcal/day (~15–20 small fish). Each holds ~0.8 km of lake shore and needs clear, calm water and bare perches over it. They are summer residents in Maine, and a few overwinter at open water.

### Cited Findings
- **Plunge depth:** "most fish are caught in water < 60 cm deep" and fish are not caught more than 60 cm below the surface (White 1953). W. J. Davis believed fish are generally caught "no more than 12 to 15 cm below the surface". The HSI model treats the top 15 cm as the capture zone and assigns maximum suitability to waters ≤60 cm deep. — [Prose 1985, USFWS HSI Model: Belted Kingfisher, FWS/OBS-82/10.87](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Prey size:** usually ≤10.2 cm (Imhof 1962). Michigan mean was <7.6 cm, range 2.5–17.8 cm, and fish >12.7 cm were hard to swallow (Salyer & Lagler 1949). Ohio streams: 4–14 cm (Davis 1982). Fish fed to nestlings averaged 9.2 cm (Minnesota, Cornwell 1963). Nestlings "require their weight in food each day". — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Diet [NA-MI, Maritime Canada]:** Michigan lakes: forage fish 53% and crayfish 12% of items; non-trout streams: 43% and 21% (Salyer & Lagler 1949). Maritime ponds and lakes: ninespine sticklebacks dominated, with white perch and alewife also eaten; warmer streams gave white suckers, sticklebacks, lake chub and banded killifish (White 1953) [closest region to Maine]. Ohio: stonerollers 37.6%, crayfish 13.3%; 90% of crayfish deliveries came during high, turbid water, and more crayfish were eaten when ice limited fishing. Alternate foods include frogs, tadpoles, small turtles and insects. — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Habitat needs:** clear water (absent from muddy waters, and absent temporarily after runoff muddies the water). Calm water matters: Michigan lake territories "invariably included" a sheltered bay, and wave action matters "almost as much as turbidity". Heavy pondweed or overhanging vegetation deters them. Kingfishers used riffles 71.3% of the time in Ohio streams. Optimal perch density is ~40 perches/km of shore (one per 25 m, each covering ~12.5 m either side). Fishing perch is preferably a bare branch at the water's edge. Night roosts are 30.5–61 m from water and 6.1–7.6 m up. — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Territory:** Michigan lakeside territories averaged ~0.8 km of shoreline (max 2.4 km); river territories were 2.4–4.8 km or more. Fishing sites usually lie within 1.6 km of the nest, with a daily range of 0.8–8.0 km. Kingfishers increased where beaver ponds opened overgrown streams. Most migrate south in late fall, but some stay north wherever water remains open. — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Intake and success [NA-CA, winter]:** a mean of **70.2 kcal/day** over 19 full days (a model predicted 72.3). Time budget: 79.6% hunting and 2.3% handling prey. Strikes from perches succeeded >50% of the time, strikes from hovering ~20%. Success fell with surface disturbance and peaked at dusk. Fish were 91% of prey. 25 birds were estimated to need 84,481 fish of 4 g per winter. — [Forsell 1983, MS thesis, Humboldt State](https://digitalcommons.humboldt.edu/etd/1592)
- Popular sources give dives from "20 feet or more" and hovering up to ~50 ft. — [Cambridge Day (popular)](https://www.cambridgeday.com/2022/06/11/belted-kingfishers-are-supreme-at-high-diving-plunging-into-unerring-captures-of-fish-to-eat/); [Audubon field guide (popular)](https://prelaunch.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/belted-kingfisher)

### Inferences
- **Sim parameters:** perch over water ~2–6 m up (a bare snag or overhanging limb at the edge). Strike only fish 3–12 cm within ~15 cm of the surface (max 60 cm) in water ≤60 cm deep, and only where clarity lets fish be seen at ≥60 cm. Disable hunting in wind or wave chop and in turbid post-storm water. Capture success: perch strikes ~50%, hover strikes ~20%.
- **Intake:** 70 kcal/day at ~1 kcal per g of wet fish (4–5 kJ/g; my assumption) is ~60–75 g/day, or ~15–18 fish of 4 g. Nestlings eat their own weight daily.
- **Presence:** from ice-out (April) to freeze-up (Nov). A kingfisher may stay into winter on the stream's open riffles, a plausible Hot Brook behaviour.
- **Density:** about one bird or pair per ~0.8 km of lake shore and per 2–5 km of stream.

### Gaps
- No measured perch-height distribution was found; the 2–6 m above is inferred from roost heights and popular dive heights.
- No Maine monthly occurrence data.
- No daily intake for breeding adults.

## 4. Osprey (Pandion haliaetus) and bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) at Maine lakes

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Ospreys (early April – early September in Maine) plunge feet-first to <1 m and take fish of mostly 150–300 g (25–35 cm). They succeed on ~25–85% of dives. An adult eats ~300–400 g/day, and a nesting family ~0.85–1.25 kg/day. Bald eagles are year-round Maine residents (733 nesting pairs in 2018). Inland they eat mostly fish, favouring brown bullhead, white sucker and chain pickerel, need ~0.3–0.5 kg/day, and scavenge more when ice closes the water.

### Cited Findings
**Osprey**
- [ME] Hog Island (coastal Maine) nest-cam pair: first adult back 6 April (2014). Fall migration starts at the end of August, and the young leave in early September. — [Journey North osprey cam](https://journeynorth.org/livecam/osprey.html)
- [ME] Ospreys nest statewide and fish rivers, lakes and ponds, feeding exclusively on fish caught live. — [Maine Rivers](https://mainerivers.org/river-life/osprey/)
- Fish are typically 150–300 g and ~25–35 cm, with a range of 50–1,200 g. Ospreys plunge ~1–2 ft (0.3–0.6 m). Success falls with wind and wave chop, and depends on water depth, fish availability and clarity. — [Avian Report (popular)](https://avianreport.com/osprey-feeding/); [Dyfi Osprey Project [EU] (popular)](https://dyfiospreyproject.com/node/1541)
- [NA-MD/VA, Chesapeake] 5.4 fish/day were delivered per nest, with a mean fish mass of 156.9 g and lengths of 4–43 cm. — [J. Raptor Res. 25(4):109–112](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v025n04/p00109-p00112.pdf)
- [EU-Finland] 5.2 ± 1.0 fish/day were brought to nests with 2–3 young, and a family with 3 young ate ~79 kg over the nestling period. German estimates are 92–166 kg. — [Ornis Fennica](https://ornisfennica.journal.fi/article/download/132953/81499)
- An adult needs ~300 g of fish/day. — [VIMS Osprey cam diet](https://www.vims.edu/bayinfo/ospreycam/about/diet/)
- [NA-CA] 523 of 639 fishing efforts (82%) succeeded; 56% needed only one dive. Success was 86% while chicks were fledging and 79% during incubation. Other sites: 83% (Flathead L., MT) and 80% (Eagle L., CA). — [Ueoka & Koplin 1973, Raptor Research 7(2):32–38](https://sora-dev.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/jrr/v007n02/p00032-p00038.pdf)
- [NE-CT] Ospreys can capture fish up to ~4 lb (1.8 kg). — [CT DEEP fact sheet](https://Portal.CT.gov/-/media/deep/wildlife/pdf_files/outreach/fact_sheets/ospreypdf.pdf)
- [NA] On lakes, ospreys soar and hover over larger lakes and hunt from perches on lakes with steep shoreline drop-offs. One observer saw them pick up dead yellow perch left by anglers. — [Wilson Bulletin 86(1):74–76 (1974)](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v086n01/p0074-p0076.pdf)

**Bald eagle**
- [ME] **Interior Maine diet:** food remains from 78 breeding and wintering areas (1976–80; ~1,400 prey) showed **fish were 79% of items inland**. **Brown bullhead, white sucker and chain pickerel** were the favoured freshwater foods. Coastal eagles ate mostly birds (gulls, black ducks; 76% of remains). Debris collections underrate fish. — [Todd, Young, Owen & Gramlich 1982, J. Wildl. Manage. 46:636–645](https://hero.epa.gov/hero/index.cfm/reference/details/reference_id/3181496)
- [ME] 733 nesting pairs in 2018, up 16% from 2013 (21 pairs in 1967). Growth is shifting west and north, and Downeast Maine is the stronghold. — [Maine IF&W 2018 release](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/news-events/single-release.html?id=815562); [Maine Public 2018](https://www.mainepublic.org/environment-and-outdoors/2018-10-04/bald-eagles-are-making-a-comeback)
- [ME] Inland eagles are primarily fish eaters. They hunt with extended low glides over open water or wait on shoreline perches. Many stay in Maine through winter, scavenging more when ice limits food. They fledge full-grown at ~3 months and reach adult plumage at 5 years. — [Maine IF&W bald eagle page](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/birds/bald-eagles.html)
- [NA-WA, winter] A wild 4.5 kg eagle needs ~494 kcal/day, equal to **~500 g of chum salmon per day**. Captive birds ate 92 g/kg/day on salmon. — [Stalmaster & Gessaman-linked dissertation, Utah State](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/etd_biology/477)

### Inferences
- **Osprey in the sim:** an aerial visitor from ~early April to early September (year-fraction ~0.10–0.52). It hovers or circles and strikes fish that sit within ~0.5–1 m of the surface, so a deep-holding fish is safe. It takes surface-oriented and pale-backed fish of ~20–40 cm: white sucker in the shallows, yellow perch, golden shiner, white perch, bass and pickerel. That is the size class the loon (≤25 cm) and heron (≤25 cm) mostly do not take. Success should be ~50–80% in calm, clear water and fall with wind. Rate: ~1–2 fish/day for an adult, ~5 fish/day while feeding young (Jun–Aug).
- **Eagle in the sim:** a year-round presence. It snatches fish at the surface, and in practice it scavenges or takes sluggish fish. Spring bullheads and spawning suckers in the stream and shallows are realistic targets. It eats ~0.3–0.5 kg/day and also preys on loon eggs and chicks.
- The bullhead/sucker/pickerel preference fits the sim's species and suggests eagles exploit shallow, sluggish or dying fish rather than fast schooling ones.

### Gaps
- No Maine lake study of osprey prey species was found.
- One search summary mentioned a shoreline study in which ospreys picked centrarchids over large suckers and pike. I could not check the source, so it is **unconfirmed** and not used.
- The ~24% minimum success figure attributed to Machmer & Ydenberg's (1990) review is unverified.
- No osprey or eagle visit frequency per lake was found.

## 5. Other fish-eating birds plausible at a small Maine lake or pond

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Mergansers matter most. Hooded mergansers breed on Maine beaver ponds and small lakes, eat fish, crayfish and aquatic insects, and their numbers track beaver activity. Common merganser broods forage on lakes and streams, and adults eat ~400 g of fish per day. Double-crested cormorants matter mainly where they colonise; inland they eat mostly yellow perch. American bitterns and green herons are minor, edge-of-marsh predators of small fish, frogs and insects.

### Cited Findings
- **Hooded merganser [ME]:** on a south-central Maine site where beaver trapping stopped, breeding hooded merganser densities rose to **23–29 per 100 km²** as beaver colonies and wetland area increased. — [McCall et al. 1996, Wetlands 16:163–172](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/beaver-populations-and-their-relation-to-wetland-habitat-and-bree/)
- **Hooded merganser diet (older U.S. samples):** fish 44%, crayfish 22%, aquatic insects 13%, other crustaceans 10% and amphibians 6% of items (Cottam & Uhler 1937; 138 birds, Nov–Mar). Michigan (n = 10): crayfish and fish each in 50% of stomachs, insects 20%, frogs 10%. — [Wikipedia summary of Birds of the World data (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooded_merganser)
- Ducklings forage in shallow marshes, small lakes, ponds and beaver wetlands. — [Northern Woodlands "Beaver Bird" [NE] (popular)](https://northernwoodlands.org/outside_story/article/beaver-bird-hooded-merganser)
- **Common merganser:** two captive females needed 9–10 oz (~255–285 g) of trout per day (~30% of body mass). They preferred 4–5 inch (10–13 cm) trout to 6–8 inch, and girth, not length, limited what they could swallow [NA-MI]. — [Michigan DNR Fisheries Research Report 1700 (abstract)](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/IFR/ifrlibra/Research/abstracts/1700abs.htm). A daily requirement of ~400 g is met at smolt densities of 2–30 per 100 m², depending on cover [NA-BC]. — [UBC thesis](https://open.library.ubc.ca/cIRcle/collections/ubctheses/831/items/1.0096565)
- Kingfisher literature notes that crayfish became a key kingfisher food in Maritime waters where **mergansers had reduced the fish**. That is evidence of a measurable merganser effect on small-fish stocks. — [Prose 1985 (White 1953)](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- **Double-crested cormorant:** [ME coast] nestling diets in Penobscot Bay were mostly sculpin, alewife and rock gunnel, with some yellow perch (673 regurgitant samples). — [OEHHA ecotox citation](https://ecotox.oehha.ca.gov/citation/foods-nestling-double-crested-cormorants-penobscot-bay-maine-usa-temporal-and-spatial). [NA-MN inland] At Leech Lake, yellow perch made up 80% (2005) and 40% (2006) of the diet. — [Waterbirds 35(sp1)](https://bioone.org/journals/waterbirds/volume-35/issue-sp1/063.035.sp110/Comparing-Bioenergetics-Models-of-Double-Crested-Cormorant-iPhalacrocorax-auritus-i/10.1675/063.035.sp110.full). [NE-VT/NY] At Lake Champlain, yellow perch dominated at one island and alewife at others. — [LCBP](https://www.lcbp.org/?p=10999)
- **American bittern:** eats insects (especially dragonflies), crustaceans, fish (eels, catfish, pickerel, sunfish, suckers, perch, killifish, sticklebacks), frogs, tadpoles, salamanders, snakes and voles. It ambush-hunts in dim light at vegetated shoreline fringes in shallow water. — [Cornell All About Birds](https://www.allaboutbirds.org/guide/American_Bittern/food)

### Inferences
- **Ranking for this sim:**
  1. Hooded merganser in the pond and beaver-influenced stream, and common merganser broods on the lake and stream. They are pursuit divers like a small loon, taking 5–13 cm fish, crayfish and insects.
  2. Green heron and bittern as minor ambushers in vegetated shallows (frogs, tadpoles, fingerlings).
  3. Cormorant only as an occasional transient. It would hit yellow perch hardest.
- Mergansers are summer residents on fresh water and leave at freeze-up. Common mergansers may stay on open stream reaches (not sourced).

### Gaps
- No green heron quantitative data was found (prey size and depth).
- No Maine inland cormorant data.
- No wild merganser brood consumption rates.
- No Maine monthly occurrence for any of these.

## 6. Mink (Neovison vison) and river otter (Lontra canadensis): diet, intake, use of a pond, winter

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Both are active all year. Otters eat mostly slow fish, especially suckers and minnows, plus crayfish (crayfish dominate in summer, fish in winter). An otter needs ~1.2–1.8 kg of prey/day, ranges widely (routes >30 km), and uses beaver lodges and dams for under-ice access. Mink are mostly nocturnal. They switch from fish (winter/spring) to crayfish (summer) to small mammals (fall), work ~300 m of shoreline per night, and live at ~1–3 per km of good shore.

### Cited Findings
**River otter**
- [ME] Diet is mostly fish, "particularly slower-swimming fish like suckers and minnow", plus crayfish and other shellfish, and occasionally turtles, snakes, muskrats and young beavers. — [Maine IF&W river otter page](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/mammals/river-otter.html)
- [ME] Maine's species assessment says otters prey on fish in proportion to availability and in inverse proportion to swimming ability, so suckers are taken more than their abundance predicts. Otters are active year round, use ice-free water in winter, and feed at any time of day with a **peak from dawn to mid-morning**. They use beaver houses and dens, log jams and rock piles for shelter. Idaho: a 10-mile (16 km) stream supports 4–7 otters (a female with 2–3 pups, 1–2 subadults, a breeding male). In Europe, lakeshore densities are ~2× stream densities. Maine's statewide carrying capacity was ~20,000 otters on 21,541 "habitat units" (1 unit = 1 mile of maximum-quality shoreline). Annual harvest was 608–812 (1976–84), half taken in the winter beaver season. — [Maine IF&W River Otter Assessment (1986)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/riverotter-speciesassessment.pdf)
- [ME] Otters are highly mobile, with travel routes that can exceed 20 miles. — [Maine IF&W mammal poster](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/mammalposter.pdf)
- [NA-AR] Doubly labelled water: **4,738 kJ/day** for a 7.84 kg otter, giving **35.1–52.7 kg wet prey per month** (low on a fish diet, high on a crayfish diet). Crayfish occurred in 85.0% of summer scats and 42.3% of winter scats; fish in 13.3% of summer and 57.7% of winter scats. — [Dekar, Magoulick & Beringer 2010, CJFAS 67:1439–1448](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70148714)
- [NA-MO] A 20–30 lb otter eats ~2.5 lb (~1.1 kg) of food per day. — [Missouri Conservationist](https://mdc.mo.gov/magazines/conservationist/2005-06/otter-appetites)
- [NA-AB boreal] Beaver remains were in only ~0.4% of 1,140 scats. Otters rarely use active lodges but use many inactive ones, and they **dig through beaver dams to keep under-ice passage between ponds**. — [U. Calgary thesis, otter–beaver interactions](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/23449)

**Mink**
- Mink are "chiefly nocturnal and remain active throughout the year". Fish are taken preferentially in winter and spring, when they are more vulnerable at low water and low temperature; crayfish are most frequent in summer (Sweden, Gerell; Quebec, Burgess 1978); small mammals rise in the fall. Each day a mink works a "core area" that **typically does not exceed 300 m of shoreline** (Gerell 1970). Core areas cover ~9.3% of the home range but take ~50% of the mink's time (Birks & Linn 1982). Activity and travel drop in winter. Female ranges are 7.7 ha (dense cover) to 20.1 ha (grazed), and ≤8 ha in Michigan. A mink can sustain itself on 1–2 km of stream (Idaho). Density on Vancouver Island coast was 1.5 to >3 mink/km of shoreline. Habitat quality falls when cottages simplify the shoreline, removing crayfish shelter. — [Allen 1984, USFWS HSI Model: Mink, FWS/OBS-82/10.61](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-61.pdf)
- [NA-IL] Mink switched strongly to crayfish in summer and preferentially occupied stream segments with crayfish hotspots. — [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/46746)
- [NA-SD] Mink killed 30% of radio-tagged hatchery and 32% of wild brown trout, with 83–92% of that predation in spring. — [USGS, Black Hills](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70193682)
- Farm-mink maintenance energy is 607–680 kJ per kg^0.75 per day. — [Energy requirement of growing mink](https://researcherslinks.com/current-issues/Energy-Requirement-Growing-Mink-Fed/20/1/4052/figures)

### Inferences
- **Otter in the sim:** a roaming visitor family (1–4 animals), not a resident. Idaho's 4–7 per 16 km of stream and Maine's ~1 per mile of top shoreline mean a given small lake or pond sees an otter group for a few days at a time, every few weeks. They hunt underwater at all depths, mostly at dawn to mid-morning. Prey: suckers, bullheads, sunfish, slow minnows, crayfish (summer) and frogs. Daily removal is ~1.2–1.8 kg per otter, a large pulse. In winter they keep hunting under the ice from beaver lodges and dams and from open stream water.
- **Mink in the sim:** a resident of the stream and pond edges, ~1 per 1–2 km of stream. Nocturnal, foraging the bank and shallows within ~300 m of the den each night. Seasonal prey switch: fish (winter/spring), crayfish (summer), voles (fall). Takes frogs and small fish in shallow water.
- **Mink intake (my estimate):** a ~1 kg mink at ~650 kJ/kg^0.75/day maintenance plus field activity needs roughly 0.8–1.3 MJ/day. At ~4–5 kJ/g wet prey that is ~150–300 g/day. This is an inference, not a measurement.

### Gaps
- No measured wild mink daily intake.
- No Maine otter diet study.
- No data on how often otters visit a particular pond.
- The Idaho and Maine otter densities are old (1980s).

## 7. Raccoon, beaver and muskrat

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Raccoons are nocturnal shoreline foragers. They take crayfish, frogs and fish in water a few cm deep and are the main predator of turtle nests: predation falls with distance from the pond edge (within ~50 m is worst) and can destroy most nests. Maine beavers live at ~0.15–0.32 colonies per km². They need gentle stream gradients (<6% best, none ≥15%), cut woody stems within ~30 m of the water, block fish passage, and greatly increase wetland area and merganser habitat. Muskrats need slow, cattail- or bulrush-rich water 15–50 cm deep and can eat out emergent stands.

### Cited Findings
**Raccoon**
- [NE-NH] Artificial turtle nests: 22% were depredated or disturbed within 7 days. Predation decreased with distance from pond edges, being higher within 50 m. Clumped nests suffered more. Raccoons were the most frequent predator on camera. — [Marchand, Litvaitis, Maier & DeGraaf 2002, Wildl. Soc. Bull.](https://scholars.unh.edu/nhaes/202)
- [NA-ON] Painted turtle hatching success was 69–79% for caged nests vs 60% uncaged. — [Chelonian Conserv. Biol. 14(1):64](https://ccb.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/ccab/14/1/article-p64.xml). [NA-NY] Raccoons destroyed 94% of identified snapping turtle nests in a northern New York study. — [Northern Woodlands [NE] (popular)](https://northernwoodlands.org/outside_story/article/snapping-turtles). [NA-IL] Natural painted turtle nest predation ran ~20% (1997) to ~96% (2005). — [ESA 2008 abstract](https://eco.confex.com/eco/2008/webprogram/Paper11350.html)
- [NA-TN] Herons and raccoons caused little crayfish mortality in deep water (0.4–0.7 m) but quickly consumed both small (25–30 mm) and large (50–75 mm) crayfish tethered in shallow water (0.05–0.15 m). — [Englund & Krupa 2000, Freshwater Biology 43:75–83](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/habitat-use-by-crayfish-in-stream-pools-influence-of-predators-de/)
- 97% of locomotor activity was nocturnal [EU-Germany, introduced raccoons]; daytime activity rose in summer for mothers with cubs. — [Michler 2016 dissertation summary](https://www.projekt-waschbaer.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Michler_2016_Ph.D.Dissertation_Summary.pdf). Densities were 0.8–18.3 per km², highest in bottomland deciduous forest near shorelines [NA-TN]. — [SEAFWA 1988](https://seafwa.org/journal/1988/demography-and-habitat-relationships-raccoons-western-tennessee)
- [ME] Skunks and raccoons are named as loon nest predators. — [Maine IF&W](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/livingwithloons.pdf)

**Beaver**
- [ME] Two 111 km² sites in south-central Maine, 1988–92. On the untrapped site, colony density rose from **0.15 to 0.32 per km²**, open water rose from 115 to 158 ha (+36%), and dams and wetlands tracked colony density (r = 0.99). On the trapped site density stayed at 0.19–0.20 per km². Breeding hooded mergansers rose to 23–29, mallards to 7–12 and Canada geese to 4–9 per 100 km². — [McCall et al. 1996](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/beaver-populations-and-their-relation-to-wetland-habitat-and-bree/)
- Beavers need a permanent, stable water level and can control it on small streams, ponds and lakes. 68% of colonies are on gradients <6%, 28% on 7–12%, 4% on 13–14%, and none at ≥15%. Valleys ≥46 m wide are best. Favourable habitat holds 0.4–0.8 colonies per km². A colony is a pair, yearlings and kits; subadults disperse 8–16 stream-km (up to 236 km). Most cutting is within 30 m of water (Massachusetts), up to 100–200 m, on stems usually <7.6–10.1 cm. Water lilies are eaten, and lodges sit in sheltered coves. — [Allen 1982, USFWS HSI Model: Beaver, FWS/OBS-82/10.30](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-30.pdf)
- [ME] At Moosehead Lake tributaries, three beaver dams on Squaw Brook explained the absence of salmon reproduction and blocked adult brook trout. Dam debris can make jumps impossible. — [Bangor Daily News outdoors blog (anecdotal fisheries report)](https://archive.bdnblogs.com/?p=459022)
- Kingfishers increased in proportion to the area flooded by beaver dams on formerly overgrown Michigan streams. — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)

**Muskrat**
- [ME] Muskrats need slow (lentic) wetlands with cattails, bulrushes, sweetflag, arrowhead, wild rice or sedges covering about three-quarters of the area. At high densities they can destroy their own food base. — [Maine IF&W Muskrat Assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/muskrat-speciesassessment.pdf). They eat cattails, reeds, rushes and pondweeds. — [Maine IF&W muskrat page](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/mammals/muskrat.html)
- Marsh densities range 0.03–86 per ha and stream densities 0.06–0.23 per ha. 1–6+ animals share a lodge. Muskrats favour tender shoots and rhizomes and can cause "eat-outs". Maine water levels of 15–51 cm are recommended (secondary). — [Hudsonia, News from Hudsonia 1994 [NA-NY]](https://hudsonia.org/s/Nfh-3-1994.pdf) (attribution of each number to this page is from a search summary; verify)

### Inferences
- **Raccoon in the sim:** a nightly shoreline visitor (dusk to dawn). It wades in ≤10–15 cm of water and takes crayfish, frogs, tadpoles and stranded or shallow fish. It digs up turtle nests within ~50 m of water; set a nest-loss probability of ~20–60% per season, with bad years up to ~90%. It also raids loon nests at the shoreline.
- **Beaver:** best as a slow landscape engineer on the stream and pond rather than an organism. A dam raises the stream pool level and widens its area, slows flow, stops fish and spawner passage, and adds woody structure (cover). Cutting happens within ~30 m of water. A dam's useful life is years.
- **Muskrat:** a cattail and pondweed grazer in the pond's shallow (15–50 cm) margin. Its lodges add structure.

- No beaver dam height or pond-area distribution for Maine.
- No muskrat consumption rates (g of plant per day).
- No data on beaver activity times.

## 9. How fish respond to bird and mammal predation risk (shallow-water avoidance, schooling, cover)

### Takeoff and calendar summary
Experiments show a size-dependent "bigger-deeper" rule. Fish larger than ~3–7 cm avoid water shallower than ~20 cm by day because herons, kingfishers and raccoons kill them there quickly. Small fish use the shallows as a refuge from piscivorous fish. Some fish move shallower at night, when diurnal birds are absent.

### Cited Findings
- Large (>3 cm) grazing fishes avoid water <20 cm deep, "where wading and diving predators feed most commonly and effectively". Small (<3 cm) fish are more abundant in the shallows, which refuge them from swimming predators [NA-OK; Panama]. **Tethering:** 20 armored catfish (7–16 cm SL) were tethered in 11–25 cm of water where herons and kingfishers fished. After 24 h, **0/6 remained at <18 cm vs 10/14 at >18 cm** (p = 0.005). **Pens:** after 12 days with little blue herons foraging nearby, catfish were largely eliminated from pens 10 and 20 cm deep but mostly survived in 30 and 50 cm. **Oklahoma stonerollers:** adults (>4 cm SL) were in water >20 cm on 20/20 scans, while young-of-year (2–3 cm) were in water <20 cm on 4/14 scans. When largemouth bass were added, adult stonerollers shifted from the 40–60 cm deep areas to shoals <20 cm within hours. Bird predation removed **77%** of the fish in a drying Florida pond (Kushlan 1976). Fish may move shallower at night (Starrett 1950, Iowa minnows), but the Panama night shift was slight. — [Power 1987, "Predator avoidance by grazing fishes in temperate and tropical streams: importance of stream depth and prey size"](https://ib.berkeley.edu/labs/power/publications/Power_1987_PredatorAvoidance.pdf)
- [NA-TN] **Harvey & Stewart 1991:** in 262 pools in 3 streams, pool depth predicted the size of the largest fish. In 5 field experiments, stonerollers, creek chubs and striped shiners ~100 mm TL survived much worse in **shallow (10 cm) than deep (40 cm max) pools**. The authors conclude that wading and diving predators (herons, raccoons) make shallow water dangerous for larger fish, not small ones. — [Harvey & Stewart 1991, Oecologia 87:336–342](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00634588)
- [NA-TN] Crayfish showed the same depth by size pattern under herons and raccoons in the shallows and fish in the deep. Small crayfish shift shallow when predatory fish are present; large crayfish stay deep. — [Englund & Krupa 2000](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/habitat-use-by-crayfish-in-stream-pools-influence-of-predators-de/)
- [NA-IL] Exclosure experiments (netting, perch manipulation) showed kingfishers and wading birds affect the two commonest minnows, striped shiner and central stoneroller. — [Steinmetz 2003 dissertation (Kohler & Soluk, chairs)](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/86629)
- Kingfishers forage riffle ends and calm shallows. Their capture success falls in deeper pools and in rippled or turbid water. — [Prose 1985](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/AK_Heritage/FWS/OBS/82/FWS-OBS-82-10-87.pdf)
- Herons strike only in ≤50 cm of clear water with a firm bottom. — [Short & Cooper 1985](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/Herons/Heron_habitat.pdf)

### Inferences
- **A mechanism for the sim:** a depth-, size- and light-dependent risk field from shore birds, which fish brains could sense via the existing `fear` input.
  - Risk is high for fish ≳5–7 cm in water <20 cm deep by day.
  - Risk falls steeply between 20 and 30–40 cm and is ~0 below ~50–60 cm (heron leg reach, kingfisher plunge).
  - It is highest at dawn and dusk (herons) and in calm, clear water (kingfishers).
  - It drops at night, except for raccoons and mink in ≤15 cm.
- **Expected behaviour:** bigger fish deeper by day. Young-of-year and small minnows stay in the shallows, where they are safe from bass and pickerel. Shallow foraging happens at night and dusk. This matches the user's observation that sunfish crowd the stream's 1 m pools: the pools are the bird refuge.
- **Loon and osprey risk** is open-water and surface-layer risk. Schooling and deeper holding should reduce it. I found no quantitative fish-response study for loons or ospreys.

### Gaps

- No quantitative study of fish behaviour responding to loons, ospreys or mergansers (schooling, depth shifts).
