# Shore geometry and shoreline vegetation of Maine lakes, ponds and small connecting streams

Scope: physical shore types, bank and beach geometry, ice push, vegetation zones from upland forest to open water, small-stream banks, shore phenology and snow, natural water-level regime, coarse woody habitat, and what is known about the Upper/Lower Hot Brook Lake shores (Danforth/Weston, Washington and Aroostook counties). Compiled 2026-10-15 for the Synapse Reef landscape (lake 0–3 m littoral shelves with a 14.4 m hole; a short rocky stream with ~1 m pools; a 4 m pond). Source region is flagged on every number: **[ME]** Maine, **[NE]** other New England, **[NY]**, **[NS]** Nova Scotia, **[WI]** Wisconsin/upper Michigan, **[other]**.

Access notes: Lakes of Maine pages sit behind a Cloudflare bot check and PMC served a reCAPTCHA. I did not try to get around either. Lakes of Maine values below come from search summaries and the project's earlier notes. The Deeds et al. (2023) Maine littoral-habitat paper was read only as its abstract, through the Europe PMC API.

## 1. Shore types on Maine lakes and ponds: frequency, bank height, slope, beach width, ice-push ridges, undercut banks

### Takeaway
Maine lake shores are glacial. Exposed shores are boulder, cobble and gravel; sand beaches occur in places; sheltered coves and inlets are organic muck with fen or marsh. The sand/cobble "Lakeshore Beach" is the commonest lakeshore community on medium and large Maine lakes. Ice push builds ramparts 0.5–1.5 m high and 1–3 m wide on gently sloping till or gravel shores of lakes roughly 0.8–4 km across, which is exactly the Hot Brook size class. No Maine survey of lake bank heights or shore-type percentages was found.

### Cited Findings
**Shore-type communities (Maine Natural Areas Program, MNAP)**
- **Lakeshore ecosystem.** MNAP's Lakeshore/Pondshore Ecosystem is the set of communities on the shoreline and in the vegetated near-shore water [ME].
  - It includes wetlands, plus shore areas whose soils are not saturated for most of the season but are disturbed by ice scour, flooding or waves.
  - Forests bordering lakes are classed separately, as upland ecosystems.
  - It occurs statewide except extreme southern Maine; state rank S5.
  - It lists 17 component communities: Alder Shrub Thicket, Bluejoint Meadow, Bulrush Bed, Cattail Marsh, Circumneutral–Alkaline Water Macrophyte Suite, Lakeshore Beach, Mixed Graminoid–Shrub Marsh, Mixed Tall Sedge Fen, Northern White Cedar Swamp, Northern White Cedar Woodland Fen, Pickerelweed–Macrophyte Aquatic Bed, Pipewort–Water Lobelia Aquatic Bed, Red Maple–Sensitive Fern Swamp, Red Maple Wooded Fen, Sweetgale Mixed Shrub Fen, Tussock Sedge Meadow, Water-lily–Macrophyte Aquatic Bed.
  - — [MNAP Lakeshore Ecosystem](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/eco_lakeshore.htm)
- **Lakeshore Beach** ("Lakeshore Sand/Cobble Beach", S4) [ME] — [MNAP Lakeshore Beach](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/lakeshorebeach.htm)
  - Setting: low sandy or gravelly lakeshores and their berms. They tolerate large water-level swings, ice scour and waves.
  - Substrate: sand, gravel or cobble. Berms of sand or gravel carry wave-cast organic wrack lines.
  - Ice push, waves and drawdown (natural or from impoundment) create broad open bands and berms. Herbs dominate or share dominance with shrubs.
  - MNAP calls it the most frequent lakeshore type on medium and large Maine lakes. It gives no numbers for slope, width or water-level range.
- **Glacial substrates and plant cover** (earlier project notes, from the USGS 43-lake study) [ME]:
  - Clear Maine lakes with rocky, gravelly or sandy littoral zones usually have only sparse rooted plants, dominated by *Eriocaulon* (pipewort).
  - Highly coloured lakes have plant communities of mainly *Scirpus* (bulrush) and floating-leaved plants.
  - — [USGS WRI 80-69, A limnological study of 43 selected Maine lakes](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf)
- **Deeds et al. (2023), littoral habitat condition indices for Maine and New England** [ME/NE]:
  - The study used National Lakes Assessment physical-habitat (PHAB) methods, with Maine-specific finer-scale surveys.
  - Lakeshore residential development was treated as the main stressor of littoral habitat.
  - The earlier project notes record two further results: deeper lakes have steeper littoral slopes, and sand cover rose with shoreland disturbance. I could not re-verify these because the full text was CAPTCHA-blocked.
  - — [Deeds et al. 2023, Lake Reserv. Manage., PMC10642257](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10642257/)

**Ice-push ramparts (New England)**
- **Gardner Lake, CT (Pessl 1969, CRREL Research Report 259)** [NE] — [Pessl 1969, CRREL RR 259](https://erdc-library.erdc.dren.mil/jspui/bitstream/11681/5743/1/CRREL-Research-Report-259.pdf) (search summary)
  - A modern ice-push ridge on the NW shore was 0.6–1.2 m high and 1.2–3.1 m wide.
  - Thermal expansion of the ice, not wind, was the main cause. An ice-temperature rise of about 1 °C/h sustained for 6 h was enough to cause thrust.
  - Over winter 1966–67, about 14 m³ of beach material was reworked into a discontinuous ridge along 260 m of shore.
- **General statements (Journal of Glaciology article)** [NE]:
  - Permanent ramparts form where ice pushes against gently sloping glacial till or gravel. They are commonly 0.5–1.5 m high, parallel to the shore.
  - Little Lake Sunapee, NH, is nearly ringed with ramparts.
  - The most violent pushes come from expansion.
  - — [J. Glaciol. article via Cambridge Core](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/product/E254F883421CFAEAFCDFFBE58FD75E74/core-reader) (search summary; I could not open the article to confirm author and year)
- **Lake Bomoseen, VT (Gatto, Ferrick & Calkins 2001, CRREL report to Vermont DEC)** [NE] — [Gatto et al. 2001, Ice-Push Damage on Lake Bomoseen](https://anrweb.vt.gov/PubDocs/DEC/WSMD/lakes/docs/lp_bomoicepush.pdf)
  - Lake size and mechanism: thermal-expansion push predominates on lakes and bays under about 2.5 mi (4 km) across; wind-driven push predominates on wider ones (citing Gatto 1982).
  - Wagner (1970) suggested lakes under about 0.5 mi (0.8 km) across are too small for appreciable expansion.
  - Pessl's Gardner Lake rampart is restated as 2–4 ft high and 4–10 ft wide, with a net shoreward ice movement of about 3 ft over 30 days.
  - Ice movement inland: March 1990, ice moved 2–7 ft (0.6–2.1 m) at different sites. February 1998, 1.5–6.5 ft (0.5–2.0 m).
  - Lake-bed slopes at the damaged reaches ranged from 0.03 to 0.3 (vertical per horizontal).
  - A gently sloping shore, including gently sloping bedrock, lets ice ride up with little resistance. A vertical bank resists ice movement.
  - Wind push (Lake Ontario, fetch ≥6 mi, so not a small-lake case): spring breakup piled ice 8 ft high on shore in 10 minutes and moved 37–450 lb boulders up to 21 ft inland (Gilbert & Glew 1986; Gilbert 1991).
- **Maine example:** the Maine Geological Survey archives a photo of an ice-push rampart at Bryant Pond, Bethel [ME] — [MGS field photo](https://digitalmaine.com/mgs_geologic_field_photos/12293).

**Bank slope (regulatory proxy)**
- Under Maine NRPA Chapter 305, riprap may be used on lake shorelines only where the eroded bank is steeper than 3 horizontal : 1 vertical (33%). On gentler banks, vegetation must be used instead [ME] — [Portland Water District, Lake Shoreline Riprap](https://careers.pwd.org/sites/default/files/Lake-Shoreline-Riprap.pdf). This shows that steeper eroding lake banks are common enough to regulate. It gives no frequency.

### Inferences
- **Shore-type mix for a Hot Brook-like lake.** Not measured; built from the IFW descriptions in §7 plus MNAP:
  - rocky (boulder/cobble over till) shores: about 60–75% of the perimeter;
  - fen, shrub and marsh wetland shores in sheltered coves and at the inlet and outlet: 15–30%;
  - sand or gravel beach: 5–10% (one large beach on Upper Hot Brook).

  A deeper, clearer lake would have more cobble/boulder and fewer wetland shores. A shallow pond would have more muck and fen.
- **Ice-push ramparts.** Both Hot Brook lakes (~3–4 km long) fall in the thermal-expansion size class (0.8–4 km). Ramparts 0.5–1.2 m high and 1–3 m wide, at the winter water line on gently sloping till or gravel, are a realistic feature. They are absent on steep bedrock and on floating or organic shores.
- **Suggested above-water bank profiles for the sim.** Built from the slope ranges above. Bank heights are not sourced (see Gaps).
  - Bedrock/boulder point: 10–30% slope that becomes steeper; 1–3 m of exposed rock and glacial boulders before forest soil.
  - Cobble-gravel shore: 5–15% slope; a 0.5–1.2 m rampart set back 1–3 m from summer water; alder and sweetgale behind it.
  - Sand beach: 2–5% slope (matching Bomoseen's gentlest bed slope of 0.03); a 0.3–1 m berm with a wrack line. A 0.3–0.5 m seasonal drop exposes 6–25 m of sand (width = drop ÷ slope).
  - Organic fen or marsh shore: mat surface within 0–0.3 m of the water. It rises imperceptibly into sweetgale and leatherleaf shrub (~1 m), then alder (1–3 m), then spruce, fir and cedar.
  - Glacial-till bank under forest: a low scarp of about 0.3–1.5 m at the high-water line, undercut where roots hold the soil. The 3:1 NRPA threshold implies many eroding banks are steeper than 33%.
- **Undercut banks.** These occur where waves or currents cut till below a root mat: on lake points and windward shores, and on stream outer bends. Expect overhangs of 0.1–0.5 m. This is an inference; I found no Maine measurement.

### Gaps
- No Maine (or New England) survey gives the percentage of lake perimeter by shore type, mean bank height above normal water, or beach width. The NLA PHAB data (bank angle and height classes, drawdown) behind Deeds et al. (2023) hold this but were not accessible. The 2017 NLA technical support document is a lead: [EPA NLA 2017 TSD](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2022-06/National%20Lakes%20Assessment%202017_TSD_June2022_220610_1.pdf).
- The bank-height bands found in a Maine DEP "OUR SHORE" tool (<3 ft low, 3–6 ft moderate, >6 ft high bluff) are for marine and coastal shores, not lakes, so they were not used. — [Island Institute OUR SHORE report](https://www.islandinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/LDI-OUR-SHORE-Full-Report.pdf)
- No Maine-specific count of lakes with ice-push ramparts. A "26 lakes in ME/NH/VT" figure appeared in one search summary but could not be traced to a source, so it is not used.

## 2. Shoreline vegetation zonation, upland forest to open water: species, zone depths and widths, seasonal timing, lake vs pond vs stream

### Takeaway
A Maine lakeshore runs through these zones:

| Zone | Main plants | Depth / height |
|---|---|---|
| Upland | spruce–fir with white pine, hemlock, red maple, birches; cedar on wet toe-slopes | — |
| Shrub border | speckled alder (1–3 m); sweetgale, leatherleaf and meadowsweet (~1 m) on peat | — |
| Emergent marsh | sedges, bluejoint, bur-reed, cattail at the edge; pickerelweed beds; bulrush beds | pickerelweed beds < 0.7 m; bulrush beds always > 0.15 m of water |
| Floating-leaved beds | white and yellow water-lily, watershield | 0.5–2.5 m |
| Submersed rosette plants | pipewort, water lobelia, quillwort, on sandy bottoms | — |

Wave and ice exposure plus water-level fluctuation strip shrubs from exposed shores, leaving open herbaceous beach. Sheltered coves, inlets and outlets carry the full marsh, fen and shrub sequence. Ponds and stream backwaters are dominated by the quiet-water types (pickerelweed, water-lily, sweetgale fen, alder).

### Cited Findings
**Aquatic and emergent zones (MNAP fact sheets, Maine) [ME]**
- **Open-water marsh** (Water-lily–Macrophyte Aquatic Bed, S5) — [MNAP Open-water Marsh](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/openwatermarsh.htm)
  - Depth and substrate: quiet water mostly 0.5–2.5 m deep, on silty organic muck.
  - Dominants: white water-lily, yellow water-lily, or (more rarely) watershield. Variants are dominated by floating-leaved pondweeds such as bigleaf pondweed. Bladderworts and pipewort are common underneath.
  - Pickerelweed is basically absent.
  - Pattern: small patches, often linear along lakeshores, and also in quiet stream reaches.
- **Pickerelweed marsh** (Pickerelweed–Macrophyte Aquatic Bed, S5) — [MNAP Pickerelweed Marsh](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/pickerelweedmarsh.htm)
  - Depth and substrate: generally under 0.7 m deep, usually on muck, in acidic to circumneutral quiet water of lakes, ponds and streams.
  - Cover: total cover 30–100%, typically over 50%.
  - Species: pickerelweed, yellow water-lily and bladderworts are almost always present. Water bulrush, white water-lily and pipewort are also characteristic.
  - Seasonal behaviour: its plants die back below the surface at the end of the season, unlike the adjacent cattail marshes, which are dominated by cattail and shrubs.
- **Bulrush marsh** (Bulrush Bed, S4) — [MNAP Bulrush Marsh](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/bulrush_marsh.pdf)
  - A deepwater marsh with standing water over 15 cm deep all year, except in unusually long low water.
  - Dominated by tall, non-persistent hardstem and softstem bulrush, chair-maker's rush and bayonet rush, with low species richness.
  - Often near the inlets and outlets of lakes, or along slow reaches of larger streams.
- **Lakeshore Beach** (see §1). Herbs and low shrubs on sand and cobble [ME] — [MNAP Lakeshore Beach](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/lakeshorebeach.htm)
  - On cobble: sweetgale, tufted hairgrass, water parsnip, spikerushes, common horsetail, creeping spearwort, three-square.
  - On sand: large cranberry, switchgrass, black chokeberry, deertongue.
  - On berms: a sparse canopy of common juniper, speckled alder, red maple, white pine and gray birch.

**Shrub and peatland zones (MNAP) [ME]**
- **Sweetgale Mixed Shrub Fen** (S4) — [MNAP Sweetgale Fen](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/sweetgalefen.htm)
  - Shrubs about 1 m tall, dominated by sweetgale, leatherleaf and meadowsweet/hardhack. Alder is usually present but not dominant.
  - Graminoids (slender sedge, tussock sedge, bluejoint) average about 20% cover.
  - Grows on peat or muck in basin wetlands along open water, or in beaver flowages. Slow-moving open water usually borders it.
- **Alder Thicket** (Alder Shrub Thicket, S5) — [MNAP Alder Thicket](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/aldershrub.htm) (search summary)
  - Tall shrubs 1–3 m, dominated by speckled alder at more than 20% cover (usually over 40%).
  - On muck or peat in basin wetlands, often only temporarily flooded.
  - Very common where wet clearings revert, such as old beaver meadows.
- **Leatherleaf Boggy Fen** (S4) — [MNAP Leatherleaf Boggy Fen](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/leatherleafboggyfen.htm) (search summary)
  - Leatherleaf is always present, at 30–60% cover at most sites. Vegetation is mostly under 1 m.
  - Peat substrate, average pH 4.0; trees under 15% cover.
- **Bog Moss Lawn** — [MNAP Bog Moss Lawn](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/bogmosslawn.htm) (search summary)
  - A *Sphagnum* carpet in the wettest peatland spots, including boggy pond margins. Vascular plants contribute under 25% cover.
- **Maine River Wetland Complex** (Washington County, Eastern Interior ecoregion; Pocomoonshine and Crawford lakes) [ME] — [MNAP focus area](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/focusarea/maine_river_wetland_complex.pdf) (search summary)
  - About 4,200 acres of peatland around the lakes.
  - Mixed tall sedge fen is the most abundant type, with sweetgale mixed shrub fen also present.
  - The fen types sit closest to the lakeshore and are hydrologically connected to it; the bogs are more isolated.
  - This is a regional analogue for wetland shores near Hot Brook.

**Speckled alder biology (USDA FEIS)** [NE/other] — [FEIS Alnus incana](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/tree/alninc/all.html)
- Size and form: up to 9 m tall and 12 cm in stem diameter, multistemmed, often forming dense thickets that sprout from the root crown.
- Habitat: streambanks, lakeshores, swamps, and bog and fen margins. Beaver-pond margins are favourable sites for establishment (Michigan).
- Flooding: tolerates spring flooding, but growth is severely reduced, and there is about 33% mortality, if water stands at or above the root crown for 30 days or more.
- Light: prefers open sites; tolerates moderate shade.
- Phenology:
  - Flowers (catkins) before leaf-out: March–May in the Northeast, late April–May in the Adirondacks.
  - Fruit ripens August–October; seeds disperse in winter, often over snow.
- Acadia NP associations [ME]: red maple / speckled alder–winterberry swamp woodland, and speckled alder–red-osier dogwood thickets.

**What controls the zones**
- Nova Scotia lakeshore wetlands [NS] — [Nova Scotia, Lakeshore wetlands](https://novascotia.ca/natr/wildlife/habitats/nswetlands/lakeshore.asp)
  - These form only in sheltered shallow margins where wave action is low and the shore is not rocky.
  - Calm water: broad-leaved pickerelweed and arrowhead. Wave-disturbed shores: thin-leaved sedges and rushes.
  - Shallow open water: cattail, bur-reed, watershield, water-milfoil, and floating water-lily mats.
  - Zones are set by water depth, nutrients, wave action and seasonal water-level fluctuation.
- Keddy's shoreline work (Ontario and Nova Scotia, 1975–1995) [NS/other] — [P. Keddy, Shorelines](https://drpaulkeddy.com/conservation/shorelines/)
  - Exposure to waves and ice scour, combined with water-level fluctuation, reduces shrub cover and allows a rich herbaceous shore flora.
- Keddy & Wisheu (1989) [NS] — (search summary of [Keddy papers via archive.org](https://archive.org/download/biostor-138319/biostor-138319.pdf))
  - Atlantic coastal-plain plants are confined to infertile sand and gravel lakeshores where fluctuating water keeps shrubs from colonising broad, open shores.

### Inferences
**Suggested shore-normal zonation for a sheltered Maine cove.** Distances and depths come from the cited ranges; zone widths are my estimates.

| Zone | Position | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Upland | > 1–2 m above water | spruce–fir with white pine and red maple; hemlock and cedar on wet toe-slopes |
| Alder thicket | 0.3–1 m above summer water; 3–15 m wide | — |
| Sweetgale / leatherleaf fen | 0–0.3 m above water; 5–50 m wide | in big coves, may be a quaking peat mat |
| Sedge / bluejoint edge; cattail and bur-reed | 0–0.3 m of water | — |
| Pickerelweed bed | 0.1–0.7 m | — |
| Bulrush bed | 0.15–1 m | near inlet and outlet |
| Water-lily / watershield bed | 0.5–2.5 m | densest at about 1 m |
| Sparse pipewort / lobelia rosettes, or pondweeds | beyond | — |

**Exposed (rocky or windward) lake shore**
- Boulders at the waterline; little or no emergent band.
- A cobble beach with sweetgale and tufted hairgrass.
- A rampart with juniper, alder, gray birch and white pine.
- Then forest.
- Water-lilies only in sheltered pockets.

**Lake vs pond vs stream**
- Deeper lake: zones are compressed onto the steeper littoral of exposed shores, with wide marsh only in coves.
- 4 m pond (mostly under 2.5 m, muck bottom): could be ringed by fen and shrub. Floating-leaved beds could cover much of the water area (0.5–2.5 m band), with pickerelweed around the edge.
- Stream: alder floodplain or thicket on the banks (see §3), with water-lily and pickerelweed only in slow pools and backwaters.

**Phenology for rendering.** Assembled; Maine-specific dates are not sourced.
- Water-lily and watershield leaves reach the surface late May–June.
- Pickerelweed leaves stand above water by June.
- Flowering July–August, with pickerelweed continuing into September.
- Pickerelweed, bulrush and lilies die back below the water in late September–October.
- Cattail stands persist as brown standing stems through winter.
- Alder catkins hang before leaf-out (late April–May); alder leaves fall without much colour.

- No Maine-specific leaf-emergence or senescence dates for water-lilies, pickerelweed or cattail.
- I found no Maine (or New England) source for wild rice (*Zizania*) depth or abundance. It is not expected at Hot Brook; IF&W does not mention it.
- I did not obtain the MNAP fact sheets for Cattail Marsh, Tussock Sedge Meadow, Mixed Tall Sedge Fen, Northern White Cedar Swamp or Red Maple Swamp, which would add species and setting detail.

## 3. Stream banks of small Maine streams like Hot Brook: alder thickets, canopy closure, bank height, undercut banks, wood

### Takeaway
Alder is the characteristic streambank shrub in Maine. MNAP's Alder Floodplain type has alder over 30% cover, plus red maple, red-osier dogwood, bluejoint, and ostrich and sensitive fern, on seasonally flooded, ice-scoured mineral soil. A Hot Brook-sized stream (5–11 m bankfull) has banks about 0.3–0.5 m above bankfull-stage bed level. Maine streams are notably wood-poor after the log-drive era, with lower Sheepscot counts around 8 pieces per 100 m. The Hot Brook connector itself is rocky, hard-bottomed and about 200 m long.

### Cited Findings
**Alder Floodplain** (S4) [ME] — [MNAP Alder Floodplain](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/alderfloodplain.htm)
- Setting: shrub wetland on moderate- to high-energy riverbanks that are seasonally flooded and ice-scoured, usually on mineral soil.
- Woody species often show ice-scour damage early in the season.
- Alder makes up over 30% cover. Red maple, American elm and red-osier dogwood may be co-dominant.
- Herbs: bluejoint is common; also ostrich fern, sensitive fern, royal fern, boneset, virgin's bower, tall meadow-rue and woodland horsetail.
- Alder Thickets instead occupy stagnant basins on muck or peat.

**Hot Brook connecting stream** [ME]
- About 1/8 mile (≈200 m) long and non-navigable [ME] — [MDIFW Upper Hot Brook Lake survey](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf)
- Rocky and hard-bottomed, with no coldwater-fish habitat. Boaters must paddle, pole or walk a boat through parts of it [ME] — [MDIFW Lower Hot Brook Lake survey](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)

**Channel size** (Maine regional curves; from earlier project notes) [ME] — [Dudley 2004, USGS SIR 2004-5042](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2004/5042/pdf/sir2004-5042.pdf)
- Bankfull width ≈ 7.67·DA^0.52 ft and mean bankfull depth ≈ 0.594·DA^0.34 ft (DA in mi²).
- For 5–20 mi² this gives 5–11 m wide and 0.3–0.5 m deep.
- Bankfull flow is about 3× mean annual flow and is equalled or exceeded about 8% of the time (about 30 days/yr).

**Large wood in Maine streams**
- Magilligan et al. surveyed coastal Atlantic-salmon rivers [ME]. They found wood loading remarkably low and logs small, with riparian trees too small to recruit as functional large wood — [EBTJV wood-addition grant application, Sheepscot/Narraguagus/Machias](https://easternbrooktrout.org/groups/whitewater-to-bluewater/funded-projects/ebtjv-projects/wood-additions-into-the-sheepscot-narraguagus-machias-watersheds-maine/application-for-wood-additions-into-the-sheepscot-narraguagus-machias-and-east-machias-watersheds-in-maine)
- West Branch Sheepscot River, 2005 survey [ME] — search summary; I did not open the source page:
  - 2.7 km reach; pieces ≥10 cm diameter and >2 m long counted.
  - 210 pieces, or 78 pieces/km (≈7.8 per 100 m), with little stable wood.
- Northern New England wood-addition guideline (VT/NH/ME) [NE] — search summary; the guideline document was not opened. Most likely source: [NRCS VT 395 practice](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/api/CPSFile/22821/395_VT_PS_Stream_Habitat_Improvement_and_Management-SWA_Practical_2020).
  - At least 4 pieces of ≥6 in (15 cm) wood per 100 ft for streams under 10 ft bankfull width (≈13 per 100 m).
  - At least 6 per 100 ft for larger streams (≈20 per 100 m).
- Log-drive history [ME]: the Maine Forest Service links wood scarcity to log driving. Dams were built and opened to flush logs, scouring channels, and landowners were long advised to remove wood (search summary of [MFS fisheries enhancement](https://www.maine.gov/DACF/mfs/policy_management/water_resources/fisheries_enhancement.html)). The page itself, when fetched, only says large wood diverts flow, traps sediment, makes pools and gives cover for juvenile fish.
- Wood longevity [other]: riparian conifer wood may persist for decades in streams, hardwood about 5–10 years (Cedarholm et al. 1997, as cited) — [KRIS Sheepscot riparian/LWD](https://wvvw.krisweb.com/krissheepscot/krisdb/html/krisweb/watershed/riparian_lwd.htm)

**Shade and canopy**
- MDIFW: healthy brook-trout streams need intact, mature wooded riparian corridors, which shade the water and supply wood and leaf litter [ME] — [MDIFW brook trout & forestry fact sheet](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/brook_trout_factsheet_forestry.pdf)
- Maine buffer and temperature studies [ME] — [KRIS Sheepscot riparian/LWD](https://wvvw.krisweb.com/krissheepscot/krisdb/html/krisweb/watershed/riparian_lwd.htm)
  - Removing 80% of riparian vegetation on the East Branch Piscataquis raised maximum temperature and daily swings (Garman 1984, as cited).
  - A 5% reduction in shading had no significant effect (Mullen & Moring 1988, as cited).
- Percent canopy cover falls as stream order rises (Lehigh watershed, PA; conference abstract, no numbers) [other] — [GSA 2005 abstract](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2005NE/webprogram/Paper83571.html)

### Inferences
- **Hot Brook cross-section for the sim.**
  - Rocky (cobble–boulder) bed in riffles, with ~1 m pools. The pools are from the project's own field notes (user observation, 1.1 m); they are not a published value.
  - Banks 0.3–0.6 m above summer water on mineral till.
  - Alder 2–5 m tall overhanging both banks, with red maple, balsam fir and spruce behind.
  - Ice-scoured, partly bare cobble at the bank toe.
  - Undercuts of 0.1–0.4 m on outer bends where alder and spruce roots bind the bank.
- **Canopy.** Alder 2–5 m tall (up to 9 m) on both banks of a 3–8 m channel should close most of the sky over riffles. Expect roughly 60–90% canopy closure, more open over wide pools and at the lake mouths. This is an estimate with no Maine measurement.
- **Wood.** About 5–15 pieces (≥10 cm) per 100 m of channel: some alder stems and spruce or fir boles, a few spanning the channel, most lying along the banks. Expect fewer than in an old-growth stream.
- **Beaver.** Beaver dams on outlet streams like this are realistic in Maine (§5 and the earlier notes). A dam would flood the alder and fen zone and create a short ponded reach.

### Gaps
- No measured canopy closure, bank height or undercut dimensions for small Maine streams or for Hot Brook itself.
- No wood count for Hot Brook. The 78 pieces/km Sheepscot figure and the 4 per 100 ft guideline were not verified from the original documents.

## 4. Seasonal changes visible at the shore: leaf-out, fall colour, leaf drop, emergent die-back, snow on ground and on lake ice

### Takeaway
In the Danforth–Houlton area, hardwoods leaf out in about the first to third week of May. Fall colour peaks in the first half of October: Maine Forest Service Zone 4, "Eastern Mid-Maine" (Houlton–Millinocket–Calais), peaked 30 Sep–19 Oct in 2020–2024. Leaves are mostly down by late October to early November.

Ground snow cover (≥1 in) lasts about 120 days at Houlton and about 134 days at Caribou (1991–2020). Continuous cover typically runs from mid–late December to the first half of April. Snow on the lake ice turns to slush and white ice. Land snow typically melts 2–3 weeks before ice-out (late April to early May), so there is a short spell of grey, snow-free ice.

## 5. Water-level regime of natural (undammed) Maine lakes and ponds; how much shore is exposed

### Takeaway
I found no published annual stage range for an undammed Maine lake. The nearest analogue is two glacial kettle lakes in central New York with natural outlets, which averaged about 1.5 ft (≈0.45 m) of annual fluctuation, against 2.5–6 ft for outlet-less lakes. A 1951 Vermont finding put Seymour Lake's natural level in a 14-inch (36 cm) band.

The pattern is a spring high with snowmelt and ice-out (late April–May), a decline through summer to an August–September low, and a recharge in October–November. For a Hot Brook-type lake with a rocky natural outlet, about 0.3–0.5 m is a reasonable annual range. That exposes metres of cobble shore and up to tens of metres of flat sand or mud by late summer.

### Cited Findings
- **Tully Lakes, NY (USGS WRIR 01-4166, Kappel, Miller & Hetcher 2001)** [NY] — [USGS WRIR 01-4166](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/2001/4166/wri014166.pdf)
  - Average annual water-level fluctuation was 2.5–6 ft in three western (landlocked) kettle lakes and only about 1.5 ft in the two eastern lakes, because those have natural outlets that discharge quickly.
  - Seasonal groundwater declines were 1.5–8 ft (spring 2000 to fall).
  - A diverted landlocked lake fluctuated about 5 ft per year.
- **Seymour Lake, VT** [NE]: the Vermont PSC found in 1951 that the natural surface level stayed within a 14-inch range, from 6 in above to 8 in below the dam crest — [VT S.227 (2014) as introduced](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2014/WorkGroups/Senate%20Natural%20Resources/Bills/S.227/S.227~Zachary%20Mauldin~As%20Introduced~1-29-2014.pdf)
- **Upper bound** [other]: natural water-level fluctuation in stratified temperate and subtropical lakes is typically up to 1.5 m a year and up to 3 m over several years — [Zohary 2017, Ecological impacts of excessive WLF](https://cybrary.friendsofmerrymeetingbay.org/FOSL%5CResearch%5CEcology/Zohary%202017%20Ecological%20impacts%20of%20excessive%20water%20level%20fluctuations%20in%20stratified%20freshwater%20lakes.pdf). Lake Constance averages 1.5 m — [Wantzen et al. 2008, Hydrobiologia 613 cover note](https://epic2-clone.awi.de/id/eprint/18960/1/Wan2008d.pdf) (search summary). These are large lakes and exceed what a small Maine drainage lake would see.
- **Maine groundwater** [ME]: levels rise in spring with runoff and rain and fall through summer and fall; the annual fluctuation is about 3–4 ft — [Maine Geological Survey, Water Resources in Maine](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mgs/explore/water/facts/water.htm) (search summary)
- **Maine DEP** [ME]: most lakes are poorly suited to large drawdowns, and managed changes are usually only a few feet at most. DEP recommends a stable level before ice-in, because big swings worsen shoreline erosion — [Maine DEP Water Level Management](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/lakes/waterlevel.html) (search summary). Maine rules define natural water-level variation without giving a number — [06-096 CMR ch. 587](https://regulations.justia.com/states/maine/06/096/chapter-587/section-096-587-2) (search summary)
- **Stream flow** [ME]: bankfull flow is about 3× mean annual flow and is reached or exceeded about 30 days a year — [Dudley 2004](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2004/5042/pdf/sir2004-5042.pdf) (via earlier project notes). The earlier notes also estimate Hot Brook's late-summer flow at about 0.3× the mean.
- **National Lakes Assessment** [US]: a national analysis found about 40% of US lakes had medium drawdown in 2007, 37% small and 21% large. In 2012, more than 75% had no measurable drawdown — (search summary; source page [EPA assessment record](https://assessments.epa.gov/risk/document/&deid=349121) not opened)
- **Ice push and water level** [NE]: at Bomoseen (regulated to within 3 in), high winter water lets ice form higher on the shore and push harder. In January 1998 the level ranged +2 to +11 in — [Gatto et al. 2001](https://anrweb.vt.gov/PubDocs/DEC/WSMD/lakes/docs/lp_bomoicepush.pdf)
- **Shore flora** [NS]: lower-watershed Nova Scotia lakes, which fluctuate more, are where rare coastal-plain shore flora is concentrated, on open shores kept shrub-free by fluctuation — [NS SAR conservation fund lakeshore report](https://novascotia.ca/NATR/wildlife/conservationfund/final08/NSSARCF07_09Lakeshore.pdf) (search summary); [Keddy shorelines](https://drpaulkeddy.com/conservation/shorelines/)

### Inferences
**Seasonal stage curve for an undammed Hot Brook-type lake** (relative to the summer-low datum):
- Ice-on (Dec): about +0.1 m; slow decline under ice through winter.
- Ice-out to mid-May: rise to about +0.3–0.5 m.
- June–July: decline.
- Late Aug–Sept: the 0.0 m low.
- Oct–Nov rains: rise to about +0.1–0.2 m.
- Dry years can drop a further 0.1–0.2 m; wet springs can add 0.2 m.

**The pond**
- If the pond drains through a fen or beaver dam, use about 0.2–0.4 m.
- A beaver dam raises the mean level and damps the summer drop.

**Exposed band width** (= stage drop ÷ shore slope)

| Shore | Slope | Exposed by a 0.4 m drop | Exposed material |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sand flat | 2% | 20 m | sand flat, then a drying wrack line |
| Sand beach | 5% | 8 m | — |
| Cobble | 15% | 2.7 m | cobble with stranded algae |
| Bedrock or boulder | 40% | 1 m | a "bathtub ring" of dark lichen and stained rock |

On a fen shore the floating or saturated mat follows the water and little mud is exposed.

**Stream**
- The stage swing is larger relative to depth: about 0.3–0.5 m between spring bankfull and late-summer low on a 5–11 m channel.
- In late summer, riffles are trickles between dry cobble bars, and the banks stand 0.3–0.6 m above the water.

### Gaps
- No stage record found for any undammed Maine lake or pond. USGS NWIS lake-stage sites in Maine are mostly regulated. A natural-outlet Maine lake gauge would settle this.
- No measured exposed-shore widths for Maine lakes.

## 6. Coarse woody habitat along Maine (northern) lakeshores: density, size, above and below water, use by turtles and fish

### Takeaway
I found no Maine or New England lake wood survey; all densities come from the northern Wisconsin / Upper Michigan lake district.
- **Undeveloped lakes:** average about 555 logs/km of shore (Christensen et al. 1996; lake means range from tens to about 640/km).
- **Developed (cabin) shores:** about 57/km.
- **Within a lake:** wood is very patchy (0 to 4,200 logs/km between sites). It follows the amount of downed wood in the adjacent forest and is lower on wetland shores.
- **Persistence:** wood lasts decades to centuries underwater.
- **Fish:** removing 73% of logs from an undeveloped lake basin nearly eliminated yellow perch and pushed largemouth bass onto terrestrial prey with slower growth.
- **Turtles:** painted turtles concentrate where partly submerged logs and rocks offer sunny basking near shore.

### Cited Findings
- **Christensen et al. (1996), Ecol. Appl.** [WI] — [abstract via urbanforestrysouth.org](https://urbanforestrysouth.org/resources/literature/impacts-of-lakeshore-residential-development-on-coarse-woody-debris-in-north-temperate-lakes-957)
  - 16 north-temperate lakes.
  - Undeveloped lakes averaged 555 logs/km of shoreline. In developed lakes, forested sites averaged 379/km and cabin sites 57/km.
  - CWD density correlated positively with riparian tree density and negatively with cabin density.
- **Literature ranges** [WI/other]: undeveloped lakes 42 logs/km (Marburg et al. 2009) to 638 logs/km (Christensen et al. 1996); developed lakes 17–128 logs/km. CWD is defined there as ≥10 cm diameter and ≥1 m long — [Hydrobiologia 2015, s10750-015-2502-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-015-2502-z) (search summary)
- **Marburg, Turner & Kratz (2006), J. Ecology 94:558–568** [WI] — [PDF via WDNR](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_NatVarCoarseWoodinLakes.pdf)
  - Sampling: 45 Vilas County lakes. Wood was counted on 50 m line transects along the 0.5 m depth contour: pieces ≥5 cm in diameter where they cross the line and ≥150 cm long.
  - Lake-level densities:
    - Table 1, as best I can align it from the PDF text: median littoral wood about 57.5 pieces/km at ≥5 cm (range 0–712.5) and 22.5/km at ≥10 cm (0–372.5) across the full development gradient.
    - Lakes with >9 buildings/km never had more than 200 pieces/km.
    - Low-development lakes had higher medians.
  - Site-level patchiness: many sites had no wood; where wood occurred, density ranged from 20 to 4,200 logs/km.
  - Predictors:
    - The best predictor among lakes was the density of riparian (on-land) coarse wood.
    - In low-development lakes, littoral wood fell with the proportion of shoreline in wetlands (partial R² 0.21).
    - Within lakes, wind/wave exposure, riparian wood, land use and beaver-chewed wood entered the best models.
    - Live tree density did not predict littoral wood.
  - History:
    - Beaver extirpation in the 18th century probably reduced wood, and clearcutting around 1900 gave a temporary pulse.
    - Wood can last centuries in the littoral zone. In an Ontario lake, large white pine dated from the logging era and none had entered in the previous 100 years (Guyette & Cole 1999).
  - Median riparian live trees ≥5 cm d.b.h. were about 1,038 per ha.
- **Marburg (2006 dissertation)** [WI]: resurveys of 4 lakes first surveyed in 1996 showed net change of −1.1 to +1.9 logs/km/yr, with gains where riparian forests were maturing — [Turner lab, Marburg 2006](https://turnerlab.ibio.wisc.edu/marburg-a-e-2006-spatial-and-temporal-patterns-of-riparian-land-cover-forests-and-littoral-coarse-wood-in-the-northern-highlands-lake-district-wisconsin-usa-phd-dissertation-university-of-wis/) (search summary)
- **Sass et al. (2006), Fisheries 31(7):321–330, whole-lake CWH removal** [WI] — [PDF via WDNR](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_WholeLakeWoodRemovalSass.pdf)
  - Site: Little Rock Lake, an 18 ha undeveloped seepage lake.
  - Before removal, the treatment basin had 475 large logs (>10 cm) per km of shoreline; the reference basin had 344/km.
  - In July–August 2002 logs and limbs were removed to 2 m depth, cutting density to 128/km (−73%). This is comparable to lakes with 2–8 buildings/km.
  - Afterwards, largemouth bass ate less fish and more terrestrial prey and grew more slowly. Yellow perch fell to extremely low densities.
- **Painted turtles** [NE]:
  - Densest populations are in shallow, weedy, mud-bottomed habitats with many basking sites; almost every pond, lake and beaver flowage holds a few — [MassWildlife, painted turtles](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/learn-about-painted-turtles)
  - Often seen basking in groups on partly submerged logs or rocks — [NH Fish & Game](https://www.wildlife.nh.gov/wildlife-and-habitat/species-occurring-nh/eastern-painted-turtle)
  - Removing logs, rocks and screening shoreline vegetation is listed as a threat — [NH LAKES](https://nhlakes.org/wp-content/uploads/5-Lake-Ecology-Native-Species-Painted-Turtles-NH-LAKES.pdf)
  - Habitat-restoration guidance [other, BC]: place basking logs perpendicular to shore, anchored at the shore end and angled upward, so turtles have a range of heights as water levels change. Turtles avoid floating boards far from shore — [Enhancement of Basking Sites](https://ncrcd.specialdistrict.org/files/48ecffa34/Enhancement_of_Basking_Sites.pdf) (search summary)
  - In a Minnesota lake, turtles basked off cooler, shallower water close to shore [other] — [CSB/SJU 2018](https://digitalcommons.csbsju.edu/ur_cscday/96) (search summary)

### Inferences
**Undeveloped spruce–fir Maine lakeshore (Lower Hot Brook-like)**

| Shore | Logs ≥10 cm per km | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Forested shores | 300–600 | — |
| Windward boulder shores | lower | — |
| Fen / marsh shores | ≤ about 100 | Marburg's wetland effect |
| Camp shore of Upper Hot Brook | 50–150 | — |

Placement:
- Most pieces sit in under 2 m of water within about 10 m of shore: whole fallen spruce, fir, cedar and pine with root wads on shore, sloping into the water.
- About a third to half of the pieces have a portion above water. This fraction is not sourced; it comes from the geometry of fallen trees.

Turtle basking:
- 1–3 good basking logs per 100 m of sheltered, sunny, weedy shore is a plausible density for painted turtles.
- Basking peaks in spring and on cool mornings.

Fish: wood should give cover and spawning or foraging structure for perch (egg strands; not sourced here), bass, pumpkinseed and bullhead, and refuge from pickerel and loon predation.

**Stream wood:** see §3.

### Gaps
- No Maine or New England lake coarse-wood survey (logs/km), and no data on the fraction of pieces emergent vs submerged, piece length or branchiness.
- The Marburg (2006) Table 1 values were read from text extraction with column misalignment. The medians of 57.5 and 22.5 pieces/km are my best alignment and should be checked against the original.
- No published basking-site density for painted turtles in Maine.

## 7. Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake shorelines: development, shore type, wetlands, vegetation

### Takeaway
- **Both lakes:** shallow, unstratified warmwater lakes ringed by spruce–fir forest, with shores "mostly rocky" and some sand and mud, and mostly muddy bottoms.
- **Upper Hot Brook Lake** (≈890–912 acres, max 18 ft):
  - wetlands on its south and west sides;
  - a large sandy beach in the northeast;
  - camps along the south shore;
  - an unimproved boat launch at the south end off Route 169.
- **Lower Hot Brook Lake** (≈684–713 acres, max 13 ft):
  - wetlands on its north and east sides;
  - no camps (per the IF&W revision of 2004);
  - reachable only through the ~200 m rocky, non-navigable connecting stream.

### Cited Findings
**Upper Hot Brook Lake (MDIFW lake survey map)** [ME] — [MDIFW Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf)
- Location: Danforth and T8 R4 NBPP, Washington County.
- Area 912 acres; maximum depth 18 ft; surface 74 °F at survey.
- Shallow; does not stratify in summer. Several small tributaries, none with coldwater habitat.

- Shore and setting:
  - Mostly surrounded by spruce–fir forest, with some wetlands on the south and west sides.
  - Shoreline mostly rocky, with some sand, a large sandy beach in the NE part of the lake, and mud. The bottom is mostly muddy.
  - Separated from Lower Hot Brook Lake by a short (1/8 mi) non-navigable connecting stream.
  - Camp development along the south shore; unimproved boat launch at the south end from Rt. 169.

**Lower Hot Brook Lake (MDIFW lake survey map)** [ME] — [MDIFW Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf)
- Location: Danforth and T8 R4 NBPP, Washington County / Bancroft, Aroostook County.
- Area 713 acres; maximum depth 13 ft. Shallow and unstratified.
- Shore and setting:
  - Surrounded by spruce–fir forest, with some wetlands on the north and east sides.
  - Shoreline mostly rocky with some sand and mud; bottom mostly muddy.
  - No camps.
- Tributaries and outlet:
  - The major tributary is the inlet from the upper lake. It is rocky and hard-bottomed, with no coldwater habitat.
  - The outlet flows to Baskahegan Stream.
- Access: the only access is through the inlet stream, which cannot be run by motorboat.
- Surveyed August 1949; revised 1965 and 2004.

### Inferences
- **Shoreline development factor** (my calculation, D_L = P / 2√(πA), from Lakes of Maine area and perimeter): Upper ≈ 2.2, Lower ≈ 1.8. Both are moderately irregular glacial outlines with several points and coves, not round basins.

**Plan view for the 2.5-D shore (Hot Brook-inspired)**
- *Two-lake layout:* Upper lake to the south and Lower lake to the north, about 2 mi apart centre to centre, joined at Upper's north end by a ~200 m rocky alder-lined brook.
  - Upper's outlet end is not stated by IF&W. Inferred from "Lower Hot Brook is 2.07 mi north".
  - Upper's wetlands are on its S and W; Lower's on its N and E.
  - So the connecting-stream mouths are on rocky or mixed shores, and the large fen and marsh pockets are at the far ends.
- *Upper Hot Brook Lake shoreline composition:*
  - mostly boulder and cobble on till, with rocky points;
  - one long sand beach in the NE;
  - sedge/sweetgale fen and alder–red maple swamp in the S and W coves, fronted by pickerelweed and water-lily beds over muck;
  - a row of camps (cleared lawns, docks, little wood in the water) along the south shore.
- *Lower Hot Brook Lake shoreline composition:*
  - an undeveloped spruce–fir rim with fallen-tree coarse wood;
  - fen and marsh wetlands in the N and E coves, including the outlet to Baskahegan Stream, likely with bulrush beds at inlet and outlet per MNAP.
- *Applying this to the sim:*
  - The lake basin's two shores should be rocky till with boulders.
  - One end of a basin can hold a sweetgale and pickerelweed cove.
  - The pond suits a fen or marsh margin all round (shallow 4 m saucer, muck).
  - Both stream banks: alder floodplain over cobble.
- *Mussels:* the project's own Hot Brook field notes (user observation) report abundant mussels and 3–4 ft stream pools. These are not published values.

### Gaps
