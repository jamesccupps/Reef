# Turtles, frogs and other shore herps of Maine lakes and ponds: how they use the shore and move between land and water through the year

Scope: painted turtle, snapping turtle, and the rare Maine turtles; green frog, bullfrog and mink frog; the vernal-pool and catchment amphibians; Maine's water and garter snakes; and a seasonal calendar for eastern Maine. Hot Brook (Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake) is in Washington County, eastern Maine. Its nearest long ice record is West Grand Lake, with mean ice-out on April 28 (1878–2008) and April 24 (1979–2008) ([USGS OFR 2010-1214](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2010/1214/pdf/ofr2010-1214.pdf), as cited in `docs/reports/Maine lakes ponds and streams.md`).

Regional priority follows the brief: Maine first, then New England, then the northern Great Lakes and southern Canada, with the region flagged on each value. Sim year-fractions (yf) use yf 0 = March 1.

## 1. Painted turtle basking: sites, timing, temperatures, bout length, share of the day

### Takeaway
Painted turtles bask out of the water on logs, fallen trees, rocks and floating root or tuber mats, often in groups. They bask in the morning and on and off through the day, alternating with foraging bouts. In northern Ontario most bouts last under about 60–100 min. A secondary source gives two or three basking and foraging cycles a day of about 2 h each in summer. Basking is most likely when air is much warmer than water: in spring, in sun, and in the morning. It drops in the hottest midday periods and on overcast days. No source gives a height above water or a measured share of the day for painted turtles. The nearest measured analogue is juvenile northern map turtles in Ontario, which bask at least 46% of daylight hours.

### Cited Findings
- **Habitat (Maine).** MDIFW gives the painted turtle's habitat as "permanent water with submerged vegetation and plentiful basking areas", including shallow ponds, marshes, bogs and slow streams. It is called the "sun turtle" because it spends much time basking on logs. Status: common and secure, statewide except northwestern Maine. Carapace about 4.5–7 in (11–18 cm). — [MDIFW painted turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/painted-turtle.html)
- **Basking sites (New Brunswick, the closest Canadian data to Hot Brook).** At Grand Lake, NB, the highest abundances are where there are "abundant basking areas" such as lily tubers, fallen trees and logs (Gillingwater pers. comm. 2015). Basking is gregarious, "often in groups, under sunny conditions". — [COSEWIC 2018, Midland & Eastern Painted Turtle](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Visible basking density (Ontario).** Christie (1997) counted 178 basking Midland painted turtles on a 5-km paddling transect in Prince Edward County, Ontario: 35.6 turtles per km of shore. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Daily timing.**
  - Basking occurs in the morning and throughout the day, interrupted by foraging bouts (Mallet 1975; Lefevre & Brooks 1995; Edwards & Blouin-Demer 2007; Rowe & Dalgarn 2009). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - In Ohio, painted turtles bask mostly in the morning in spring. In summer the morning peak is weaker, and in autumn the peak moves nearer midday. — [Nocturnal basking in freshwater turtles: a global assessment (ScienceDirect), via search summary](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2351989423000793)
- **Thermal targets (northern Michigan marsh, C. p. marginata).** The lab-set thermoregulatory range was 25–31 °C. Thermal quality was highest in late morning and late afternoon. Midday body-temperature spikes come from aerial basking. — [Daily and annual patterns of thermoregulation in painted turtles, northern Michigan (ScienceDirect abstract, via search summary)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306456513001423)
- **Air–water temperature trigger (northern Ontario).** Schwarzkopf & Brooks found turtles most likely to bask when the difference between air and water temperature was greatest, agreeing with Boyer (1965). The date is given as 1985 in one citation and 1995 in another. — [search summary citing Schwarzkopf & Brooks](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241721610_Seasonal_temperature_variation_in_the_painted_turtle_Chrysemys_picta)
- **Activity threshold.** Feeding and breeding begin in spring once turtles can raise body temperature above 15–20 °C (Sexton 1959a; Ernst 1971b). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Seasonal pattern (North Carolina, 34 turtles with micro-dataloggers).** More basking occurred in February–March, when basking raised shell temperature 5–16 °C above the maximum water temperature. In summer, shell temperatures reached similar values without leaving the water. This is a southern population. — [Seasonal temperature variation in the painted turtle, Herpetologica 60(3)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241721610_Seasonal_temperature_variation_in_the_painted_turtle_Chrysemys_picta)
- **Hot periods (Pennsylvania).** Few turtles basked during the hottest periods at a pond where air reached 42 °C (Ernst 1972, "Temperature-activity relationship in the painted turtle"). — [search summary of Ernst 1972](https://bcreptilesandamphibians.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/ernst_1972.pdf). The PDF is a scanned image and its numbers could not be read.
- **Bout length (northern Ontario).** Most painted turtle basking bouts last under about 60–100 min (Schwarzkopf & Brooks 1985; Lefevre & Brooks 1995; Carrière et al. 2008). For comparison, a camera study of an Australian river turtle found diurnal bouts with a mean of 19 min and a median of 10 min (range 2–195 min). — [McKnight et al. 2021, Ecology & Evolution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8366836/)
- **Cycles per day (Adirondack natural-history summary, secondary).**
  - In summer, two or three basking/foraging cycles a day, with a typical basking period of about 2 h.
  - Basking mostly at 0–25% cloud cover; it stops on cloudy days.
  - The same page says water at 59–64 °F (15–18 °C) triggers emergence from hibernation "typically in March". That conflicts with northern ice-out timing, so treat it with caution.
  - — [Wild Adirondacks, painted turtle](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)
- **Sex and season (Algonquin and other Canadian studies).** Females bask more often and longer before nesting (Krawchuk & Brooks 1998) and during autumn follicle growth (Carrière et al. 2008). In midsummer the sexes bask equally (Lefevre & Brooks 1995). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Disturbance.** Boat traffic curtails basking (Moore & Seigel 2006; Bulté et al. 2010). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Competition for basking sites.** Aggression among eastern painted turtles while basking was most frequent at 09:00–10:00, at water temperatures of 30–32 °C (Lovich 1988, Herpetologica). — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/243654705_Aggressive_basking_behavior_in_eastern_painted_turtles_Chrysemys_picta_picta)
- **Offshore basking (Minnesota, preliminary student study).** Painted turtles basked an average of 12.64 m from shore, and basked where water was cooler. — [CSB/SJU student abstract](https://digitalcommons.csbsju.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1096&context=ur_cscday)
- **Closest measured share of the day (Ontario, juvenile northern map turtles).** Implanted loggers showed at least 46.0% of daylight hours spent basking. Energetically optimal basking would be 73.8% of daylight. This is a related emydid, not a painted turtle. — [QUBS project page](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/estimating-the-energetic-significance-of-basking-behaviour-in-a-temperate-zone-turtle); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232694024_Estimating_the_energetic_significance_of_basking_behaviour_in_a_temperate-zone_turtle)

### Inferences
- **Basking behaviour.** Sun, plus air warmer than water, should drive basking: strongest in spring after ice-out, on sunny mornings, and for gravid females in late May–June and again in autumn. Basking should be suppressed on overcast days and in the hottest midday hours of late July. Turtles should return to the water to forage between bouts and bask again.
- **Timing in sim terms.** With 2–3 bouts a day of up to about 1–2 h each, a sunny summer day gives roughly 3–6 h of aerial basking. That is about 20–40% of daylight, consistent with the map-turtle measurement of ≥46% for juveniles. On the sim's 60-s diel clock (1 h = 2.5 s), a bout is about 2.5–5 s. On the physiology clock (1 day = BIOE.DAY ≈ 4.9 s), it is about 0.2–0.4 s.
- **What the sim does now.** `TURTLE.BASK` is [8, 20] s "at the surface". That is surface floating, not haul-out basking. The real behaviour is haul-out onto logs, mats and banks, often in groups. The behaviour also includes floating at the surface.
- **Sites to model.**
  - Shoreline logs and fallen trees ("deadfalls").
  - Rocks.
  - Floating lily-tuber and root mats.
  - Shallow bank edges.
  - Several turtles share one log, and larger ones may displace others.
  - Offshore logs are used too, not only the shore edge.

### Gaps
- **Height above water.** No source gives how high above the water painted turtles bask, or the slope of the logs they use. I found no quantitative source on basking height.
- **Direct painted-turtle data.** I found no direct measurement of daily basking hours or share of daylight for painted turtles in Maine or New England. The primary papers are Krawchuk & Brooks 1998 (Herpetologica 54:112–121), Lefevre & Brooks 1995, Edwards & Blouin-Demer 2007 (Can. J. Zool., Ottawa region) and Carrière et al. 2008. Their full texts were not accessible here.
- **Mean bout duration.** No mean bout duration for painted turtles was found; only "most bouts <60–100 min".

## 2. Painted turtle diet, density, home range, survival and hibernation

### Takeaway
Painted turtles are omnivores. Young are mostly carnivorous and adults mostly herbivorous, though northern populations are said to be more carnivorous. A 1941 New York sample was about half plant and half animal.

Densities on the Canadian Shield and in New Brunswick are low, about 2.6–16 per ha of water. Small productive ponds reach about 100 per ha, and rich southern marshes several hundred per ha. Adult survival is about 97–98% a year, maturity takes about 9–11 years, and lifespans exceed 50–60 years.

Adults hibernate in shallow water, on or in the bottom sediment, choosing water at about 0–1 °C. They stay submerged for at least about 143 days in central Ontario.

### Cited Findings
- **Diet (Maine).** Omnivorous: aquatic insects, snails, crayfish, vegetation and carrion. — [MDIFW painted turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/painted-turtle.html)
- **Diet by age.**
  - Young are mainly carnivorous and adults mainly herbivorous (Ernst & Lovich 2009). Ephemeral ponds supply insect larvae as high-quality food for juveniles (Moldowan et al. 2015).
  - Fingernail-clam shells appear in spring feces in Algonquin Park.
  - — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Diet in Algonquin Park.** Adult painted turtles foraged on land for odonate (dragonfly and damselfly) larvae, probably emerging larvae at the shoreline. Juveniles ate mosquito and amphibian larvae in ephemeral pools (Canadian Field-Naturalist 129(4):403–408, field data 2009–2014). — [Canadian Field-Naturalist, Moldowan et al.](https://canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/index.php/cfn/article/view/1764)
- **Northern populations.** Described as more carnivorous, which lets them grow faster in a short season. This is a general statement, not a measurement. — [Toronto Zoo](https://www.torontozoo.com/animals/Midland%20painted%20turtle)
- **Stomach study (New York, 1941).** 76 adult stomachs were about evenly split between plant and animal material. — [Wild Adirondacks](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)
- **Densities** (turtles per ha of wetland or water) — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html):
  - **New Brunswick, Rockwood Park:** 7.5–7.8 (7.4–7.7 ha; Browne pers. comm. 2016), also given as 6.5–10 (Browne & Sullivan 2017). This is the Eastern painted turtle and the closest data to eastern Maine.
  - **Canadian Shield (Ontario and Québec):** 2.6–15.7. Examples:
    - Algonquin Mew Lake 2.6.
    - Whitefish Lake 10.3.
    - West Rose Lake 9.77.
    - Wolf Howl Pond East 15.7.
  - **Small productive Algonquin pond:** Wolf Howl Pond (1.9 ha) 97–109 (Samson 2003; Keevil).
  - **Québec:**
    - Curtis Pond 37.1.
    - Outaouais 9.9–36.4.
    - Cherry River marshes 40.
    - StoneyCroft Pond (0.41 ha) 276–289.
  - **Great Lakes and St Lawrence region:** typical 20–40 (range 9.9–289).
  - **Michigan:** E.S. George Reserve 25.5–134.4; Sherriff's Marsh 576–828.
  - **Pennsylvania:** 239.
  - **Long Island, NY:** 136.8.
- **Adult-to-juvenile ratio.**
  - New Brunswick 1.03:1 (Browne & Sullivan 2017).
  - New Hampshire 0.81 ± 0.12 adults per juvenile (Marchand & Litvaitis 2004).
  - Québec 2.6:1; New York 5:1; Pennsylvania 4:1.
  - — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Home range and movement (Beaver Island, Michigan).** Mean home range is about 1.8 ha for females and 2.9 ha for males (overall 1.2–2.1 ha). Daily aquatic movements run 0–300 m, with means of about 60–150 m. Two Algonquin males moved at least 13.5 km through water over 8–11 years. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Survival and age** — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html):
  - Adult annual survival is 97–98%; Algonquin long-term data give about 98%.
  - Survival from age 1 to maturity is 30.3% in Algonquin (Samson 2003).
  - Age at maturity is 11.2 years in Algonquin (Samson 2003) and 9 years in Midwood et al. 2015.
  - Longevity exceeds 60 years in the wild, and at least 50 in Algonquin.
  - Generation time is 29–44 years.
  - Life history of the Eastern subspecies in Canada has not been studied; Midland values are used.
- **Maturity in New York (secondary).** Males mature at about age 4, females at 6–7, with "maturity later in northern populations". Some individuals live 30–40 years. — [Wild Adirondacks](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)
- **Predators of adults.** American mink, river otter, canids (coyotes, dogs), raccoons and corvids. Great blue heron, bald eagle, northern water snake and American bullfrog are also listed among predators, mainly of young. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Hibernation.**
  - Maine: hibernates in muddy bottom sediments of lakes and ponds. — [MDIFW](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/painted-turtle.html)
  - Adults overwinter in shallow water, on the substrate surface or buried. At southern Ontario sites (Taylor & Nol 1989): water depth 0.32 ± 0.08 m, sediment depth 0.79 ± 0.15 m, sediment temperature 4.1 ± 0.75 °C. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - In winter, turtles select temperatures near 0–1 °C. Submergence lasted at least 143 days in central Ontario (Rollinson et al. 2008) and 56–117 days (mean 92) in southern Michigan (Crawford 1991). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - Painted and snapping turtles can survive several months submerged in anoxic water. Turtle body fluids freeze at about −0.6 °C, so hibernacula must stay above 0 °C. — [search summary citing Brown & Brooks 1994 and the Piczak & Chow-Fraser 2019 snapping-turtle paper](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/bitstream/11375/30745/2/Piczak-and-Chow-Fraser-2019-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Density and numbers.** For an eastern Maine lake or pond, about 5–15 painted turtles per ha of shallow, weedy water is a defensible central value, based on New Brunswick (7.5–10/ha) and the Shield lakes (2.6–15.7/ha). The 4-m saucer pond could justify the high end, or up to tens per ha if it is weedy and productive (Wolf Howl Pond: about 100/ha in 1.9 ha). The sim's area scaling makes this a small count of agents, and that is realistic.
- **Life history in the sim.**
  - `TURTLE.MATURE` = 6 yr is low for a northern population (Algonquin 11.2 yr). About 9–11 years fits eastern Maine better.
  - `LIFE` = 30 yr is low: adults survive about 97–98% a year and some live past 50–60.
  - With about 98% adult survival, the population hardly turns over within a sim run. Recruitment should be a trickle, about 30% of yearlings reaching maturity, after heavy nest and hatchling losses.
- **Hibernation sites.** Painted turtles hibernate in the SHALLOWS: about 0.3 m water over soft sediment, or buried up to about 0.8 m into it. They pick the coldest unfrozen water (0–1 °C), not the deep hole. The current sim description ("buried in the mud of the shallows") matches.
- **Activity threshold.** `ACTIVE_T` = 10 °C is a reasonable torpor threshold. Feeding should ramp in only once turtles can bask body temperature above 15–20 °C. Spring activity is therefore mostly basking, with feeding lagging until water warms.

### Gaps
- No Maine or New England painted turtle density estimate was found. The New Hampshire study (Marchand & Litvaitis 2004) reports age structure but its densities were not accessible.
- No percent-by-volume diet study from Maine or the Canadian Shield was found. The only quantified figure is the NY 1941 "about half plant".

## 3. Painted turtle nesting and hatchlings: timing, distance from water, substrate, clutch, incubation, sex by temperature, overwintering in the nest, nest predation

### Takeaway
Females nest from late May to early July, peaking in mid-June, mostly in the afternoon and early evening. Nests are dug in open, sloped, often south-facing sand, loam or gravel. Most are close to water: the Ontario median is 11 m, but the mean is 78 m and the tail runs past 1 km.

Clutches in Nova Scotia average about 8–9 eggs. Incubation takes 65–80 days, or 83–97 days in Algonquin. Cool nests (≤26 °C) give males and warm nests (≥29 °C) give females.

In the north most hatchlings do not leave in autumn. They overwinter in the nest, surviving brief freezing to about −4 °C or supercooling, and emerge the next spring. In New Hampshire about 1 in 6 hatchling observations were autumn emergences.

Nest predation, mostly by raccoons and red foxes, ranges from about 17% to over 90%, and is often concentrated in the first nights after laying.

### Cited Findings
- **Nesting season and time of day.** Nesting spans about 20–40 days, typically late May to early July, peaking in mid-June (Browne 2003; Christens & Bider 1987; Congdon & Gatten 1989). It occurs mainly in the afternoon and early evening, though some morning nesting is recorded. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Distance from water.**
  - Ontario: mean 78 ± 243 m, median 11 m, minimum 1 m, maximum 1,233 m (Steen et al. 2012).
  - Québec: about 16 m to more than 600 m from the home pond (Christens & Bider 1987).
  - Overland nesting trips of 100–200 m are common, 400–600 m occasional, and over 1 km uncommon.
  - — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - NatureServe reports nests in open areas up to several hundred metres from water, with travel up to 600 m. — [NatureServe, via search summary](https://explorer.natureserve.org/Taxon/ELEMENT_GLOBAL.2.637723/Chrysemys_picta)
- **Substrate and fidelity.** Preferred substrate is sand, loam, clay and/or gravel, on sloped sites often facing south (Christens & Bider 1987; Riley et al. 2014b). Females often nest 10 cm to 10 m from previous nests. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Clutch size** (mean, with range) — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html):
  - Nova Scotia 8.6 (5–11) (Powell 1967); 6–11 (NSM 2015). These are Eastern painted turtles, the nearest to Maine.
  - Ste-Anne-de-Bellevue, QC: 9.2 (5–12).
  - Ontario: 6.8–7.25.
  - Michigan: 4.7–6.6.
- **Clutches per year.**
  - Midland and Western painted turtles can lay two clutches a year. — [Turtle Guardians, citing COSEWIC](https://www.turtleguardians.com/2023/03/reproductive-ecology-of-freshwater-turtles-in-ontario/)
  - Earlier nesting raises the chance of a second clutch (Krawchuk & Brooks 1998, via search summary). — [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.808621/full)
  - Northern populations lay larger clutches but fewer clutches a year. — [Wild Adirondacks](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)
- **Incubation.** About 65–80 days (Ernst 1971b), and 83–97 days in Algonquin Park (Riley & Litzgus 2013). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Sex by temperature.** Incubation at ≤26 °C gives males, ≥29 °C gives females, and temperatures between give a mix (Bull & Vogt 1981; Schwarzkopf & Brooks 1987). A rise of less than 2 °C can skew the sex ratio (Janzen 1994). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Hatch success without predation.** About 92% in Québec (Christens & Bider 1987) and 69–79% in Algonquin (Riley & Litzgus 2013). Overall nest survival is generally about 2–30% (Wilbur 1975b; Tinkle et al. 1981). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Overwintering in the nest, Canada.**
  - Share of hatchlings that overwinter in the nest: 100% in Québec (Christens & Bider 1987); 56–92% in central Ontario (Riley et al. 2014b); 100% in Storey et al. 1988.
  - Overwinter success in Algonquin nests: 67% ± 8%.
  - Overwinter mortality: 0–80% (mean 19% over 5 years; Breitenbach et al. 1984); up to 12% in Michigan (Tinkle et al. 1981).
  - Some hatchlings delay emergence until June or July.
  - About 40% of hatchlings seek cover in grass, debris or litter before reaching water (Riley et al. 2014b).
  - — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Overwintering in the nest, New England (south-central New Hampshire, 1988–1994).**
  - Of 42 hatchling observations, 7 were autumn emergences, from Aug 10–15 to Sep 26. One nest produced hatchlings in both seasons.
  - Overwinter survival in screened nests varied by year:
    - 1988–89: 100% mortality in 6 nests.
    - 1990–91: 74% survival of 34 hatchlings in 8 nests.
    - 1993–94: 53% mortality, 16 of 30.
  - — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007, Northeastern Naturalist 14(2):307–310](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)
- **Freezing physiology (Ottawa, C. p. marginata).**
  - Mean supercooling point −3.28 ± 0.24 °C.
  - Hatchlings survived 24 h at −4 °C with 53.4% of body water frozen.
  - Field nests reached −6 to −8 °C.
  - Freezing at −10.9 °C (about 67% ice) was lethal.
  - — [Storey et al. 1988, PNAS 85:8350](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC282428)
- **Supercooling (North Dakota hatchlings).** Survival fell with colder exposure:
  - 30 of 32 at −2.5 °C.
  - 24 of 32 at −4.5 °C.
  - 14 of 32 at −6.5 °C.
  - 7 of 32 at −10.5 °C.
  - The authors concluded these northern hatchlings rely mainly on staying unfrozen (supercooled). Whether freezing or supercooling matters more is still debated. — [PubMed 9361141, via search summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9361141). The record itself could not be fetched, so the citation to this specific PubMed ID is unverified.
- **Nest predation rates.**
  - **Québec:** raccoons were the top predator, and 85.7% of nests were destroyed the same night they were laid (Christens & Bider 1987).
  - **Southeast Michigan:** 20% of nests failed to predation within 48 h, and nest survival was about 80% (Tinkle, Congdon & Rosen 1981).
  - **Beaver Island, Michigan:** 17.4% predation, independent of distance from water.
  - **Algonquin:** 86% of painted-turtle nest predation came more than a week after laying.
  - **Point Pelee:** 33–100%.
  - **Rondeau:** 87–98%.
  - **Québec, Mont-Orford:** 75%.
  - **A Québec gravel pit (North Missisquoi):** 94%.
  - — [search summary of COSEWIC and the Beaver Island paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232679144_Nest_Placement_Nest-site_Fidelity_and_Nesting_Movements_in_Midland_Painted_Turtles_Chrysemys_picta_marginata_on_Beaver_Island_Michigan); [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - Raccoons and red foxes are the major nest predators (Ernst & Lovich 2009). Raccoon density at Point Pelee was 24.6–28.2 per km². A population tolerated 70% nest predation, but 90% would cause decline (Browne 2003). — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - Cryptic painted-turtle nests were taken less often than the more detectable snapping-turtle nests. — [Oecologia 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00442-011-2158-y)

### Inferences
- **Nesting on the new banks.** In mid-June (yf ≈ 0.28–0.32), gravid females should bask more, then leave the water in the afternoon or evening. They climb onto an open, sunny, sandy or gravelly, south-facing bank, usually within about 10–20 m of the water, sometimes much farther. Each digs one nest of about 7–10 eggs. A minority lay a second clutch.
- **Off-screen nesting.** In a 2-D slice, a nest more than about 20 m inland can be "off-screen": the female walks up the bank, disappears, and returns hours later.
- **Hatchling timing.** The current sim has hatchlings entering the lake at yf 0.42–0.50 (early to late August). That fits only the autumn-emerging minority (about 15–20% in New Hampshire). The northern norm is spring emergence: April–May after the ground thaws, with stragglers to June or July. Spring hatchlings walk from the bank to the water.
- **A nest-and-winter mechanism.** A nest state on land would carry these stages:
  1. Predation, often in the first night or two, about 20–90%.
  2. Incubation by degree-days, about 80–95 days in Maine.
  3. Sex by nest temperature.
  4. Winter mortality, about 20–50% in a typical year and near total in a bad one (New Hampshire 1988–89).
  5. Spring emergence.
- **Expected yield.** A nest of 8–9 eggs that escapes predation yields about 4–6 spring hatchlings. Across all nests, expected yield is about 1–3 hatchlings per female per year. The current `CLUTCH` = 3 "hatchlings that reach the water" is about right as an average.

### Gaps
- No Maine-specific painted turtle nesting data were found: dates, distances or predation rates. The 1999 and third editions of *Maine Amphibians and Reptiles* (Hunter et al.) cover it, but the text was not accessible.
- No single pivotal temperature for 1:1 sex ratio was found, only the ≤26 / ≥29 °C bounds.
- I found no source separating the predator mix in Maine (raccoon, skunk, fox, crow).

## 4. Snapping turtle in Maine: density, diet, nesting, effect on fish, frogs and ducklings

### Takeaway
Snapping turtles are common and secure across all regions of Maine. Northern densities are low: 1.5–2.4 adults per ha in Algonquin Park, against up to about 60–70 per ha in eutrophic ponds. They are slow-growing and long-lived: adults survive 93–97% a year and northern turtles mature at 12–19 years.

They are omnivores, eating fish (often slow or dead), crayfish, invertebrates, frogs, birds, mammals, carrion and a large share of aquatic plants. Estimates of the plant share range from about one-third to about two-thirds.

Females nest in June in sand or gravel, including road shoulders, up to about 3 km (1.9 mi) from water. Hatching comes about 85 days later. In the north, hatchlings emerge in September; overwintering in the nest almost always fails. Nest loss is heavy: 11–94% a year, and often only about 14% of clutches produce emerging hatchlings. Measured impact on ducklings and game fish is small.

### Cited Findings
- **Maine status and basics.**
  - Common and secure; found in the southern, central, eastern and northern regions.
  - Habitat: permanent waters, including ponds, lakes, rivers, bogs and marshes.
  - Diet: fish, crayfish, aquatic invertebrates, reptiles, birds, mammals and plants.
  - Eggs are laid in June in sandy soil, including gravel road shoulders. Nests can be up to 1.9 mi (3 km) from home ponds.
  - Hibernates in lake-bottom sediments. Carapace 8–14 in (20–36 cm).
  - — [MDIFW snapping turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/snapping-turtle.html)
- **Maine nesting detail (MDIFW biologist Derek Yorks).** Egg-laying can begin in late May and run into the first week of July; most is in June. Hatching comes about 85 days later. Typical nests hold about 25–45 eggs. — [Sun Journal, via search summary](https://new.sunjournal.com/?p=9255515)
- **Maine naturalist column (secondary).** Up to 90% of nests are taken by crows, foxes, mink, raccoons and skunks. It also says that in cold climates young often stay in the nest over winter and emerge in late spring. The primary northern data below contradict that claim. — [Sanford Springvale News, Maine Master Naturalist](https://sanfordspringvalenews.com/lucky-few-snapping-turtles-become-apex-predators/)
- **Density.**
  - Ranges reported: 1–75 adults per ha; northern populations overall about 1.5/ha (Obbard 1983).
  - Algonquin Park, Ontario: 1.5–2.4/ha (Brooks et al. 1988; Galbraith et al. 1988).
  - Hudson River tidal marshes: 16/ha (Kiviat 1980).
  - Eutrophic Ontario lake: 60–70/ha.
  - Ponds: Tennessee 59/ha, West Virginia 62.5/ha.
  - — [Tortoise Trust review](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm). Some of that page's citations are mis-numbered; values should be checked against the originals.
  - Virginia rivers (mark–recapture): 2.5–9.35/ha, varying with harvest. — [Virginia DWR status report](https://dwr.virginia.gov/wp-content/uploads/Status-of-Snapping-Turtles-in-Coastal-Plain-of-Virginia.pdf)
  - Nebraska lake: 2.32 adults and subadults per ha. Density is inversely related to wetland size across studies. — [Crescent Lake NWR study](https://journals.ku.edu/reptilesandamphibians/article/view/16122)
- **Diet.**
  - Animal Diversity Web lists carrion, invertebrates, fish, birds, small mammals, amphibians and "a surprisingly large amount of aquatic vegetation". — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Chelydra_serpentina/)
  - **Michigan stomach study (173 stomachs, 4,133 cc of food):** about one-third game fish and one-third plant matter, almost all leaves and petioles of aquatic plants. — [Michigan DNR Fisheries Research Report 553, via search summary; the PDF is a scanned image I could not read](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR0501-RR0600/RR0553.pdf)
  - **Tortoise Trust review:** about 65% aquatic vegetation, citing several sources. Snapping turtles take mainly slow, non-game fish and pose no hazard to game fish (Hammer 1972; Coulter 1957; Lagler & Applegate 1943). Feeding begins once water exceeds 16 °C (Graves & Anderson 1987). Adults eat roughly their own body weight a year (Kiviat 1980). — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
  - **Michigan versus Tortoise Trust:** the Michigan "one-third game fish" figure conflicts with the Tortoise Trust conclusion. The difference may be scavenged versus live fish, which the search summary does not separate.
  - **Algonquin Park observations:** adult snapping turtles ate aquatic vegetation and seeds, and juveniles ambushed or stalked mosquito larvae in ephemeral pools. — [Canadian Field-Naturalist 129(4)](https://canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/index.php/cfn/article/view/1764)
  - **National Biological Survey:** describes a broad diet in which waterfowl and game fish are infrequent items. — [NBS 1994 report](https://npshistory.com/publications/wildlife/nbs-rib/94-32.pdf)
- **Predator of frogs.** Snapping turtles take male bullfrogs defending mid-pond territories (Emlen 1976; Howard 1978a). — [Virginia Herpetological Society, bullfrog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php). Snapping turtles are also listed among green frog predators. — [ADW, green frog](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
- **Ducklings (South Dakota).**
  - Removing turtles did not change brood size or survival; only populations raised above twice natural density showed a small effect (Hammer 1972).
  - Weather affected brood survival far more than turtles did. Birds in the diet are "incidental and rare".
  - Densities above 0.5 per acre (1.3/ha) were once thought harmful to waterfowl, but cutting density below 0.1 per acre raised brood survival only minimally.
  - — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
- **Nesting movements (Ontario).** Overland paths are typically 500–1,625 m. Nests are found up to about 800 m from water. Females migrate 4–14 km one way through water to nest, at up to 1.7 km a day. — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
- **Clutch size, incubation and sex.**
  - Clutch: 11–87 eggs (means 28–49); northern mean 34 (Galbraith 1986).
  - Clutch mass is about 7% of female mass. About 72–85% of females lay each year.
  - Embryos develop only above 20 °C (Obbard 1983).
  - Sex is set by nest temperature, with a 50:50 ratio at 28 °C.
  - — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
  - New Hampshire Fish and Game gives 20–40 eggs, laid in soil banks or sand and gravel piles that may be far from water. — [NH Fish & Game, via search summary](https://wildlife.nh.gov/wildlife-and-habitat/species-occurring-nh/snapping-turtle)
- **Nest success.**
  - 11–94% of nests are destroyed by mammals each year, with high year-to-year variation; up to 90% can be lost, often on the first night.
  - About 14% of clutches (range 3–36%) produce emerging hatchlings each year (Obbard 1983; Hammer 1972).
  - Successful nests yield about 15 hatchlings, with 20–45% emergence success.
  - — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
  - In Algonquin, 83% of snapping-turtle nest predation came more than a week after laying. — [COSEWIC 2018, via search summary](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Hatchling emergence.**
  - **New Hampshire:** all 14 known emergence dates fell between Sep 5 and Sep 24, and no overwintering in the nest was recorded. — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)
  - **Ontario:** only 1 of 129 monitored clutches overwintered successfully in the nest (Obbard & Brooks 1981). — [Turtle Guardians remark, via search summary](https://www.turtleguardians.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Remark-Hatchling-SNTU-Overwintering-in-Natal-Nest-2.pdf)
  - Hatchlings overwintering in northern nests freeze to death (Obbard & Brooks 1981; Galbraith & Brooks 1987). — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
- **Survival and maturity.**
  - Adult annual survival 93–97%. Only about 1–1.8% of juveniles reach breeding age each year.
  - Northern maturity: males 12–19 years, females 11–19 years, with an average first nesting age of about 19. Nesting females average 34–40 years old.
  - A 10% rise in adult mortality halves adult numbers in about 15 years (Congdon et al. 1994).
  - — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
  - Ontario maturity 17–19 years. — [Turtle Guardians, citing COSEWIC 2016a and ECCC 2016](https://www.turtleguardians.com/2023/03/reproductive-ecology-of-freshwater-turtles-in-ontario/)
- **Overwintering needs.** Snapping turtles need hibernacula that protect against freezing, hide them from predators and give access to dissolved oxygen. Muddy substrate avoids freezing but can be hypoxic; sites above the sediment have more oxygen but may freeze (Brown & Brooks 1994). — [Piczak & Chow-Fraser 2019](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/bitstream/11375/30745/2/Piczak-and-Chow-Fraser-2019-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Role in the sim.** The snapping turtle is the one lake animal that can kill an adult painted turtle's competitors, adult frogs, and fish of most sizes. It is still a low-density, mostly sit-and-wait scavenging omnivore.
- **Numbers.** At about 1.5–2.4 per ha for a northern lake, the sim landscape would hold only a few snapping turtles; a single adult in the pond is realistic.
- **Behaviour to model.**
  - Ambush and stalk on the bottom; rarely basks out of water.
  - Scavenge carcasses.
  - Eat plants (to be confirmed with Maine data).
  - Take a frog, fish or duckling occasionally.
- **Nesting and hatchlings.** A June overland nesting walk is needed. Hatchlings should emerge in September (yf ≈ 0.52–0.57), not the following spring.
- **Turtle hatchlings as distinct cohorts.** Snapping turtles emerge in autumn and painted turtles mostly in spring, which gives two distinct hatchling cohorts.

### Gaps
- No Maine or New England snapping turtle density estimate was found. A Rhode Island study across 58 wetlands is collecting such data, but no figures were available. — [URI turtle study brief](https://web.uri.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2078/Turtle-Study-Brief-03.pdf)
- **Sex-determination pattern.** The pattern for snapping turtles, which may produce females at both low and high temperatures, is not documented in the sources found. Only "50:50 at 28 °C" is.
- **Diet by volume.** Live fish versus carrion shares are not separated in any accessible source.

## 5. Other Maine turtles at lakes, ponds and streams: which belong in a Hot Brook-type system

### Takeaway
Of Maine's other freshwater turtles, only the wood turtle plausibly belongs at Hot Brook, and only on the stream. It is uncommon statewide, uses slow sandy streams with deep pools and woody debris, and hibernates under undercut banks. The spotted turtle (southern and central Maine) and Blanding's turtle (York and Cumberland counties only) are outside eastern Washington County. Painted and snapping turtles are the lake and pond turtles.

### Cited Findings
- **Wood turtle.**
  - State Special Concern; uncommon, statewide. Carapace 5.5–10 in.
  - Habitat: slow-moving streams or rivers with sandy or silty bottoms, often with deep pools and woody debris. It is semi-terrestrial, using vegetated floodplains and adjacent forest.
  - Hibernates in small ponds or under undercut banks of slow streams and rivers.
  - Lays eggs in June–July in loose sand, such as streamside sandbars.
  - Omnivorous: leaves, grasses, berries, insects, earthworms, tadpoles, newborn mice. It "stomps" to bring up earthworms.
  - Matures at about 11–12 years.
  - — [MDIFW wood turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/wood-turtle.html)
  - In New Hampshire, wood turtle hatchlings emerged Aug 13–Sep 14, with no overwintering in the nest. — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)
- **Spotted turtle.**
  - State Threatened (listed in 1986); rare; southern and central regions only.
  - Habitat: slow streams, wet meadows, bogs, ponds, vernal pools and swamps, usually with sphagnum.
  - Inactive in summer, resting under forest leaf litter. Hibernates under hummocks or shrub roots in red maple swamps.
  - Nests within 400 ft of water. Visits vernal pools each year to eat breeding amphibians.
  - — [MDIFW spotted turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/spotted-turtle.html); [search summary of the MDIFW listing](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/endangered-threatened-species/reptiles-amphibians.html)
- **Blanding's turtle.** State Endangered; documented in Maine only in York County, or southern York and Cumberland counties. It is linked to the eastern New Hampshire and Massachusetts populations. — [MDIFW Blanding's turtle species assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/blandings-turtle-speciesassessment.pdf); [MDIFW Blanding's turtle](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/blandings-turtle.html)

### Inferences
- **Stream reach.** A wood turtle on the sim's stream would be a defensible rare addition. It forages on the banks and floodplain in summer, overwinters under an undercut bank in a pool, and nests on a sandbar in June. It is optional; the core shore herps are painted and snapping turtles.
- **Exclusions.** Spotted and Blanding's turtles should be excluded for an eastern Maine setting.

### Gaps
- No township-level confirmation was found of wood turtles in the Baskahegan/Hot Brook drainage. MARAP township maps (PDF) were not readable here.

## 6. Green frog, bullfrog and mink frog: where adults sit, foraging distance, diet, densities along the shore, breeding, tadpole duration, hibernation, predators

### Takeaway
**Green frogs** are the classic shore-edge frogs in Maine (statewide, common). Adults sit at the water's edge in shallow water among emergent plants, rarely more than about 1 m from water except on rainy nights. Breeding males space about 1–1.5 m apart in dense vegetation and 4–6 m apart on open shores. Home ranges are about 20–200 m². Breeding runs May–August in Maine. In the north, tadpoles overwinter once and metamorphose the next summer. Adults hibernate underwater on the bottom, or occasionally underground, from October to March.

**Bullfrogs** (statewide) are more aquatic. Large males hold floating territories 2–5 m across in open water. They breed later, mid-June to July, when air is above about 20 °C. Their tadpoles take about 2 years, up to 3 in Michigan and Nova Scotia. They hibernate on the pond bottom, unburied, in shallow water.

**Mink frogs** (Maine is the southern edge of their range, including the eastern region) are highly aquatic frogs of shallow dense vegetation. They breed late June to early August.

All three take mostly insects, many of them terrestrial or emerging aerial insects. Larger frogs also eat frogs, small fish and crayfish.

### Cited Findings
**Green frog**
- **Maine.** Statewide, common and secure; 2–4 in long. Mostly aquatic and rarely found far from water. Uses everything from lakes and large streams to small marshes, vernal pools and ditches. Tadpoles overwinter in permanent water. Adults overwinter underwater or underground. — [MDIFW green frog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/green-frog.html)
  - The same page says its eggs and tadpoles are "toxic and unpalatable" so fish largely avoid them. That phrasing may be carried over from bullfrog text; it should be verified.
- **Breeding.** Breeding runs from April through summer by latitude; May–August in Kentucky and Maine. Clutches hold about 1,000–7,000 eggs, scaling with female size. Some females near Ithaca, NY lay a second clutch of 1,000–1,500 (Wells 1976). — [VHS green frog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
- **Eggs.** Eggs hatch in 3–6 days. — [ADW green frog](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
- **Tadpoles.**
  - In the north, tadpoles typically overwinter one year and metamorphose the next spring or summer (Martof 1952; Vogt 1981). Near Ithaca they metamorphose late June to mid-July.
  - Tadpoles reach 60–80 mm total length before metamorphosis; metamorphs are 21–38 mm.
  - In Maine, tadpoles overwinter under silt and dead vegetation (Hunter et al. 1999).
  - — [VHS green frog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
  - Overwintering tadpoles remain active and probably feed in cold water. — [Wild Adirondacks green frog, via search summary](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-amphibians-green-frog-lithobates-clamitans.html)
  - Tadpoles feed mainly on diatoms and other algae. — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
- **Maturity and life span.** In Maine, females mature at 65–75 mm and males at 60–65 mm, about 1 year after metamorphosis (Hunter et al. 1999). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php). Wild life span is typically 5–6 years. — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
- **Where adults sit, and spacing.**
  - Males hold territories in shallow water with dense emergent vegetation. They are 1.0–1.5 m apart in dense vegetation and 4.0–6.0 m apart on open shorelines. Males stay on a territory for about 1 week on average (range under 1 to 7 weeks) (Wells 1977b).
  - Adults rarely go more than 1 m from water except on rainy nights (Minton 1972).
  - Home range is about 62 m² (Hamilton 1948); in Michigan about 20–200 m², averaging about 60 m² (Martof 1953).
  - — [VHS green frog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
  - Typical territory is 4–6 m², and males usually move no more than 50 m from their territory. — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
  - Adults spend most of their time near the shoreline and jump into deeper water when approached. — [Wild Adirondacks, via search summary](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-amphibians-green-frog-lithobates-clamitans.html)
- **Dispersal.** Subadults move 200 m to 4.8 km in their first season after metamorphosis (Schroeder 1968b). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
- **Shoreline development.** Adult green frog numbers were significantly lower on developed Wisconsin lakes than on undeveloped ones (Woodford & Meyer 2003, Biological Conservation 110:277–284). Frogs depend on shoreline cover and debris. — [Grist summary quoting M. Meyer](https://grist.org/article/ness-development/). The paper's own per-km densities were not accessible.
- **Diet.**
  - Adults eat various small, mainly terrestrial invertebrates and occasionally small amphibians. — [Ontario Nature](https://ontarionature.org/programs/community-science/reptile-amphibian-atlas/green-frog/)
  - ADW instead gives moths, snails and small fish. — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
  - Werner et al. (1995) found bullfrogs relied more on aquatic prey than green frogs did, which implies green frogs take relatively more terrestrial prey. — [VHS bullfrog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Hibernation.** Adults usually overwinter in water, occasionally on land (Bohnsack 1951; Lannoo et al. 1998). In Maine they hibernate underwater or underground from October to March (Hunter et al. 1999). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
- **Predators.**
  - **Adults and juveniles:** bullfrogs, crows, herons, mink, river otters, raccoons, snapping turtles, water snakes, garter snakes, catfish, black basses and rock bass. — [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Lithobates_clamitans/)
  - **Also of adults:** ducks, bitterns, rails and northern harriers. Larger green frogs eat smaller ones (Martof 1956b; Klemens 1993).
  - **Eggs:** turtles.
  - **Tadpoles:** diving beetles, dragonfly nymphs, giant water bugs, backswimmers.
  - — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)

**Bullfrog**
- **Maine.** Statewide, common and secure; up to about 8 in. Highly aquatic: permanent streams, slow rivers, ponds and shallow lake bays. Tadpoles need permanent water and typically take two years to develop. Breeds later than most Maine frogs, when air is above 68 °F (20 °C), typically mid-June to July. Hibernates in mud at the bottom of ponds or streams. — [MDIFW bullfrog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/american-bullfrog.html)
  - Maine tadpoles overwinter as first- or second-year tadpoles. — [Maine Biota Project](https://mainenaturalhistory.org/Biodiversity_Project/checklistsME/Animalia_Chordata_Amphibia/Anura.htm)
- **Temperatures.** Adults stay inactive until water approaches 15 °C (Harding 1997). Preferred body temperature is 26–33 °C. Embryos develop abnormally above 32 °C. — [VHS bullfrog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Breeding.**
  - Québec: late May to mid-July, peak mid-June to July. Southern Ontario: peak calling May 17 – June 8.
  - Eggs are laid as surface films of 0.5–1 m² and hatch in 3–5 days. Clutches hold up to about 20,000 eggs.
  - — [VHS bullfrog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Larval period.** Up to 3 years in Michigan and Nova Scotia. Michigan tadpoles transformed in the first or second season after hatching, and larval period falls with frost-free season length (Collins 1979). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php). In Canada the larval stage can exceed two years. — [Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/american-bullfrog)
- **Maturity and life span.**
  - Maturity at 1–3 years: 2–3 years in New York (Raney & Ingram 1941); breeding size 95–110 mm in Québec.
  - Wild adults live about 8–10 years.
  - — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Where adults sit, spacing and density.**
  - Males defend roughly circular territories 2–5 m across (Harding 1997).
  - Ontario pond: mean activity radius 2.6 m, and home ranges shrink at higher density (Currie & Bellis 1969). The same study gives adult density as 0.9–1.3/m², which seems implausibly high and should be checked against the original.
  - Illinois lake: 8.8–45.8/ha. California canals: 6.6–119 per km of shore. Arizona: one adult about every 1.8 m of shoreline in places.
  - — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Movement.** In New York, mean movement was 402 m and the maximum 1,600 m, with homing (Ingram & Raney 1943). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Diet (Michigan, Kellogg Biological Station, postmetamorphic bullfrogs; Werner, Wellborn & McPeek 1995, J. Herpetol. 29:600–607)** — [OEHHA ecotox record](https://ecotox.oehha.ca.gov/citation/diet-composition-postmetamorphic-bullfrogs-and-green-frogs-implications-interspecific):
  - Hemiptera 19.2–23.2%.
  - Odonata (adults) 12.4–17.5%.
  - Miscellaneous terrestrial 10.5–11.1%.
  - Diptera 3.7–10.5%.
  - Coleoptera 2.3–6.5%.
  - Frogs 5.1–14.5%.
  - Larval Odonata 3.4–5.0%.
  - Larval Diptera 4.4–7.2%.
  - Miscellaneous aquatic 1.9–7.4%.
  - The basis (volume, number or frequency) is not stated in the record.
- **Diet elsewhere.** Large bullfrogs eat frogs, crayfish and mice (Frost 1935). In two western studies, cannibalism made up 80–87% of stomach volume. — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Hibernation.**
  - Bullfrogs usually hibernate underwater, in surface mud or in pits.
  - Ohio: in water under 1 m deep near inlet streams, 1–2 m offshore, active and resting on the bottom without silt cover (Stinner et al. 1994).
  - Nova Scotia: radio-tagged frogs tolerated pond PO₂ below 20 Torr for more than 8 weeks (Friet & Pinder 1990).
  - Bullfrogs are not freeze tolerant.
  - — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
  - At 2 °C, submerged bullfrogs did not activate their breathing muscles. — [J. Exp. Biol. 220:1181](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/220/7/1181/19591/Activation-of-respiratory-muscles-does-not-occur)
- **Predators and palatability.** Snapping turtles take males defending mid-pond territories. Bullfrog tadpoles are relatively unpalatable to fish but vulnerable to invertebrate and salamander predators (Werner & McPeek 1994). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Larval survival (Kentucky).** 11.8–17.6% (Cecil & Just 1979). — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)

**Mink frog**
- **Maine.** Western, central, eastern and northern regions; Maine is the southern edge of its range. It is a Species of Greatest Conservation Need but common.
  - Habitat: ponds, lakes, bogs and streams with shallow areas of dense emergent vegetation. Highly aquatic and rarely on land; calls mostly after midnight.
  - Breeds late June through early August. Hibernates in mud at the bottom of ponds or streams.
  - Adults eat aquatic invertebrates and sometimes small minnows; tadpoles eat algae. Length 1.5–3 in.
  - — [MDIFW mink frog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/mink-frog.html)
- **Diet study.** A comparative diet study of mink frogs, bullfrogs and green frogs in the Adirondacks exists (Stewart & Sandison 1972, Cranberry Lake), but its numbers were not accessible. — [OEHHA record](https://ecotox.oehha.ca.gov/citation/comparative-food-habits-sympatric-mink-frogs-bullfrogs-and-green-frogs)

### Inferences
- **Where frogs sit (the new shore).**
  - **Green frogs:** at the waterline among emergent plants, on the bank edge, or half-submerged in shallow water. A density of about 1 green frog per 1–6 m of vegetated shore is reasonable: about 0.2–1 per metre in good cover, fewer on open or developed shores.
  - **Bullfrogs:** float in open water off the edge, with large males spaced at about 2–5 m.
  - **Mink frogs:** float among lily pads and emergents in shallow water, rarely on land.
- **Shore foraging.** The current lump-sum `SHORE_FOOD` ("mostly land insects") is consistent in spirit. Green frog foraging should happen within about 1 m of water on dry days, extending farther on rainy nights. A frog on land should flee to water when a terrestrial predator (heron, mink, raccoon) approaches; in water it should dive from fish.
- **Tadpole timing in the sim.** `FROG.TAD_DEV_FULL` = 0.12 yr (2–3 months in a warm summer) is right for spring peepers and wood frogs. It is too short for green frogs in Maine, which overwinter once, and for bullfrogs, which take about 2 years. Overwintering tadpoles should stay on the bottom under silt and leaves, active at low rates, and metamorphose in June–July of the next year.
- **Breeding in the sim.** `FROG.BREED_T` = [10, 20] °C may be too early for green frogs, bullfrogs and mink frogs. Their breeding runs late May or June to August, when shallows are well above 15–20 °C; the bullfrog cue is air above 20 °C. A 10–20 °C window fits spring peepers and wood frogs.
- **Hibernation in the sim.** Overwintering adults (green frog, bullfrog, mink frog) should rest on the bottom in shallow, oxygenated water, often near an inflow, and not buried deep in anoxic mud. They are not freeze tolerant. Under-ice oxygen therefore matters to them: the sim's winter O₂ field could kill frogs in hypoxic shallows.

### Gaps
- No green frog or bullfrog densities per metre of shore from Maine or New England were found. The Woodford & Meyer 2003 per-km values (Wisconsin) were not accessible.
- No percent terrestrial-prey figure for green frogs was found. Jenssen & Klimstra 1966 (Illinois) is the classic stomach study, but its numbers were not accessible.
- No water-temperature threshold for green frog breeding was found. The common 42/50/56 °F NAAMP run thresholds could not be confirmed.
- **Fish predation.** No quantitative data were found on chain pickerel or smallmouth bass predation on frogs or tadpoles in Maine lakes.

## 7. Other shore- and catchment-linked herps: spring peeper, wood frog, spotted salamander, red-spotted newt, northern water snake, common garter snake

### Takeaway
**Wood frogs and spotted salamanders** are forest animals. They breed mostly in fishless vernal pools and beaver flowages; fish make lakes "sinks" for them. They migrate on the first warm, rainy nights after thaw: mid-March in southern Maine to mid-May in northern Maine. Spring peepers follow wood frogs within days and also prefer fishless ponds.

**Red-spotted newts** live as aquatic adults in Maine lakes and permanent ponds with fish. Tetrodotoxin makes adults and especially efts largely unpalatable to fish, but not to bullfrogs. Larvae are heavily eaten.

The **common water snake** (a fish-eater that basks on logs, rocks and beaver lodges) is listed for southern, central and eastern Maine, but its Maine range is patchy and disjunct. Its presence at Hot Brook is unconfirmed. The **common garter snake** is statewide and abundant but mainly a terrestrial earthworm-eater that takes some amphibians and fish.

### Cited Findings
- **Maine vernal-pool amphibians (MDIFW/UMaine assessment, Calhoun & deMaynadier 2001)** — [Vernal Pool Assessment PDF](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/species_planning/reptiles/vernalpools/vernal_pools.pdf)
  - **Pool use by species.** Wood frog, spotted salamander and blue-spotted salamander "prefer" vernal pools for breeding. Green, pickerel and leopard frogs breed in them "facultatively".
  - **Fish.** Fish presence or short hydroperiods can make beaver flowages, lakeshores and other sites "biological sinks" for spotted salamanders.
  - **Spotted salamander migration.** Adults emerge on the first warm, rainy nights of spring: as early as mid-March in southern Maine, as late as mid-May in northern Maine. Males arrive first.
  - **Spotted salamander eggs and larvae.** Females lay on average 2–4 egg masses. Eggs hatch in about 4–8 weeks and larvae take another 10–14 weeks. Metamorphs leave pools in July in southern Maine and as late as September in central and northern Maine.
  - **Spotted salamander uplands.**
    - Adults live underground in forest, in burrows up to 3 ft deep.
    - Home ranges are 1–40 m².
    - Adult density within 200 m of a Massachusetts pool was 1–4 per 100 m² (Windmiller 1996).
  - **Wood frog breeding.** An explosive breeder: mating and laying can finish in under a week. Females lay 1–2 egg masses of up to 1,000+ eggs, often communally. Larvae hatch in about 3 weeks, and metamorphs emerge in 7–8 weeks.
  - **Wood frog uplands.** Outside the breeding season, wood frogs live in forests, wooded wetlands, bogs, and "along vegetated pond and lake shores". Their terrestrial range can be more than half a mile from breeding pools, with home ranges of 45–64 m². In Maine and Massachusetts they have also moved to pools during drought and cold.
- **Migration triggers.** Night temperature above about 40 °F (4–7 °C) with rain and thawed ground triggers movement. Big Night usually falls in early to mid-April in southern and midcoast Maine, with smaller movements from mid-March to mid-May. — [MDIFW blog](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/blogs/mdifw-blog/why-did-amphibian-cross-road); [Portland Press Herald 2026](https://www.pressherald.com/2026/03/27/hop-into-big-night-maines-evening-of-amphibian-migration/); [Maine Coast Heritage Trust](https://www.mcht.org/story/spring-is-for-vernal-pools/)
- **Spring peeper.**
  - Calls within days after the first wood frogs. — [Maine Biota Project](https://mainenaturalhistory.org/Biodiversity_Project/checklistsME/Animalia_Chordata_Amphibia/Anura.htm)
  - Calls early April to May. Clutches hold 800–1,000 eggs, which hatch in 6–12 days. The larval period is 45–90 days. Breeds preferentially in ponds without fish. Hibernates under logs and in tree holes. — [ADW spring peeper](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Pseudacris_crucifer/)
  - ADW's calling-start temperature of "28 °C" looks like an error.
- **Red-spotted (eastern) newt.**
  - **Maine:** statewide, common and secure. Adults live in streams, lakes and relatively permanent ponds; efts live in forested uplands under logs and debris. Some skip the eft stage.
  - Adults overwinter in ponds, some active all winter, while others hibernate underground. Adults and efts eat insects, worms, leeches, small fishes and amphibian eggs and larvae.
  - — [MDIFW eastern newt](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/eastern-newt.html)
  - **Defence against fish:** fishes largely avoided newts, and unpalatability is concentrated in the dorsal skin, likely tetrodotoxin (TTX). The defence is ineffective against bullfrogs, and possibly turtles, which ate many tethered newts in the field. — [PLOS ONE 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0027581)
  - TTX makes the newt unpalatable to predatory fish and crayfish. — [USFWS](https://www.fws.gov/media/red-eft-terrestrial-eastern-red-spotted-newt)
  - The larva is the most heavily preyed-upon stage (fish, aquatic insects, adult newts). Eft skin carries about 10 times the toxin of adult skin. — search summary; I did not isolate which of the [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_newt) and [Finger Lakes](https://www.gofingerlakes.org/the-eastern-newt/) pages carries which statement.
- **Common (northern) water snake.**
  - **Maine:** common and secure in the southern, central and eastern regions; 24–48 in. The most aquatic snake in Maine, in still or slow water with open canopy and emergent vegetation.
  - Basks on rocks, logs, and beaver dams and lodges. Mostly eats fish, with some amphibians and invertebrates.
  - Hibernates near water in rock crevices, beaver dams, or beaver and muskrat tunnels in stream banks. Breeds in April–May and early fall, and gives birth to live young August–October.
  - — [MDIFW common watersnake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/common-watersnake.html)
  - **Patchy Maine range:** a disjunct Maine range, with one occupied region about 125 km from any other known population. Occupied lakes were warmer than unoccupied ones. Lake pH, surface temperature and area did not predict presence; bioclimatic variables did. — [UMaine honors thesis](https://honors.umaine.edu/?p=9938)
  - Water snakes are also listed as predators of painted turtle young. — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
- **Common garter snake.** Statewide; the most commonly seen snake in Maine, 18–36 in long.
  - Uses uplands, fields and wetter areas (streams, bogs, marshes).
  - Earthworms make up 60–95% of its diet, plus amphibians, fish and occasional small birds.
  - Overwinters in uplands, in ant mounds, rodent burrows and root tunnels. Gives birth July to mid-September.
  - — [MDIFW common gartersnake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/common-gartersnake.html)
  - Garter snakes have evolved resistance to newt TTX. — [search summary](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0027581)

### Inferences
- **Wood frogs, spotted salamanders and peepers.** In a lake–stream–pond landscape with fish in all three water bodies, these belong mostly in the catchment: off-screen vernal pools. On-screen they would be visible as spring migrants crossing the banks on rainy April or May nights, and later as adult wood frogs along the vegetated pond shore in summer.
  - A fishless or fish-poor 4-m pond would still be a poor wood-frog site (permanent water, fish).
  - Spring peepers could chorus from the pond's emergent edge, but their recruitment there would be low if fish are present.
- **Red-spotted newt.** This is the one salamander that belongs in the lake and pond with fish. Adults can be modelled as slow bottom and weed foragers that fish reject. Bullfrogs, and possibly turtles, eat them. Larvae are edible to fish. Some adults stay active under the ice.
- **Water snake.** If included, it is a shore-to-water fish predator. It basks on logs, rocks and beaver lodges, hunts in the shallows, and hibernates in the bank. It would compete with herons for small fish and take frogs and turtle hatchlings. Given the uncertain eastern Maine occurrence, it is optional.
- **Garter snake.** A shore-edge, mostly terrestrial predator that occasionally takes frogs and fish; low priority.

### Gaps
- Whether *Nerodia sipedon* occurs in the Hot Brook or Baskahegan area is unknown; the MARAP township map PDF was not readable.
- No water-temperature data were found for peeper or wood frog breeding in eastern Maine.
- No data were found on green frog, bullfrog or newt use of beaver flowages versus lakes in Maine.

## 8. Seasonal calendar for eastern Maine (Washington County), tied to ice-out and temperature

### Takeaway
Eastern Maine's herp year is anchored on ice-out, about April 24–28 at West Grand Lake. Wood frogs, spotted salamanders and peepers move and breed first, from mid-April to early May on rainy nights above about 4–7 °C. Painted turtles surface and bask right after ice-out but feed only once they can warm above about 15–20 °C. Turtle nesting peaks in mid-June. Green frogs and mink frogs breed in June–August, and bullfrogs from mid-June to July once air is above 20 °C. Snapping turtle hatchlings emerge in September. Painted turtle hatchlings mostly wait in the nest until the next spring. Turtles submerge to hibernate from mid-September to mid-October, and frogs from October.

### Cited Findings
- **Ice-out.** West Grand Lake (Washington County) mean ice-out April 28 (1878–2008, standard deviation 8.8 days; extremes April 2 and May 19), and April 24 over 1979–2008. — [USGS OFR 2010-1214, as cited in the project's Maine report](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2010/1214/pdf/ofr2010-1214.pdf)
- **Painted turtle.**
  - Hibernation begins around mid-September to mid-October. Spring emergence falls in early to late April or May (Cagle 1954; Sexton 1959a; Ernst 1971b).
  - In northern regions the active season is about four months, early May to early September; in southern Ontario up to six months.
  - Nesting runs late May to early July, peaking mid-June. Hatching is in autumn, but most northern hatchlings overwinter in the nest and some delay emergence to June–July.
  - — [COSEWIC 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html)
  - Northern populations may hibernate five to six months. — [Wild Adirondacks](https://wildadirondacks.org/adirondack-reptiles-painted-turtle-chrysemys-picta.html)
  - New Hampshire autumn emergences were Aug 10 – Sep 26; most hatchlings emerged in spring. — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)
- **Snapping turtle.**
  - Maine laying runs late May to the first week of July, mostly June, with hatching about 85 days later. — [Sun Journal, via search summary](https://new.sunjournal.com/?p=9255515)
  - New Hampshire hatchlings emerged Sep 5–24. — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)
  - Feeding begins once water exceeds 16 °C. — [Tortoise Trust](https://www.tortoisetrust.org/articles/snappers.htm)
- **Wood frog and spotted salamander.** Breeding migrations run from mid-March in southern Maine to mid-May in northern Maine. — [Calhoun & deMaynadier 2001](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/species_planning/reptiles/vernalpools/vernal_pools.pdf). In Maine, the program surveys spring peepers and wood frogs in "early spring"; American toads and leopard and pickerel frogs in "late spring"; and gray treefrogs, green frogs, mink frogs and bullfrogs in "early summer". — [Maine Amphibian Monitoring Program, via search summary](https://scistarter.org/maine-amphibian-monitoring-program)
- **Green frog.** Breeds May–August in Maine. Hibernates October–March, underwater or underground (Hunter et al. 1999). — [VHS green frog](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/green-frog/index.php)
- **Bullfrog.** Breeds mid-June to July when air is above 68 °F. — [MDIFW](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/american-bullfrog.html). Inactive until water approaches 15 °C. — [VHS](https://www.virginiaherpetologicalsociety.com/amphibians/frogsandtoads/american-bullfrog/index.php)
- **Mink frog.** Breeds late June to early August. — [MDIFW](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/mink-frog.html)
- **Water snake and garter snake.** Water snakes mate April–May and early fall and give birth August–October. Garter snakes give birth July to mid-September. — [MDIFW watersnake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/common-watersnake.html); [MDIFW gartersnake](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/common-gartersnake.html)
- **Wood turtle.** Lays eggs in June–July. — [MDIFW](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/fish-wildlife/wildlife/species-information/reptiles-amphibians/wood-turtle.html). New Hampshire hatchlings emerged Aug 13 – Sep 14. — [Carroll & Ultsch 2007](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-14-2/22-Ultsch.shtml)

### Gaps
