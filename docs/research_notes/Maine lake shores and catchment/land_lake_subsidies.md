# Land–water exchanges at Maine / New England lakes, ponds and small streams: terrestrial subsidies in, aquatic exports out

Region tags on each number: [ME] Maine, [NH] New Hampshire (Hubbard Brook), [NE-US] other northeastern US, [ON] Ontario / Canadian Shield, [WI] Wisconsin / NTL-LTER, [App] southern Appalachians (Coweeta, VA, WV), [PNW] Pacific Northwest, [AK] Alaska, [EU] Europe, [JP] Japan, [IS] Iceland, [NS] Nova Scotia.
"(abstract)" means the number was read in the paper's abstract. "(full text)" means it was read in the PDF. "(snippet)" means it came only through a search-engine summary and needs checking before it becomes a tuned constant.

## 1. Terrestrial invertebrate fall-in: rates, season, distance from shore, forest vs open, wind; terrestrial prey in fish diets; surface rises and leaps

### Takeaway
Forested streams 1–5 m wide receive about 10–250 mg dry m⁻² d⁻¹ of terrestrial invertebrates in summer, with a typical forested value of about 40–80. The input is near zero in winter, peaks in July–August, and is about 1.7–4× higher under closed deciduous canopy than open or conifer banks. Lakes get the same kind of input, but it is confined to a narrow band along a forested shore and is diluted by the open water. It is a large share of the diet for surface- and drift-feeding fish (brook trout: more than 50% of energy in the warm seasons; surface cyprinids: dominant in late summer). It is a small but pulsed share for centrarchids (about 0–8% annually, with summer beetle pulses). Developed shorelines remove most of it.

### Cited Findings
**Input rates and seasonality (streams)**
- [EU, Sweden] Sundtjärnsbäcken is a first-order outlet stream 1–1.5 m wide under more than 70% canopy (spruce and pine, with some birch and alder). Uncovered enclosures received a mean of 39.5 ± 7.2 and 39.4 ± 9.6 mg dry m⁻² d⁻¹ from May to October. Covered enclosures received 9.4 and 8.5 mg m⁻² d⁻¹, about 4× less. Input peaked in July. (full text) — [Erős et al. 2012, PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3344874/)
- [AK] Surface inputs to 12 southeast Alaska streams were about 80 mg m⁻² d⁻¹, mostly terrestrial invertebrates. Traps under red alder caught consistently more terrestrial-invertebrate biomass than traps under conifers. Conifers carried much less invertebrate biomass per stem than deciduous overstory and understory plants. Terrestrial and aquatic prey were roughly equal fractions of juvenile coho diets. (abstract) — [Allan et al. 2003, CJFAS 60:309](https://doi.org/10.1139/f03-019)
- [Wyoming, rangeland] From June to September, inputs were 248.7 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ at better-vegetated grazing sites and 108.0 at season-long-grazed sites. Input peaked in August and fell to low levels by late September. (snippet of abstract) — [Saunders & Fausch 2007, TAFS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1577/T06-260.1)
- [JP] In a deciduous forest stream (3.5 m wide reach), terrestrial invertebrate input arrived mainly in summer, when aquatic prey biomass was nearly at its lowest. Input tracked terrestrial prey biomass (r = 0.92). About 97% of the stream width was shaded during the leafing period. Buds break in early June and leaves drop in mid-October. Absolute fluxes appear only in a figure. (full text) — [Nakano & Murakami 2001, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)
- [review] Terrestrial invertebrate fluxes to streams "can provide up to half the annual energy budget for drift-feeding fishes such as salmonids, despite the fact that input occurs principally in summer." Inputs are highest from closed-canopy deciduous riparian zones and "vary markedly with invertebrate phenology and weather." (abstract) — [Baxter, Fausch & Saunders 2005, Freshwater Biology 50:201](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2004.01328.x)
- [JP] Annual terrestrial invertebrate input was 1.7× greater for forested than for grassland stream reaches (Kawaguchi & Nakano 2001). (snippet) — [via Kawaguchi et al. 2003 / Wiley search record](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084%5B0701:TIIDTL%5D2.0.CO;2)
- [JP] When the stream was experimentally covered with greenhouse roofing to cut terrestrial input, fish biomass decreased dramatically (Kawaguchi et al. 2003). (snippet) — [Kawaguchi et al. 2003, Ecology](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084%5B0701:TIIDTL%5D2.0.CO;2)
- [temperate vs tropical] In temperate forested streams, "terrestrial invertebrate input to streams approaches zero during the winter season." This came from a tropical study contrasting itself with temperate data. (snippet) — [Small et al. 2013, Biotropica](https://pringlelab.ecology.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/Small-et-al.-2013-Biotropica.pdf)
- [VA piedmont] Terrestrial invertebrates were most abundant in drift nets in April. Drifting terrestrial invertebrates were concentrated at the surface near the thalweg, rather than at the margins or on the bed. (abstract) — [Cloe & Garman 1996, Env. Biol. Fishes](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02028848)

**Input to lakes, and distance from shore**
- [EU, German lowland lake, 0.12 km², mesotrophic, mixed forest] A bioenergetics/decay model for the bleak population assumed terrestrial-insect fall-in of 50.4 mg dry weight per metre of shoreline per day. (snippet. This figure is from a search summary attributed to the ResearchGate record of this paper; it is not in the abstract.) — [Mehner et al. 2005, L&O 50:2022](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230729865_Can_feeding_of_fish_on_terrestrial_insects_subsidize_the_nutrient_pool_of_lakes)
- [EU] Bleak ate 84% terrestrial insects, a consumption of 432 g of lake-external P per year, which is about 2.1% of the lake's internal P pool. Excretion of terrestrial P equalled about 11% of mean epilimnetic SRP. A substantial fish-mediated subsidy is likely only in lakes under about 0.03 km² (3 ha), "owing to the increasing perimeter-to-area ratio". (abstract) — [Mehner et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2005.50.6.2022). Wurtsbaugh (2007) argued that three aspects of the analysis overstate the effect. — [Wurtsbaugh 2007 comment](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/wats_facpub/117)
- [lakes, general] Airborne terrestrial particulate C (leaves, twigs, insects and insect parts, pollen, dust) is deposited per metre of shoreline. Seven empirical estimates were fitted with a negative exponential (mean 1.88 g C m⁻¹ shoreline yr⁻¹). Deposition depends on vegetation type, "location relative to the dominant wind direction, and major weather events". Leaf litter falls mostly within 10 m of shore. Fine POC is also elevated near shore, with "notable deposition extending substantial distances out into the lake". TPOC deposition exceeds insect emergence in small lakes, and the reverse holds in large lakes. (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011, CJFAS 68:170](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)
- [AK lakes, coastal cutthroat] Juvenile intake of terrestrial prey correlated with overhanging shoreline vegetation (r = 0.89, n = 6 lakes) and with decaying wood in the littoral zone (r = 0.87). The lake with intact old forest had about 87% overstory and 75% overhanging cover, and its adult trout ate almost entirely terrestrial invertebrates. (full text) — [PMC 2025, adfluvial coastal cutthroat trout](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12127772/)

**Shoreline development**
- [PNW + N. America literature, 24 lakes] Terrestrial insects made up to 100% of fish diet mass in undeveloped lakes and averaged 2% in developed lakes. Trout in undeveloped lakes had about 50% greater daily energy intake, of which up to 50% was terrestrial prey. The subsidy "is distinctly pulsed". (abstract) — [Francis & Schindler 2009, Oikos 118:1872](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0706.2009.17723.x). A secondary account gives "up to 100% (average 18%)" for wholly undeveloped lakes and "maximum 2% (average 0.2%)" for highly developed lakes. This differs from the abstract's wording, so check it before using. — [ESA press summary](https://esa.org/blog/2009/08/04/food-for-fish-dwindling-on-developed-lakes/)
- [PNW] In July, terrestrial prey supplied 7–30% of total energy intake in undeveloped lakes, and total ration was 3–15× greater in undeveloped lakes than urban ones (n = 2 each). (conference slides, full text) — [Francis & Schindler 2008, UW Water Center review](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/bitstreams/8d96c48b-c4e0-427d-a1cd-adb0e9500094/download)

**Terrestrial prey in fish diets (species asked about)**
- Brook trout [App, WV]: in every season except winter, brook trout "derived significantly more energy from terrestrial organisms than aquatic organisms". Terrestrial Coleoptera and Lepidoptera were key prey in warm seasons. (abstract) — [Utz & Hartman 2007, Hydrobiologia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-006-0372-0)
- Brook trout [App]: terrestrial invertebrates were 7% of available resources but 54% of the diet by abundance (intermittent streams). Age-1+ fish often gain more than 50% of annual energy from terrestrial invertebrates. (snippet) — [WVU thesis record](https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/etd/2228); [JMU thesis](https://commons.lib.jmu.edu/master201019/178)
- Brook trout and rainbow trout in a lake [California, Castle Lake]: more than 30% terrestrial prey. "Terrestrial insects are rarely dominant in lentic fish diets" but are commonly present. (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)
- Stream salmonids, literature compiled in a 2025 paper: 30–45% of trout prey biomass in Colorado (Saunders & Fausch 2012); more than half of ingested biomass for Dolly Varden, coho and cutthroat in southeast Alaska (Wipfli 1997); up to 28% of energy for juvenile coho (Nielsen 1992). (full text) — [PMC12127772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12127772/)
- [JP] Terrestrial invertebrates supplied 44.0% of the annual energy budget of the fish assemblage: masu salmon 56.7%, white-spotted char 50.5%, rainbow trout 46.3%, Dolly Varden 22.6%, sculpin (a benthic feeder) 12.0%. During leafing they supplied 60–100% of the food of the three water-column fishes. (full text) — [Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562). Kawaguchi & Nakano (2001) found 50–90% of fish diets terrestrial in summer and 0% in winter. (cited in full text) — [Owens et al. 2022, Front. Ecol. Evol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2022.788625/full)
- Surface-oriented cyprinid, a proxy for golden shiner and creek chub [VA]: in August, terrestrial prey dominated the diet of *Notropis ardens* even though terrestrial availability had fallen about tenfold since April. "Several other co-occurring fishes only rarely consumed prey of terrestrial origin." The authors attribute this to morphology, behaviour and predator avoidance. (abstract) — [Cloe & Garman 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02028848)
- Smallmouth bass and redbreast sunfish [VA, Shenandoah, 2002]: annual diet by river was 0.1–2.6% terrestrial insects for smallmouth and 1.9–8.0% for redbreast sunfish. Both took "sizeable percentages" in summer, mainly green June beetles, especially redbreast over 150 mm. Smallmouth diet was otherwise 32–48% aquatic insects, 19–31% crayfish and 22–29% fish. Stomachs containing food fell from 93% in spring to 26% in winter for smallmouth, and from 99% to 8% for redbreast. (full text) — [Murphy et al. 2005, Proc. SEAFWA](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/murphy-325-335.pdf)
- Creek chub: larvae ate about 90% chironomids [OH] (full text) — [Ward & Coburn 2008, Northeastern Naturalist](https://eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-3/12-Coburn.shtml). Adults are opportunistic insectivores that take adult terrestrial insects as they grow; no percentage was found. — [ADW account](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Semotilus_atromaculatus)
- Golden shiner: an omnivore that feeds at the surface, in midwater or on the bottom, and can filter-feed zooplankton. Terrestrial insects are listed among its foods, without a percentage. — [NC fish profile](https://hrla.com/resources/site/ncfish/goldenshiner.htm); [FishBase](https://fishbase.se/summary/276). Young-of-year golden shiner in a Lake Ontario embayment ate benthic tipulids in summer. — [USGS 2017 record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70179782)

**Rises and leaps**
- Aerial (above-surface) prey capture is uncommon: "relatively few taxa feed terrestrially or aerially". Some species specialize. Others, including largemouth bass, do it opportunistically and alter their locomotor and feeding kinematics to do so, because suction feeding fails in air. (full text record) — [Axlid et al. 2025, Proc. R. Soc. B](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12646744/)
- Drifting terrestrial prey rides at the surface (see Cloe & Garman above), so fish that eat it take it at or from the surface film.

### Inferences
- **A fall-in mechanism for the sim.** Model input per metre of forested bank, F(t), in mg dry m⁻¹ d⁻¹. Spread it over the water surface with an exponential kernel, exp(−x/λ)/λ, from each forested bank.
  - Sanity check of the lake-shore value: the summer F ≈ 50 mg m⁻¹ d⁻¹ attributed to Mehner et al. reproduces the stream numbers. A 4 m wide stream with two forested banks gives 2 × 50 / 4 ≈ 25 mg m⁻² d⁻¹, close to Sweden's 39.5. A 1.5 m stream gives about 67, close to Alaska's 80. This makes F ≈ 50 (range about 20–120) a defensible summer peak per forested bank.
  - Choose λ ≈ 5–15 m. This is set by canopy overhang plus wind drift, and no lake-specific measurement was found. The analogy is that leaf litter lands mostly within 10 m of shore. On a lake that gives a peak of about 3–10 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ at the bank, decaying to about 0 beyond 30–50 m. The stream is narrow, so it is entirely "near-shore".
- **Season.** Keep it at 0 from about mid-November to April. Ramp up after leaf-out (late May–June in Maine), peak in July–August, and decline through September to leaf drop in October. Add short multiplicative bursts on windy or rainy days, since inputs "vary markedly with… weather" and wind direction matters.
- **Shore type multipliers, relative to a closed deciduous forest = 1.0.**
  - mixed or conifer forest: about 0.4–0.6 (alder ≫ conifer; Allan 2003);
  - grass or open bank: about 0.6 (1/1.7, Kawaguchi & Nakano);
  - developed or lawn shore: about 0.05–0.2 (Francis & Schindler).
- **Prey identity.** The terrestrial items are mostly beetles, ants and other Hymenoptera, caterpillars and spiders. As food, give them the energy density of aquatic insects and roughly 10% N and 1% P of dry mass (the measured midge stoichiometry in §6). Every gram eaten is new N and P entering the lake, so the closed budgets need a matching "land" source term.
- **Diet propensity by species, an inference from feeding mode.** Rank terrestrial-prey use as: brook trout (drift and surface) and creek chub > golden shiner (surface-capable) > smallmouth bass and pumpkinseed (episodic summer beetles) > yellow perch, white perch, white sucker, brown bullhead and chain pickerel (rare). The surface position of the prey suggests a simple sensory gate: only fish in the top layer, roughly the upper 0.5–1 m, see it.
- **Rises.** A "rise" is a cosmetic surface-strike effect when a fish takes surface prey. Real leaps for flying prey should be rare and opportunistic (bass), not routine. This is a render-only effect and must not feed back into the sim.

### Gaps
- No directly measured lake data were found for terrestrial-insect fall-in versus distance from shore (mg m⁻² d⁻¹ at 1, 5, 20 m). λ above is inferred. Francis & Schindler 2009 (Oikos full text) likely has pan-trap fluxes, but the publisher pages were blocked (403/Cloudflare).
- No percent-terrestrial values were found for golden shiner, pumpkinseed, yellow perch or white perch in lakes, or for Maine smallmouth. The species ranking above is an inference.
- No quantitative frequency was found for surface rises or leaps by these species.
- I saw a northern Japan figure of trout eating 57 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ out of a total input of 74 mg m⁻² d⁻¹, but could not trace it to a fetched source, so it is excluded.
- New England stream fall-in values: there is a Maine/New Hampshire deciduous-vs-coniferous headwater study (Damkot & Coghlan, AFS 2011 abstract), but its page could not be reached.

## 2. Leaf litter and other plant inputs: amounts, the band of shore reached, timing in Maine, decomposition rates, and fates (FPOM, DOC, shredders)

### Takeaway
A northern hardwood forest drops about 570 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹ of litter [NH], about half of it leaves (234–370 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ of leaves across 13 sites). About half the annual total falls in autumn, with a mid-October peak. Lakes receive leaves mainly within about 10 m of a forested shore, and lateral blow-in along the ground adds only about 0.3–0.5 m. Leaves break down at k ≈ 0.004–0.03 d⁻¹: about 0.01 for red maple in streams, about 0.005 for white pine, faster for alder, and roughly 2× slower in lakes. Up to about 25% of the mass leaches within days to weeks. Shredders turn about half of what they process into FPOM.

### Cited Findings
**Litterfall amounts and timing**
- [NH, Hubbard Brook] Total aboveground litterfall averaged 5,702 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (570 g m⁻²). By part it was 49.1% leaves, 22.2% branches, 14.1% stems, 1.7% bark and 10.9% other (bud scales, flowers, fruit, insect frass). By season: autumn 49.7%, summer 21.2%, winter 22.4%, spring 6.7%. "Storms were very important in the timing of litter and nutrient fall." The litter carried 140.4 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ of 11 elements, ranked N > Ca > K > Mn > Mg > S > P. (abstract) — [Gosz, Likens & Bormann 1972, Ecology 53:769](https://doi.org/10.2307/1934293)
- [NH, 13 northern hardwood sites] Leaf litterfall averaged over the sites varied between years from 234 to 370 g m⁻². P concentration in litter varied the most among sites (CV 56%). (abstract) — [Yanai et al. 2012, CJFR 42:1597](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/variation-in-mass-and-nutrient-concentration-of-leaf-litter-acros/)
- [NH, Bear Brook, 2nd-order stream] Annual energy input was 6,039 kcal m⁻², of which over 99% was allochthonous. Moss production was under 1%, and there were no algae or vascular hydrophytes. Litter plus throughfall made up 44% of the input and inflowing water 56%. 47% of all input arrived as dissolved organic matter. The detritus store held 4,730 kcal m⁻², split about equally between leaves (turnover about 1 yr) and branches (about 4.2 yr). 66% of input was exported downstream and 34% respired. (abstract) — [Fisher & Likens 1973, Ecol. Monogr. 43:421](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942301)
- [Ontario, 16 NW Ontario lakes] Lateral ground transport of riparian litter averaged about 0.3 m yr⁻¹ for conifer needles and 0.5 m yr⁻¹ for deciduous leaves. Lateral input was about 6% of total allochthonous leaf fall to the lakes; most arrives by direct airborne fall. Lateral movement increased with slope, exposure and lack of ground vegetation. (abstract via snippet) — [France 1995, Freshwater Biology 34:495](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/aspen_bib/1975)
- [lakes] Airborne litterfall could be at least 10% of phytoplankton production in oligotrophic and very small eutrophic lakes. The ratio of shoreline to lake area is the key scaling variable. (abstract) — [Gasith & Hasler 1976, L&O 21:253](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1976.21.2.0253)
- [EU, Wales, Llyn Frongoch, small oligotrophic lake] Airborne plant litter input was about 100 kg dry yr⁻¹ (45 kg C), about 5% of phytoplankton production. It "followed an exponential decline with distance from the shore." (abstract) — [Hydrobiologia, Llyn Frongoch](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00018365)
- [lakes, general] Most leaf litter enters lakes within 10 m of shore (Gasith & Hasler 1976; France & Peters 1995). TPOC deposition across 7 lakes was fitted with a negative exponential, mean Dshore = 1.88 g C m⁻¹ shoreline yr⁻¹. The measurements mix methods and seasons. (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)
- [ON boreal lakes] Forested shores delivered 17.8 g DOC m⁻¹ shoreline yr⁻¹ from leaf leachate, versus 0.4 for clear-cut shores. TP fell from 2.9 to 0.3 (units printed as "g/m shoreline/yr"). Models predicted up to 9% lower plankton primary production and 17% lower respiration after riparian clear-cutting. (abstract; 1996 France et al. per the search record. Given that DOC is 17.8 g, the TP figure is more plausibly mg m⁻¹ yr⁻¹; check the full text) — [Springer, "Decreased carbon and nutrient input to boreal lakes… following riparian clear-cutting"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01474657)
- [ON] Riparian clear-cutting cut small woody debris inputs to the littoral by over 90%. (snippet) — [France 1997, Conservation Biology](https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1046/j.1523-1739.1997.95289.x)
- [NE-US, MELNHE sites incl. Hubbard Brook] Litter-chemistry sampling targets "peak litterfall (i.e., mid-October)". (snippet of a MELNHE dataset description; the exact record is uncertain) — [NSF PAR dataset record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10627841)

**Breakdown rates (k, d⁻¹, exponential mass loss)**
- [App, Coweeta, 1st–2nd order streams] Across 40 published measurements, mean leaf k = 0.0098 d⁻¹ (range 0.0016–0.0316, SD 0.0067). Most of the variation was between species, not streams. (full text, Table 4)
  - red maple (*Acer rubrum*): 13 measurements, 0.0075–0.016, central about 0.010–0.011;
  - eastern white pine (*Pinus strobus*) needles: 0.0054–0.0056;
  - black birch (*Betula lenta*): 0.0036 (one value; the column layout of the extracted table makes this assignment slightly uncertain);
  - white oak: 0.0056–0.0108;
  - rhododendron: 0.0016–0.007;
  - dogwood: 0.016–0.032.
  - Sticks under 3 cm: 0.00017–0.00103 d⁻¹.
  - FPOM: respiration of 1.4 mg O₂ g AFDM⁻¹ d⁻¹ over 6–22 °C implies k ≈ 0.00104 d⁻¹.
  - Leaves move about 1.6 m per transport event. Artificial leaves moved 10–19 m yr⁻¹.
  - Particles are "more likely to be transported than broken down", but sticks and leaves do not travel far.
  - Source: [Webster et al. 1999, Freshwater Biology 41:687](https://ww2.coastal.edu/jjhutche/Pubs/webster%20et%20al,%201999,%2041-%20687-705.pdf)
- [NS, shallow rocky rivers] Red maple and speckled alder litter in shaded tributaries decayed exponentially at k = 0.013–0.032 d⁻¹ in spring and early summer. Where the shredding caddis *Lepidostoma togatum* congregated (40–120 per bag), mass loss was linear at 2–5% d⁻¹ and bags were empty in 35–50 days. Alder with 30 larvae lost about 2.1% d⁻¹, against 0.1% d⁻¹ without them. The congregations ran from early June to mid-July. (abstract) — [Hydrobiologia 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-016-3001-6)
- [ON, Scott Lake littoral, Algonquin] Fall-shed leaves in a lake littoral:
  - sugar maple k = 0.0039 d⁻¹ and beech 0.0058;
  - leaves conditioned overwinter on the ground: maple 0.0042, beech 0.0014.
  - Packs were colonized within 2 days, mostly by chironomids (25–94%) of the collector type. The authors judged that "leaf litter is being used as habitat rather than a direct food source."
  - Leaves lost substantial mass to leaching within 48 h.
  - (abstract) — [Hydrobiologia, Scott Lake](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1003537232319)
- [ON, Canadian Shield lake water] Undried leaves of 6 riparian tree species lost 6–18% of mass after 2 weeks. Woody debris lost 0.2–27% after 7 weeks. Leaching "is a prolonged process developing over weeks." (abstract) — [France et al. 1997, Hydrobiologia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1002953629795)
- Wave turbulence during storms significantly reduced leaf mass in a pre-alpine lake littoral, so physical abrasion matters on exposed shores. (snippet) — [Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/e91ec8cb-375e-4f9b-8835-16d52262d932)

**Fates: leachate DOC, FPOM, shredders**
- Willow leaves leached 8.1 mg DOC g⁻¹ dry in 24 h, under 8% of their C. (snippet; attribution to this Japanese forest-research paper is probable, not confirmed) — [J. For. Res. 2006](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10310-006-0254-3)
- Forest litter: 6–39% of total C loss left as leached DOC, depending on litter chemistry. (snippet; attribution probable) — [Magill & Aber 2000, Soil Biol. Biochem.](https://harvardforest1.fas.harvard.edu/publications/pdfs/Magill_SoilBiol&Biochem_2000_Dissolved.pdf)
- For maple and oak leaves, mass loss rose by up to an extra 10% after a 24-h leaching step, and leaching continues well beyond day 1. (snippet of poster abstract) — [SFS 2025 poster](https://sfs-2025.p.asnevents.com.au/days/2025-05-21/abstract/117749)
- In shredder microcosms with maple, alder and beech, about 50% (37–69%) of the decomposed leaf mass was recovered as FPOM. FPOM production was highest on alder and lowest on beech. (snippet. Which of two candidate papers holds this figure is uncertain.) — [Freshwater Science 32(3), shredder community composition and FPOM](https://bioone.org/journals/freshwater-science/volume-32/issue-3/12-090.1/The-effect-of-shredder-community-composition-on-the-production-and/10.1899/12-090.1.full); [PMC12795619, shredder size and FPOM](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12795619/)
- Terrestrial leaf litter partially supports lake benthic macroinvertebrate production (Rau 1976; Solomon et al. 2008). Whole-lake ¹³C additions show in-lake POC and zooplankton up to 50% terrestrial in origin (Pace et al. 2004). Terrestrial DOC dominated bacterial respiration but contributed little to higher trophic levels (Cole et al. 2006). (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf). Autumn litterfall tPOC moves through shallow-lake food webs mainly by benthic pathways. (snippet) — [Scharnweber et al. 2014, Ecology](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1890/13-0390.1)

### Inferences
- **Lake litter mechanism.** In the leaf-drop window, deposit leaf litter as a pulse onto the water within about 10 m of each forested bank. The window is late September to early November, with about 50% of the annual total centred on mid-October. Use an exponential kernel with λ ≈ 3–5 m to match "most within 10 m". Spread a smaller year-round trickle of twigs, bark and frass (summer 21%, winter 22%).
  - Magnitude per metre of shoreline is uncertain by roughly 10×. It ranges from about 4 g dry m⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (1.88 g C at about 45% C, the Llyn Frongoch C:dry ratio, for the TPOC compilation, which is weighted toward fine summer material) to several hundred g m⁻¹ yr⁻¹. The upper value comes from 300 g m⁻² of leaf fall on a canopy overhanging 1–5 m of water.
  - The France et al. figure of 17.8 g leachate-DOC m⁻¹ yr⁻¹ implies on the order of 10² g leaves m⁻¹ yr⁻¹, which supports the higher end for a fully forested shore.
- **Stream (Hot Brook) litter mechanism.** The stream is narrow and mostly under canopy, so it receives about the full forest litterfall per unit area: about 300 g leaves plus about 250 g other litter m⁻² yr⁻¹. Bear Brook's litter + throughfall of about 2,650 kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹ is about 590 g dry m⁻² at about 4.5 kcal g⁻¹, which agrees. Leaves should mostly be retained locally (they travel only metres) and processed within about 1 year.
- **Decay pathway for each leaf cohort in the sim:**
  1. Fast leaching: 5–20% of mass goes to DOC (plus its N and P) over 2–14 days.
  2. Microbial and shredder breakdown at species k (d⁻¹, scaled by the sim's Q10). Suggested values at stream temperatures: red maple 0.010; birch about 0.006–0.010 (weakly sourced); alder 0.02–0.03 (and faster with shredders); white pine needles 0.005; oak 0.006. Use about half these rates in the lake littoral.
  3. Of the mass lost after leaching, about 50% becomes FPOM, which goes to the sim's detritus and sedOM layer and decays at k ≈ 0.001 d⁻¹. The rest is respired or assimilated. The share assimilated by shredders is unsourced here; about 10–20% is a placeholder.
- **Habitat role.** Leaf packs in the lake littoral act mostly as invertebrate habitat. It is reasonable to raise benthos (chironomid) carrying capacity where litter lies, not just to add food.

### Gaps
- No Maine-specific shoreline litterfall rates (g m⁻¹ shoreline yr⁻¹ or g m⁻² of water by distance) were found. The Gasith & Hasler and France & Peters full texts, which hold those curves, were not accessible.
- No k values were found for paper birch, yellow birch or speckled alder in New England lakes specifically. The Webster & Benfield (1986) species table was not accessed.
- Maine leaf-drop timing: mid-October peak litterfall is documented for New Hampshire. Northern Maine is plausibly 1–2 weeks earlier, but this is unsourced.
- The share of shredder ingestion that is assimilated, as opposed to egested as FPOM, was not found.
- These claims came only through search summaries whose source could not be pinned down, so they are excluded from the findings:
  - red alder decomposes about 2× faster in streams than in lakes after correcting for degree-days;
  - up to 25% of litter mass is lost by leaching in the first 24 h (attributed to Webster & Benfield 1986);
  - the caddis *Pycnopsyche guttifer* (Nova Scotia) prefers alder and red maple and removes 3.0–5.4% d⁻¹.

## 3. DOC and water colour: catchment export, lake concentrations and colour in Maine, storm and snowmelt pulses, light attenuation and thermal structure

### Takeaway
Forested New England catchments export about 2 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ of DOC (Hubbard Brook 1976–80). Later data across nine Hubbard Brook catchments span 1.3–15.3 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, and wetland-rich catchments export more. Maine streams on thin till carry typically under 3 mg L⁻¹ DOC (range 1–7). Maine's lake-assessment practice treats lakes above 30 Pt-Co colour units as "highly colored": about a quarter of the 35 study lakes in 1974–76. DOC is the main control on PAR attenuation in non-eutrophic Shield-type lakes. Kd scales roughly as DOC^0.45, and coloured small lakes have shallower (2.5 m → 0.75 m), cooler epilimnia. DOC in northeastern lakes has risen about 0.5–0.7 mg L⁻¹ per decade.

### Cited Findings
**Catchment export**
- [NH, Hubbard Brook, 1976–80] The streamflow DOC flux was about 20 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, or about 2 g m⁻² yr⁻¹. Throughfall carried 47 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ and soil solution 263; most of that is sorbed in mineral soil. (snippet of abstract) — [McDowell & Likens 1988, Ecol. Monogr. 58](https://lternet.edu/biblio/origin-composition-and-flux-of-dissolved-organic-carbon-in-the-hubbard-brook-valley)
- [NH, 9 forested headwater catchments, 1992–2022] Stream DOC exports were 13–153 kg C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (1.3–15.3 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹). The authors say DOC export has probably increased over 50 years. (snippet, site summary) — [Hubbard Brook news: Solomon et al.](https://hubbardbrook.org/carbon-on-the-run-how-hidden-electrified-waters-speed-earths-forests-toward-the-sea/)
- [NH, Bear Brook] 47% of all organic energy input arrived as dissolved organic matter, and DOM "does not tend to accumulate… very rapid rate of turnover". (abstract) — [Fisher & Likens 1973](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942301)
- [ME, Bear Brook Watershed in Maine] Stream DOC was 1–7 mg L⁻¹, "typically less than 3". Episodic acidification at high discharge came with "slightly increased DOC" and higher NO₃. (snippet of abstract) — [Norton et al. 1999, EMAS 55:7, via USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70022030)
- [VT, forested and agricultural catchments] About 20% of annual nitrate and DOC loads were mobilized in winter rain-on-snow events. Nitrate export during these events was disproportionate compared with spring snowmelt. (snippet of an AGU poster; the hosting record is uncertain) — [UVM record](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/hcoltheses/211)
- [ON boreal] Leaf leachate from forested shorelines supplied 17.8 g DOC m⁻¹ shoreline yr⁻¹. (abstract) — [France et al. 1996](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01474657)

**Maine lake colour and DOC**
- [ME, USGS–Maine DEP study of 43 lakes, 1974–76] 9 of 35 regular study lakes had mean colour above 30 Pt-Co units. In such lakes Secchi- and TP-based trophic indices were not computed, because "phosphorus is tied up with the color producing compounds, such as humic acids". Secchi-based TSI was restricted to lakes of colour ≤ 10 units; the OCR reads "TO", almost certainly 10. Chlorophyll-based TSI = 77.7 − 27.3 log(mean chl a). (full text) — [Cowing & Scott 1980, USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri8069)
- [ME] Maine DEP late-summer baseline sampling measures true colour and DOC (as well as TP, chl a and ions) on more than 1,400 of Maine's 5,785 lakes. (snippet) — [Maine DEP lake data](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/lake/lakedata.htm)
- [ME, 30-yr fall DOC trends] All 29 high-elevation lakes had positive DOC trends (19, or 65%, significant). Of 16 low-elevation lakes, 7 (44%) increased, 2 decreased and 7 were unchanged. (snippet of abstract) — [Gavin et al., UMaine Mitchell Center abstract](https://umaine.edu/mitchellcenter/?p=14118)
- [NE-US] Adirondack DOC rose about 0.5 mg L⁻¹ per decade over 1994–2012, and two long-term lakes rose 0.62 and 0.69 mg L⁻¹ per decade. Iron absorbs light without contributing DOC, which alters the colour:DOC ratio. (full text) — [Rose & Stetler 2020, NALMS LakeLine 40(1)](https://www.nalms.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/40-1-4.pdf)
- [NE-US, hundreds of lakes] Most lakes increased in DOC and colour, but trends were spatially incoherent. Colour trends were more variable than DOC trends and only weakly correlated with them. (abstract) — [Lapierre et al. 2021, Ecosphere 12:e03415](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70222098)

**Light attenuation**
- [65 lakes, AK/CO/PA/Argentina] DOC explained 87–96% of among-lake variation in Kd. On average, dissolved absorbance accounted for 33% of Kd for PAR and 68% for 305 nm UV. UV Kd follows a univariate power model in DOC. (abstract) — [Morris et al. 1995, L&O 40:1381](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1995.40.8.1381)
- [EU, 75 boreal Norwegian lakes; DOC 0.25–12.3, median 5.9 mg L⁻¹] log(Kd_PAR) = 0.24 log(TP) + 0.45 log(DOC), R² = 0.67, with TP in µg L⁻¹ and DOC in mg L⁻¹. This means Kd ∝ DOC^0.45 × TP^0.24. CDOM absorbed on average 56.3% of PAR (36.9–76.2%) and phytoplankton pigments 6.6%. Areal GPP was 45–993 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹ (median 286), lowered by DOC through shading. Examples: DOC 0.57 mg L⁻¹ with Secchi 10 m; DOC 11.1 mg L⁻¹ with Secchi 1.8 m. (full text) — [Thrane, Hessen & Andersen 2014, Ecosystems](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10021-014-9776-2)
- [ON, 21 Shield lakes] Midsummer Kd ranged from under 0.2 to over 2.5 m⁻¹. In non-eutrophic Shield lakes, transparency is controlled by DOC. Lower DOC would deepen epilimnia by 1–2 m in small lakes (under 500 ha), with no such effect in large lakes. (abstract) — [Fee et al. 1996, L&O 41:912](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1996.41.5.0912)

**Thermal structure**
- [north-temperate small lakes; colour 1.5–19.8 m⁻¹]
  - Epilimnion depth went from 2.5 m in the clearest lake to 0.75 m in the most coloured; colour explained 91% of the variance.
  - Summer morning epilimnion temperature was about 2 °C cooler in the most coloured lake.
  - The diel range was 1.4 ± 0.7 °C in clear lakes and 2.1 ± 1.0 °C in the most coloured lake.
  - Whole-lake heat gain fell with colour, and mean epilimnetic irradiance was lower.
  - (abstract) — [Houser 2006, CJFAS 63:2447](https://doi.org/10.1139/f06-131)
- [ON, 86 lakes under 500 ha] Clear lakes (DOC < 2 mg L⁻¹) lost cold-water (<10 °C) volume in warm years. Coloured lakes (the abstract prints "DOC < 4", evidently meaning > 4 mg L⁻¹) had more stable deep temperatures. "Clear lakes exhibit large thermal changes in response to small differences in DOC." (abstract) — [Snucins & Gunn 2000, L&O 45:1639](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2000.45.7.1639)

### Inferences
- **DOC source term.** Catchment DOC load = area × export coefficient × runoff timing. Use about 2 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ for upland forest; 5–15 for wetland-rich catchments (the upper Hubbard Brook range); plus a near-shore leaf-leachate term in October–November (§2).
- Deliver DOC with discharge: a spring snowmelt pulse (April–May in Maine), autumn storms, and winter rain-on-snow events (about 20% of the annual load in Vermont). Concentrations rise only modestly at high flow (Bear Brook), so load scales mainly with water volume.
- **Light.** Replace a fixed background Kd with Kd = Kd_w × (DOC/DOC_ref)^0.45 × (TP/TP_ref)^0.24, the Thrane form. Calibrate so that a Maine "moderately coloured" lake gives Kd ≈ 0.5–1.0 m⁻¹ and Secchi ≈ 3–5 m, and a >30-unit lake about 1.5–2.5 m⁻¹. These are calibration targets, not sourced Maine values.
- Optionally keep a linear term per mg L⁻¹ for numerical simplicity, but tie it to the power law in the 3–8 mg L⁻¹ range.
- **Thermal coupling.** In the 4 m pond and a small lake, higher DOC should absorb shortwave nearer the surface. That gives a shallower, warmer-by-day mixed layer with larger diel swings, and a cooler, more stable hypolimnion. This follows Houser (0.75–2.5 m epilimnia) and Fee and Snucins & Gunn (1–2 m differences).
- **Coloured-lake P effect.** In lakes above about 30 Pt-Co units, part of the TP is complexed with humics and is not algal-available. A "humic-bound P" fraction that scales with DOC would reproduce Maine DEP's observation.
- **Trend.** Browning of about +0.5 mg L⁻¹ per decade is a candidate what-if driver for the sim.

### Gaps
- No Maine-wide distribution of lake DOC (mg L⁻¹) or colour (Pt-Co) was retrieved. The Maine DEP/Lake Stewards datasets (Gulf of Maine Research Institute catalogue) were found but not opened. The colour value for Hot Brook Lakes is unknown.
- No verified linear Kd-per-mg-DOC slope for PAR was found; Morris 1995's PAR model is in the full text, which was blocked. The Thrane power law is the sourced alternative.
- The storm-versus-baseflow split of annual DOC export in Maine (rather than Vermont) was not found.
- The web search budget ran out before Maine-specific DOC distributions could be pursued.

## 4. Pollen and other small subsidies (spring pine pollen)

### Takeaway
Conifer pollen is a real but brief late-spring nutrient pulse. At a 0.27 km² Ontario headwater lake it delivered 11–56 µmol P m⁻² d⁻¹ (0.34–1.7 mg P m⁻² d⁻¹) for about 45 days after ice-out, about 10 kg P yr⁻¹ in total. In mesocosms this measurably boosted phytoplankton, zooplankton and periphyton. Pine pollen is about 3 mg P g⁻¹, and much of its C, N and P leaches within days.

### Cited Findings
- [ON, ELA Lake 373, 0.27 km², jack pine] Pollen input was 11–56 µmol P m⁻² d⁻¹, measured with floating collectors on transects over the 45 days after ice-out. That totals about 10 kg P yr⁻¹. Ambient, 3× and 10× pollen additions in 1 m³ littoral mesocosms raised total phytoplankton and herbivorous zooplankton biomass, filamentous greens and large diatoms, and periphyton. "Conifer pollen subsidizes nutrient levels and promotes production in small boreal lakes." (abstract) — [Graham, Vinebrooke & Turner 2006, L&O 51:1524](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2006.51.3.1524)
- [ON, Dorset] Pollen deposition was 5,202–12,250 grains cm⁻² at three lakes (2014). White and red pine pollen contains about 3 mg P g⁻¹ and can be up to one-third of annual bulk P deposition. Bulk collectors also catch insects, bird droppings and leaves, which can double apparent P deposition. (snippet, thesis) — [Trent University thesis: Phosphorus deposition in forested watersheds: importance of pollen](https://batadora.trentu.ca/objects/etd-391)
- [EU, lab, *Pinus sylvestris*] Incubation of pollen raised SRP to 0.04 mg L⁻¹ and TDN to 1.5 mg L⁻¹ after 5 days. Leaching plus microbial degradation released at least 80% of pollen C, 40% of N and 50% of P. (snippet of abstract) — [Aquatic Sciences 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00027-011-0198-3)
- [MA, Cape Cod kettle ponds, pitch pine] "Large amounts of pollen" are deposited in late spring. Where influx is high, pollen is a short-term stimulatory nutrient subsidy in May–June. (snippet; hosting record uncertain) — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfe38b63b8185d9cafc55b)
- [App] Corn pollen used as an FPOM surrogate moved less than 10 m in streams except at high discharge. (full text) — [Webster et al. 1999](https://ww2.coastal.edu/jjhutche/Pubs/webster%20et%20al,%201999,%2041-%20687-705.pdf)
- [NH, Mirror Lake] Atmospheric fallout was a significant P source, "greatly exceeding stream-derived phosphorus loading" (Cole et al. 1990). (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Mechanism.** After ice-out and pine anthesis, roughly mid-May to late June in Maine, deposit a pollen flux on the lake surface. Make it higher near pine-forested shores and on the downwind side, and spread it over about 45 days.
  - Magnitude: about 10 kg P ÷ 0.27 km² ≈ 37 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹. That is comparable to, or larger than, Maine DEP's 16 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.16 kg ha⁻¹) atmospheric-deposition coefficient for open water, which plausibly already includes some pollen (§7).
  - At 3 mg P g⁻¹ this implies about 12 g pollen m⁻² per season, or about 0.3 g m⁻² d⁻¹ at peak.
- **Fate.** About 50% of pollen P and 40% of N leach to SRP and DIN within days. The rest goes to the sedOM and detritus pool.
- **Mass balance.** To keep the budget closed, either count pollen inside the atmospheric P term or add it explicitly and reduce the atmospheric term, to avoid double counting.
- **Render.** The visible "pollen slick" on the surface in late May–June is a candidate cosmetic effect.

### Gaps
- No pollen deposition measurement from Maine or a New England lake was found. Maine lakes have more white pine and spruce/fir and less jack pine than the ELA site, so magnitudes may differ.
- Other small subsidies (bird guano, catkins, seeds) were not quantified here.

## 5. Shading by shore trees: water temperature, light in the shallows, macrophytes and periphyton, width of the shaded band

### Takeaway
Under a closed deciduous canopy a small stream is about 97% shaded in summer. Stream primary production then becomes negligible in summer: a New Hampshire headwater had under 1% autochthonous input, mosses only and no algae. Algal production peaks instead in spring and autumn, when the canopy is open. Wooded banks mainly suppress daily maximum stream temperatures, by up to several °C. For lakes, no quantitative littoral shading or temperature data were found.

### Cited Findings
- [JP, deciduous forest stream 2–5 m wide] About 97% of stream width was shaded by canopy during the leafing period. Stream productivity peaks in the defoliated seasons (autumn and spring) and declines in summer as the canopy closes. (full text) — [Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)
- [NH, Bear Brook] Moss production was under 1% of energy input, and "algae and vascular hydrophytes are absent from the stream." (abstract) — [Fisher & Likens 1973](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942301)
- [EU, Sweden, under more than 70% conifer canopy] Mean PAR at the stream surface was 22.8–25.6 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ under canopy, versus 55.5–59.8 with experimental light addition. Added light raised grazer numbers but not chlorophyll a. Light affected young trout growth only in May–June. (full text) — [Erős et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3344874/)
- [NS] Leaf decomposition was exceptionally rapid in unshaded downstream reaches, where shredders congregated, compared with shaded tributaries. (abstract) — [Hydrobiologia 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10750-016-3001-6)
- [review, temperate] Wooded riparian zones reduce spring and summer stream temperatures, with a larger effect on maxima than on means. (abstract) — [Environmental Evidence systematic review 2012](https://environmentalevidencejournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/2047-2382-1-3)
- [N. America, 11 paired sites, June–Sept 2011] Forested streams had over 90% canopy and open streams under 10%. Daily maxima in open reaches were up to 8.4 °C higher, and maximum temperature rose 0.34–2.9 °C from the upstream to the downstream end of open reaches. Groundwater and microhabitat can outweigh canopy effects. (snippet of conference abstract) — [ESA 2012 abstract](https://eco.confex.com/eco/2012/webprogram/Paper35550.html)
- Forested sites were cooler, fluctuated less daily and had lower daily maxima than open sites, fairly uniformly across vegetation and elevation. (snippet, news summary) — [Fondriest summary of continental study](https://fondriest.com/news/?p=18836)

### Inferences
- **Stream (Hot Brook).** Give the stream a canopy-openness factor tied to leaf phenology: about 0.03–0.1 light transmission from leaf-out (late May) to leaf-fall (October), and about 0.5–0.8 in leafless months, allowing for conifers and bank trees. This gives spring and autumn algal and periphyton peaks and a light-limited summer, matching Nakano and Bear Brook.
- Water temperature: cap the summer daily maximum about 2–5 °C below the open-water equivalent, with a smaller effect on daily means. Hot Brook's deep pools probably also get groundwater buffering.
- **Lake littoral.** A shaded band about one tree-height wide (about 15–25 m for mature Maine forest; unsourced) lies along shores that face the sun. On a north shore the trees shade the land, not the water. This lowers light reaching macrophytes and periphyton within that band in summer.
- No sourced °C effect exists for the lake littoral. With open fetch and mixing it is probably small (under 1 °C) except in sheltered coves; this is an inference.

### Gaps
- No studies were found quantifying shoreline-tree shading of lake littoral light, temperature or macrophyte and periphyton growth, or the width of the shaded band.
- No Maine/New England stream canopy–temperature pairs were found. The paired-site study above is continental, its °C values are snippets, and a peer-reviewed version was not retrieved.
- The web search budget ran out before lake-shading literature (e.g., riparian clear-cut effects on littoral periphyton) could be pursued.

## 6. Aquatic → terrestrial exports: emergent insect flux from lakes, seasonality, consumption by birds, bats and spiders (vertebrate predators only briefly)

### Takeaway
Lake benthic secondary production has a median of about 2.3 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. About 70% of it is insects, and about 30% of insect production emerges. That puts typical lake emergence on the order of 0.3–0.5 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, about 0.7–1.1 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹. Measured values span 0.15–3.7 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹ (Mývatn) and up to 1.8 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ (Lake Constance littoral). Emergence is chironomid-dominated, temperature-driven and peaks in spring and early summer (sometimes bimodal). 70–99% of emerged insects stay on land, mostly within about 100 m of shore. Spiders, swallows, bats and riparian birds take 25–100% of their energy or carbon from this flux.

### Cited Findings
**Emergence magnitude**
- [literature synthesis] Zoobenthic production is 1.46 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ in lakes versus 6.67 in streams, and the emergence:production ratio is 0.30 in lakes versus 0.19 in streams. Lake radius exceeds stream width, so flux per metre of shoreline is about 2.5× greater for lakes. Deposition within 100 m of shore is 0.021 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ for a 10-ha lake and 0.002 for a 4-m stream. Deposition declines inland as a negative exponential or inverse power. (abstract) — [Gratton & Vander Zanden 2009, Ecology 90:2689](https://doi.org/10.1890/08-1546.1)
- [49 whole-lake estimates] Benthic secondary production was right-skewed with a median of 2.3 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Insects made up 0.70 ± 0.33 of it, and the emerging fraction of insect production was 0.30 ± 0.04. Areal emergence is independent of lake area. (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)
- [IS, Mývatn, eutrophic] Midge emergence was 0.15–3.7 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹ (2008–2011). Peak deposition was 20–25 m inland, with 70% within 100 m. In a high year, deposition within the first 50 m was 100 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, carrying 10 kg N and 1 kg P ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, 3–5× background N deposition. Midges are about 10% N and 1% P by dry mass. 75–99% (70–99%) of aquatic insects that disperse to land never return to the water, and at least 50% of individuals (the males) stay on land. (full text) — [Dreyer et al. 2015, Ecology 96:499](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Dreyer_15_Ecology_quantifying-insect-deposition.pdf)
- [EU, three south German lakes] Chironomidae dominated emergence in all three lakes. Patterns were unimodal or bimodal, with peaks in spring, summer or early autumn depending on the lake. Water temperature explained the variation in emergence; nutrients did not. Up to 1.8 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ emerged from the Lake Constance littoral. Aquatic insects were at least 17% of aerial arthropods at the shore. (abstract) — [Salvarina, Gravier & Rothhaupt 2017, Limnologica 62:47](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/a38a7017-821a-4130-97a6-56ebdbc696b2/full)
- [JP, stream] Aquatic insect emergence was greatest around bud break (May–June) and low through winter. Emergence peaked in spring, when terrestrial invertebrate biomass was low. (full text) — [Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)
- [review, streams] Emergence "typically peaks in early summer in the temperate zone", with a low-level flux from autumn to spring in ice-free streams. It "declines exponentially with distance from the stream edge". (abstract) — [Baxter et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2004.01328.x)
- [CA, Sierra Nevada] Lakes with introduced trout had about 20× less aquatic insect emergence (Finlay & Vredenburg 2007). Removing trout raised emergence (Pope et al. 2009). (full text) — [Vander Zanden & Gratton 2011](https://jvzlab.limnology.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/104/2022/10/Vander-ZandenGratton_11_CJFAS_blowin.pdf)

**Who eats it on land**
- [review] Emergent insects contribute 25–100% of the energy or carbon of riparian birds, bats, lizards and spiders. (abstract) — [Baxter et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2004.01328.x)
- [JP] Aquatic insects supplied 25.6% of the annual energy budget of the forest bird assemblage (winter wren 98.2%, great tit 38.6%, flycatchers about 32%). During the leafless season they supplied 50–90% of the monthly budget for about half of the year-round species. (full text) — [Nakano & Murakami 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC14562)
- [OH, Scioto River] Shares of energy from aquatic primary producers: shoreline tetragnathid spiders 50%, raccoons 48%, adult damselflies 44%, riparian swallows 41%. (abstract) — [Kautza & Sullivan 2016, Ecology](https://doi.org/10.1890/15-1095.1)
- [EU, stream and lake] Spider diets were 17–99% aquatic: web-builders about 78%, ground hunters about 42%, vegetation hunters about 31%. Horizontal web-builders (*Tetragnatha*) average about 70% aquatic. Use of aquatic prey falls with distance from water and follows emergence peaks. (snippet) — [Sci. Rep. 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-023-50420-w); [PMC10746743](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10746743/)
- [Alberta, river] Aquatic insects supplied 25% of spider C and 36% of spider N, falling from about 50–55% at 1 m from the river to about 0% at 30 m. (snippet) — [U. Lethbridge thesis](https://opus.uleth.ca/items/12f5cd3e-28fa-494a-96dc-9d37220c466c/full)

### Inferences
- **Export mechanism in the sim.** The current benthic insects emerge at the surface and fish eat them. Every adult that is not eaten should leave the lake and become a sink in the N and P ledger at about 10% N and 1% P by dry mass. Optionally, about 1–25% of females return to oviposit, which recycles a small fraction (eggs) back into the lake. Without this sink, the closed budget silently keeps nutrients that should be leaving.
- **Magnitude and phenology targets.** Annual emergence ≈ 0.3 × insect production, about 0.5–2 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹ for a Maine mesotrophic lake littoral, and less from the profundal. Spread it as a temperature-triggered peak in late May–June (chironomids, mayflies at about 12–18 °C), a smaller mid- to late-summer pulse (chironomids, caddis, damselflies and dragonflies), and near zero under ice.
  - Daily: about 1.8 g ÷ 150 days ≈ 12 mg m⁻² d⁻¹ on average from a productive littoral, with 3–5× peaks.
  - Order-of-magnitude check: emergence (about 1 g dry m⁻² yr⁻¹ ≈ 10 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹) is comparable to the lake's atmospheric P coefficient (16 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹, §7). So for a small lake it is not negligible in the P budget.
- **Terrestrial predation on emergers.** If the sim models emergence-time predation by swallows, bats and spiders, that loss happens after the insect has left the lake. For lake budgets it is already "exported", so terrestrial predators need not be simulated explicitly.

### Gaps
- No New England lake emergence time series (mg m⁻² d⁻¹ by month) were found. The values come from Germany, Iceland and literature syntheses.
- No fraction of emergence eaten by bats or tree swallows, as a share of flux, was found.
- Fish and amphibian removal by terrestrial predators was left to the birds/mammals researcher, as instructed.

## 7. Nutrient loading from forested Maine catchments: P and N export coefficients; storm vs baseflow delivery

### Takeaway
Maine DEP's lake phosphorus TMDL / PCAP coefficients:

| Source | kg TP ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (range) |
|---|---|
| Undeveloped forest | 0.04 (0.01–0.04), based on the New England Hubbard Brook work (Likens et al. 1977) |
| Passively managed forest | 0.02 |
| Wetlands | 0.10 (0.10–0.20) |
| Harvested ("operated") forest | 0.40 |
| Atmospheric deposition on open water | 0.16 (0.11–0.21) |
| Shoreline residential lots | 0.25–2.7 |
| Camp roads | 2.0 |
| Cropland | 1.5 |

DEP explicitly frames watershed P delivery as happening "during storm events and springtime snow melting". Event sampling in forested catchments roughly doubles annual P estimates compared with routine sampling. No Maine forest N export coefficient was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- [ME, Webber Pond PCAP-TMDL, Maine DEP 2003] Land-use TP coefficients, in kg TP ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹:
  - non-developed (forest) land 0.04, range 0.01–0.04, "based on a New England regional study (Likens et al 1977)";
  - inactive/passively managed forest 0.02 (0.00–0.05);
  - wetlands 0.10 (0.10–0.20);
  - operated (harvested) forest land 0.40, from the Cobbossee Lake TMDL (Monagle 1995);
  - row crops 1.50; manured hayland 0.65; low-intensity hayland 0.35; pasture 0.81 (Reckhow et al. 1980; Dennis & Sage 1981);
  - shoreline residential 0.25–2.70 (Dennis et al. 1992, algal-available P); private camp roads 2.00;
  - atmospheric deposition on open water 0.16 (0.11–0.21).
  - Coefficients for roads and shoreline residential were reduced to reflect algal-available P.
  - Source: (full text) [Maine DEP Webber Pond PCAP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/tmdl/2003/tmdlwebberrep.pdf)
- [ME, same report] Forest was 47.6% of the direct watershed but supplied only 9.3% (49 kg) of a 526 kg yr⁻¹ external load. Open water (19% of area) received 78 kg (15%) from the atmosphere. Internal sediment P release (537 kg) was comparable to the whole external watershed load. The land-use assessment targeted sources that "run off from land areas during storm events and springtime snow melting". (full text) — [Maine DEP Webber Pond PCAP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/tmdl/2003/tmdlwebberrep.pdf)
- [ME] Other DEP PCAPs, such as Threecornered Pond, report watershed totals by land use (e.g., 196 kg P yr⁻¹) using the same coefficient approach. (snippet) — [Maine DEP Threecornered Pond PCAP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/tmdl/2003/tmdl3corneredrep.pdf)
- [ME, USGS] In coloured lakes (over 30 Pt-Co), P is tied up with humic compounds and less biologically available. (full text) — [Cowing & Scott 1980, USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/wri8069)
- [NH, Livermore Cove Brook, about 1.77 km², about 92% forest, Squam Lake tributary] Stream P rose quickly at the onset of each of three rain events, stayed high for a few hours around peak flow, and subsided with flow. Dissolved organic P dominated both baseflow and event flow. (snippet) — [Plymouth State, P transport to Squam Lake during storm events](https://plymouth.edu/sites/default/files/media/2023-12/phosphorus-transport-to-squam-lake-during-storm-events.pdf)
- [EU, Germany, 21 ha forested catchment] Routine weekly to biweekly sampling gave mean TP 8 µg L⁻¹ and 19–44 g ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. Including events raised the annual export to up to 83 g ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, so routine sampling "will substantially underestimate P exports". (snippet of abstract) — [TU Dresden, Forests 2017](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/en/publications/quantification-of-phosphorus-exports-from-a-small-forested-headwatercatchment-in-the-eastern-ore-mountains-germany(dfd324b2-86a6-439b-a029-5f5b0ed9c388).html)
- [VT, Missisquoi, mixed land use] 82% of annual dissolved P and 74% of reactive soluble P were delivered during spring runoff in 2014. (snippet; hosting record uncertain) — [UVM thesis](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/items/4534b322-7c1d-4bbf-8777-b4c430a0d6d7)
- [VT] About 20% of annual nitrate and DOC loads moved in winter rain-on-snow events. (snippet; hosting record uncertain) — [UVM record](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/hcoltheses/211)
- [ME, Bear Brook Watershed] West Bear still retained over 80% of N after more than 20 years of experimental (NH₄)₂SO₄ addition. N retention in the reference East Bear increased after 3 years, as at many northeastern watersheds. (snippet of 2010 BBWM review; authors not confirmed) — [EMAS 2010 BBWM special issue](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10661-010-1527-z)
- [MD, for contrast] Literature forest P export coefficients reach a maximum of about 0.83 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, the upper tail of national compilations. (snippet) — [Wickham & Wade 2002](https://www.gulfofmaine.org/2/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/wickham-comp_elect_ag-2002.pdf)
- [NH] Hubbard Brook litterfall cycles 140 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ of 11 elements internally, with N the largest. Nutrient-budget studies show only small losses from the ecosystem. (abstract) — [Gosz et al. 1972](https://doi.org/10.2307/1934293)
- Insect-mediated P transfer, for scale [EU]: bleak moved 432 g P yr⁻¹ from land into a 0.12 km² lake, about 3.6 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹. (abstract) — [Mehner et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2005.50.6.2022)

### Inferences
- **Replace the constant nutrient inflow** with land-use-weighted export coefficients delivered on a hydrograph. For the Hot Brook landscape, mostly forest with wetlands, use:
  - TP_forest = 0.04 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ = 4 mg P m⁻² of catchment yr⁻¹;
  - TP_wetland = 0.10–0.20 (10–20 mg m⁻² yr⁻¹);
  - harvested patches 0.40 (40 mg m⁻² yr⁻¹) for a few years after cutting;
  - shoreline camps and roads 0.25–2.0;
  - direct atmospheric 0.16 kg ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ (16 mg P m⁻² yr⁻¹) on the water surface.
- **Timing.** Deliver about 50–80% of annual P with spring snowmelt (April) and storm events, and the rest as baseflow at low concentration. Mostly the dissolved organic P form in forested streams (NH). Event weighting is roughly 2× what baseflow alone would suggest (Germany, Vermont).
- **Relative sizes for a small Maine pond with a forested catchment about 10× its area:**
  - catchment P ≈ 10 × 4 = 40 mg P m⁻² of lake yr⁻¹;
  - atmospheric ≈ 16;
  - pollen ≈ up to about 37 (partly inside the atmospheric figure);
  - leaf litter, of order 10⁰–10¹ mg P per m² of near-shore water at about 0.05–0.1% P in litter (unsourced P concentration);
  - terrestrial insects eaten ≈ about 1–4;
  - emergence export ≈ −5 to −15.
  - So the land subsidies other than streamflow (pollen, litter, insects) are a meaningful fraction of external P in a small oligotrophic lake, consistent with Gasith & Hasler and Mehner on lake-size dependence.
- **N.** Without a sourced Maine coefficient, a placeholder of about 1–3 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ for forested catchments is plausible given high retention (more than 80% at Bear Brook). It is unsourced here and must be verified before use.
