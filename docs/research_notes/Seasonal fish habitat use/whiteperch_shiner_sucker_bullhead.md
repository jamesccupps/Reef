# Seasonal and diel habitat use: white perch, golden shiner, white sucker, brown bullhead (northern lakes and ponds)

Scope: where these four species are by season and by time of day in northern lakes (Maine, New England, Ontario, upper Midwest), with depths, temperatures and O₂ levels. The goal is to design mechanisms that make the patterns emerge and to write validation checks. Searched 2026-10-15.

**Source labels.**
- **[sci]**: peer-reviewed paper or thesis.
- **[agency]**: state or provincial fisheries agency document, including MDIFW lake-survey sheets and management plans.
- **[angler]**: angling magazine or forum.
- **[secondary]**: an encyclopedia or database that compiles other sources (Animal Diversity Web, Ontario LHD, Fishes of Texas).
- **[snippet]**: I saw the source only through a search-engine summary because the page was blocked or gone. Treat these as leads, not facts.

**Units.** Converted from the source: 1 ft = 0.305 m; °F → °C.

## 1. White perch (*Morone americana*), landlocked lake populations

### Takeaway
- **Habitat.** An open-water schooling fish of level, fine-sediment bottoms. It does not use vegetation or structure for shelter. Depth itself is its daytime refuge.
- **Summer.** A diel onshore–offshore and vertical migration: about 4–9 m by day, about 1 m inshore at night. Feeding peaks in low light (dusk, dawn, overcast days).
- **Autumn and early winter.** Schools gather in shallow weed edges, then move to the deepest basin by midwinter.
- **Under ice.** Active and feeding all winter in large, roaming, compact schools. They hold near the bottom or suspend over basins: usually 12–18 m in deep lakes (to 40 m), about 4.6–7.6 m in ponds, and in the deepest hole of shallow lakes.
- **Late winter.** Activity drops in midwinter as O₂ falls and picks up again with March runoff.
- **Winter O₂ is the limiting condition.** MDIFW says perch thrive in Maine lakes of about 20 ha or more and 7.6 m or more deep, provided deep water is not O₂-depleted in winter.
- **Spawning.** In spring, in very shallow water (0–1.5 m in lakes) over any bottom, or up tributaries. MDIFW puts it at about 15 °C; Maine thesis data say 18–21 °C (a disagreement). Egg release peaks at dusk.
- **Thermal preference.** Warm-water. Final preferendum is about 29–31 °C, and the preferred temperature tracks acclimation: about 5 °C in February, up to 32 °C in summer.

### Cited Findings

#### Habitat type and summer diel migration
- **Substrate.** White perch prefer fairly level bottoms of compact silt, mud, sand or clay. In Sebasticook Lake, Maine, they used soft muck, decomposing organic bottom, gravel and rock less (AuClair 1956, M.S. thesis, Univ. Maine) — [sci, Stanley & Danie 1983, FWS/OBS-82/11.7, Maine Coop. Unit](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf) ([USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/fwsobs82_11_7))
- **Cover and depth.** They do not depend on vegetation, rocks, debris or structures for shelter, and are commonly found in open water. Depth may give daytime shelter in both estuarine and freshwater habitats — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)
- **Summer diel migration.** Fish have been observed moving each day from shallow water (0.9–1.2 m) at night to deeper water (4.0–9.0 m) in daylight during summer. Marine populations behave similarly: onshore at night, offshore at dawn — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)
  - The light-dependence evidence is Sheri & Power 1969 (Can. Field-Nat. 83:160–161), "Vertical distribution of white perch … modified by [light?]". The last word is illegible in the scan's reference list, and I did not see the paper.
- **Lake populations (NJ agency).**
  - They feed day and night but are generally more active in low light.
  - Freshwater and saltwater populations spend the day in deeper water and move to shallows and inshore water at night.
  - They prefer open water and avoid cover, though they are found near bridges and submerged logs.
  - They school from youth through adulthood.

  — [agency, NJ Fish & Wildlife Digest 2012, Gordon](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/njfw/digest-marine-2012-feisty-white-perch-by-maryellen-gordon.pdf)
- **Maine lake, dusk feeding.** At Northeast Pond (315 ha, max 14.9 m, 25 °C surface in August), white perch are commonly caught in numbers out in the open lake at dusk with bait — [agency, MDIFW lake survey, Northeast Pond (1966, rev. 1990)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/york/northeast_pond.pdf)
- **Maine lake, summer deep hole.** Fifth Machias Lake (433 ha, max 8.2 m) was weakly stratified in August: surface 23.3 °C, 15 °C at 7 m. The deep hole near the outlet is described as a great summer perch spot — [agency, MDIFW lake survey, Fifth Machias Lake (1961, rev. 2001)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/fifth_machias_lake.pdf)
- **Surface schooling in Maine.** White perch schooling or feeding at the surface, sometimes with dorsal fins out of the water, is a recognised sight — [agency, MDIFW White Perch Management Plan (Smith 2002)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- **Summer range of movement.** Summer movements are local and random, rarely more than 19 km. That figure comes from estuarine studies (Mansueti 1964; Hardy 1978) — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)
- **Season changes diel behaviour (estuary telemetry).**
  - Adults in the Carmans River, NY moved upstream and downstream each day.
  - The frequency of diel movement was 13.8% in winter and 49.4–57.5% in summer. Low temperature and high salinity reduced it.
  - Nightly downstream movements were preferred in winter.

  — [sci, TAFS 143(5), 2014, *Diel behavior in white perch revealed using acoustic telemetry*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00028487.2014.938193) (abstract, via search)
- **Day trawl avoidance in clearer water.** In western Lake Erie after dreissenid mussels arrived, white perch (with yellow perch and trout-perch) had lower daytime than nighttime bottom-trawl catchability. The authors attribute this to greater daytime trawl avoidance as the water cleared. This is a gear-and-light effect, not necessarily a depth shift — [sci, USGS, effects of dreissenids on monitoring of western Lake Erie fisheries](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/effects-dreissenids-monitoring-and-management-fisheries-western-lake-erie) [abstract]
- **Ontario LHD summary.**
  - Habitat: "shallows of lakes and bays … usually over mud substrate".
  - Benthopelagic, schooling, tolerant of turbidity.
  - Preferred temperature 26–30 °C; spawning at 11–16 °C in May–June.

  — [secondary, Ontario Freshwater Fishes LHD](https://ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=138)

#### Temperature preference and avoidance
- **Final preferendum.** Freshwater Wicomico River (MD) fish acclimated at 6–33 °C gave a final preferendum of 28.9, 29.3 and 30.6 °C by three methods — [sci, Hall, Hocutt & Stauffer 1979, Estuaries 2:129](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/temperature-preference-of-the-white-perch-morone-americana-collec-2/)
- **Acclimation-dependent preference** — [sci review, Fischer, Hall & Sullivan 1989, *Synthesis of water quality … data for white perch*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CZIC-sh171-s94-1989/html/CZIC-sh171-s94-1989.htm):
  - Juveniles: final preferences of 15.2–31.0 °C depending on acclimation.
  - Adults acclimated at 5, 8 and 10 °C preferred 23, 22.8 and 21.5 °C (brackish water).
  - Field-collected adults preferred about 32.2 °C maximum in summer and about 5 °C minimum in February (Meldrim & Gift 1971).
  - Hudson River adults preferred 27.8 °C.
- **Avoidance** (same synthesis):
  - Hudson River adults avoided water below 9.5 °C and above 34.5 °C.
  - February fish acclimated at 1.1 °C avoided 6.7 °C; August fish acclimated at 25 °C avoided 35 °C.
  - Adults avoided water below 35% O₂ saturation at 8–21 °C (Meldrim et al. 1974).
- **Maine thermal note.** Maine perch "do better" in waters reaching 75 °F (23.9 °C) or more in summer — [agency, MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)

#### Autumn
- In late fall and early winter, perch gather in shallow weeds. As midwinter approaches they move to the nearest deep pool or basin — [agency, NJ F&W Digest 2012](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/njfw/digest-marine-2012-feisty-white-perch-by-maryellen-gordon.pdf)
- In estuaries, juveniles leave the shoals as water cools in August and overwinter in deep pools of creeks or deep river and bay water. Adults show similar temperature-driven seasonal movements — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)

#### Winter under ice
- **Overwintering depth.** White perch overwinter at depths to 40 m, but usually at 12.2–18.3 m (AuClair 1956, Sebasticook Lake, Maine; Sheri & Power 1969, Bay of Quinte, Lake Ontario) — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)
- **Lake requirement (Maine).** Perch can survive, and often thrive, in most Maine lakes and ponds of about 50 acres (20 ha) or more and about 25 ft (7.6 m) or more deep, if the deeper water is not depleted of O₂ in winter — [agency, MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- **Ice fishing is a major activity.**
  - Ice anglers caught 0.88 white perch per angler-day in 1996 and 0.76 in 1999. Open-water anglers caught 1.35 and 1.30.
  - Most perch waters are open to winter fishing from first ice to the end of March.

  — [agency, MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- **Shallow mixed Maine lake with a winter fishery.** Horseshoe Lake, Hancock Co. (82 ha, max 6.1 m) was isothermal in August (22.2 °C at the surface, 21.7 °C at 6.1 m). It is light-use in open water but "quite popular" with ice anglers as a white perch lake. Evidence that winter perch are caught in lakes with no basin deeper than about 6 m — [agency, MDIFW lake survey, Horseshoe Lake (1960, rev. 1988)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/hancock/horseshoe_lake.pdf)
- **Angler location guidance (New England)** — [angler, On The Water, *Ice fishing for white perch in New England*](https://onthewater.com/ice-fishing-for-white-perch); [same text](https://onthewater.com/ice-fishing-strategies-winter-white-perch):
  - Depths and structure:
    - Start at about 30 ft (9 m) in large lakes and work shallower.
    - In small ponds, basins may be only about 15 ft (4.6 m) deep; start at the deepest spot.
    - Target basins, inside turns and steep breaks. Perch congregate over basins because of plankton and baitfish.
  - Position in the water:
    - Expect fish suspended, but not always; for example, suspended 12 ft (3.7 m) off the bottom in 40 ft (12 m).
    - If a school vanishes, fish on the bottom.
  - School movement:
    - Schools are "constantly on the move" and corral baitfish into inside turns.
    - If the bite stops for more than about 15 min, the school has moved.
  - Timing:
    - Bites peak at dawn, dusk and the last hour of daylight on sunny days.
    - On overcast or snowy days the bite runs all day; midday is slower.
  - Season:
    - Early ice is good (O₂ high, regular feeding, 25–30 fish per trip).
    - Midwinter brings lower O₂ and colder water, sluggish fish and fewer than a dozen per trip.
    - Mid-March, with oxygen-rich rain and runoff, is the most aggressive feeding of the year.
  - Named waters: Sebago Lake (ME), Lake Champlain (VT), Lake Winnipesaukee (NH), Wachusett Reservoir (MA).
- **NJ ice depth.** Ice-fishing perch tend to congregate at about 15–25 ft (4.6–7.6 m) and spend more time in dense schools hunting food in late winter and early spring — [agency, NJ F&W Digest 2012](https://dep.nj.gov/wp-content/uploads/njfw/digest-marine-2012-feisty-white-perch-by-maryellen-gordon.pdf)
- **Deep lakes** [angler, low confidence]:
  - A forum angler on Lake Champlain reports white perch through the ice at 30–90 ft (9–27 m), sometimes on the bottom and sometimes right under the ice — [snippet; iceshanty.com thread, page blocked by bot-check](https://iceshanty.com/threads/white-perch.23030/)
  - A Lake Winnipesaukee report suggests jigging at about 33 ft (10 m) — [snippet; On The Water NH/ME report, Feb 2018 (attribution not verified)](https://onthewater.com/fishing-reports/2018/02/new-hampshire-and-maine-fishing-report-february-8-2018)

#### Spring spawning
- **Maine (agency).**
  - Spawning is mainly in spring at about 59–60 °F (15–15.6 °C), in shallow water over almost any bottom. Eggs hatch in 4–5 days at that temperature.
  - Year-class failures come only from major weather-induced mortality at spawning time.
  - People travel to watch the heavy spawning runs of white perch in streams.

  — [agency, MDIFW White Perch Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf)
- **Depths, timing and behaviour** — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf):
  - Spawning depth is under 7 m in general: 0–1.5 m in lakes (Hardy 1978) and 0–3.7 m in marshes (Scott & Crossman 1973).
  - Freshwater populations spawn April–May; spawning was most intense in June for freshwater fish in New York.
  - Ripe adults gather in groups of hundreds. Egg release lasts 10–21 days, with peaks at dusk and after rain.
  - After spawning, they may seek deeper water.
- **Disagreement on spawning temperature.** In Maine lakes, the temperature had to stay at 18–21 °C for several days before spawning (AuClair 1956) — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf). Other values: MDIFW about 15 °C; Ontario LHD 11–16 °C; Chesapeake 12–14 °C.
- **Juvenile nurseries.** Inshore areas of lakes. Juveniles in lakes have been described at 2.4–3.6 m — [Stanley & Danie 1983](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_white_perch_0199.pdf)

#### Low O₂
- In hypoxia (≤40% saturation), juvenile routine metabolism rose up to ×4, growth fell ×3 and consumption fell ×2 — [sci, Hanks & Secor 2011, Mar. Biol.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00227-010-1608-0)
- Young-of-year had 60% survival after 19 h at 0.5–1.0 mg/L (6.7–28.3 °C). The synthesis's own table instead lists this as 40% mortality, so the two figures conflict — [Fischer et al. 1989](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CZIC-sh171-s94-1989/html/CZIC-sh171-s94-1989.htm)

### Inferences
- **Summer diel cycle.** Daytime depth (4–9 m) against nighttime shallows (about 1 m) is a light–predation–foraging trade-off. The sources state that depth is the daytime refuge and that feeding peaks in low light.
  - In a **stratified** lake, the daytime band of 4–9 m will usually sit in the warm epilimnion or metalimnion above a hypoxic hypolimnion. White perch avoid water below about 35% saturation and are warm-preferring (inferred).
  - In a **3–6 m mixed** lake, no 4–9 m stratum exists. Expect daytime schools over the deepest open bottom (the basin floor or suspended mid-water offshore) and nighttime spreading onto 1 m flats.
- **Winter.** The move to the deepest basin matches seeking the warmest water under inverse stratification (about 4 °C at the bottom against about 0 °C under the ice), plus following plankton and baitfish that concentrate there.
  - In a shallow lake, the deepest basin may be only 4.6–6 m deep, and fish still use it (Horseshoe Lake).
  - The midwinter slump and the March pickup match a late-winter O₂ decline followed by meltwater re-oxygenation.
  - If bottom O₂ falls below the avoidance level, temperature preference (go deep) and O₂ avoidance (go up) conflict. The angler reports of perch "right under the ice" in some winters are consistent with O₂ winning (inference).
- **Schools under ice** are large, compact, mobile foraging units. Residence at a spot is minutes to hours (anglers wait about 15 min before moving).
- **Spawning** is a mass, communal, shallow-water event with dusk peaks. Lake fish either spawn on the shoreline (0–1.5 m) or run up inlets.

### Gaps
- No lake telemetry or hydroacoustic study of white perch day/night depth was found. The 4–9 m and 0.9–1.2 m figures come from older sources compiled in a 1983 profile.
- No measured under-ice depth or position data for a shallow (≤6 m) mixed Maine lake.
- No lethal O₂ for adults in winter.
- AuClair 1956 (the Sebasticook Lake thesis, the key Maine source) was not seen directly.

## 2. Golden shiner (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*)

### Takeaway
- **Habitat.** Quiet, weedy, mud-bottomed littoral water of lakes and ponds; also found to about 10 m.
- **Summer days.** Schools hold in the littoral zone and weed beds.
- **Dusk.** Schools break up and individuals move into open water to eat zooplankton (mostly *Daphnia*) as it rises.
- **Feeding.** Concentrated within about 1 h after sunset, minimal through the night, some at dawn.
- **Age differences.** Young-of-year feed by day; adults are more crepuscular. Where flying insects are the food, shiners feed by day.
- **Winter.** Extremely hypoxia-tolerant: survived a week or more below 0.5 ppm, and at 0.2–0.3 ppm. In a winterkill lake they crowd at the surface of ice holes, at inlets, and wherever O₂ remains.
- **Spawning.** Late spring–summer, above about 20 °C, scattering adhesive eggs over submerged vegetation in quiet water.

### Cited Findings

#### Habitat
- **Maine.** Quiet water of lakes and sluggish streams, "preferring thick vegetation and mud bottoms". Spawns in late spring–early summer by scattering adhesive eggs over beds of submerged vegetation in quiet water. Lives up to 5 years and matures at 2–3. It is Maine's most popular baitfish for culture — [agency, MDIFW Non-sport/Commercial Fish Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- **Depth.** Occupies "deep-water habitats" to about 10 m, including vegetated lakes, ponds and swamps. Larvae of 10–30 mm school in vegetated littoral areas; 5–10 mm larvae stay at the surface at the edge of the littoral zone (Faber 1980, Lac Heney, Quebec) — [secondary, Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Notemigonus_crysoleucas/)
- **Weed cover.** Found "anywhere there is still water and a little weed cover"; travels in large shoals — [secondary, UW Center for Limnology blog 2017](https://blog.limnology.wisc.edu/2017/05/12/golden-gophers-worried-about-golden-shiners/)
- **Offshore in a mixed lake.** In Lake Opinicon, ON, golden shiner is one of the five dominant species of the *offshore* assemblage. The lake is 787 ha, under 11 m deep and unstratified in summer; vegetation grows only to about 3.5 m. Gill nets were set at the 1.5–7.0 m contours in May and July to study diel and spring/summer space use (Keast & Fox 1992) — [sci, QUBS summary](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/space-use-and-feeding-patterns-of-an-offshore-fish-assemblage-in-a-shallow-mesotrophic-lake). Results not seen.

#### Diel behaviour
- **Hall et al. 1979** (a small Michigan lake; visual observation and gill nets):
  - Golden shiners schooled in the littoral zone by day.
  - Just after sunset the schools broke up and fish moved to open water.
  - They fed on zooplankton in low light, after sunset and just before sunrise.

  — [secondary, Fishes of Texas account citing Hall et al. 1979](https://fishesoftexas.org/taxa/notemigonus-crysoleucas); [secondary, ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Notemigonus_crysoleucas/)
  - A search summary of the same work says most prey were taken within 1 h of sunset, feeding was minimal through the night with some at dawn, and the main prey was *Daphnia*. *Daphnia* showed strong diel vertical migration and a size and density gradient decreasing from the limnetic to the littoral zone, so evening prey size rose as large *Daphnia* came to the surface [snippet; primary not seen].
- **Plasticity (review)** — [sci review, Reebs 2002, Rev. Fish Biol. Fish. 12:349](https://www.umoncton.ca/sites/prof.prod.umoncton.ca/files/users/user109/Reebs%20fish%20review.pdf):
  - Plankton-feeding populations appear crepuscular (Zaret & Suffern 1976; Hall et al. 1979; Keast & Fox 1992).
  - In one lake (Gascon & Leggett 1977), shiners were diurnal where they ate flying insects, but their gut fullness was high day and night in a more productive area with benthos.
  - Helfman (1981a) found young-of-year fed by day while adults were more crepuscular. He suggested young fish avoid crepuscular predators.
  - Hall et al. suggested adults avoid daytime plankton feeding because of predation risk from largemouth bass and pike.
  - Shiners feed on the bottom, on macrophytes, in the water column and at the surface. They can filter-feed on dense zooplankton without vision (Ehlinger 1989).
  - They raid pumpkinseed nests at night to eat eggs (Popiel et al. 1996).
  - In the lab they anticipate daily feeding times and places (Reebs 1996).
- **Aggregation by light.** In an acoustic study of a small oligotrophic lake, golden shiners were more aggregated by day than at night — [sci, *Influence of moon phase on acoustic estimates of fish performing daily horizontal migration in a small oligotrophic lake*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/237185687_Influence_of_moon_phase_on_acoustic_estimates_of_fish_performing_daily_horizontal_migration_in_a_small_oligotrophic_lake) [snippet]
- **An earlier-dispersal variant.** Adults school heavily by day and in late afternoon individually disperse into the limnetic zone to feed — [snippet, secondary summary seen in search; source not identified]. This disagrees on timing (late afternoon vs just after sunset).

#### Winter and low O₂
- **Cooper & Washburn 1946** (southern Michigan winterkill lakes, 1944–45) — [sci/agency, Michigan IFR Report 1078](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1001-RR1100/RR1078.pdf):
  - Survival under winter hypoxia was best in bullheads and golden shiners, which survived below 0.3–0.2 ppm. Bass and bluegill tolerated only about 0.6 ppm.
  - **Third Sister Lake** (no inlet or outlet):
    - DO was 0.2–0.4 ppm on 5 Feb and 0.2–0.3 on 14 Feb, staying mostly below 1 ppm for two more weeks.
    - On 5 Feb, at 0.4–0.8 ppm, "several hundred golden shiners" with young bluegills, chubsuckers and mud pickerel crowded distressed near the surface of a 3.7 m air-pump opening.
    - By 14 Feb, at 0.2 and 0.07 ppm (0.3 ppm in the skimmed surface film), 10–20 thousand fish, mostly golden shiners and bullheads, were milling at the hole's surface, still wary.
    - The authors conclude thousands of shiners and bullheads lived at least a week below 0.5 ppm.
  - **Green Lake:**
    - A 137 m inlet (about 3 L/s) became a refuge.
    - About 15,000 fish (bullheads, chubsuckers, golden shiners, pumpkinseeds, perch, mudminnows, mud pickerel; no bluegill, crappie or bass) packed into it.
    - DO was 7.1 ppm at the inlet head and 0.05 ppm at its mouth, after the lake had been below 1 ppm for several days.
  - Moore's 1942 laboratory threshold for golden shiner is illegible in the scan.
- **Minnesota rearing ponds.** In Turtle Pond, golden shiners were still caught after ice-out even though maximum DO stayed below 1 mg/L for at least 6 days in February — [agency, Shroyer 2007, MN DNR Investigational Report 547](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2007/other/070599.pdf)
- **Cold improves tolerance.** Golden shiners exposed to 1 h of severe hypoxia at 14 and 24 °C were "extensively" tolerant, and more so at the lower temperature — [snippet, SICB meeting abstract](https://sicb.org/?p=10273)
- **Not always spared.** Dead golden shiners were among the fish seen at ice-out after a Minnesota winterkill; in another lake they survived — [snippet, MN DNR Hutchinson winterkill page](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/areas/fisheries/hutchinson/winterkill.html)
- **Assemblage context.** In 18 northern Wisconsin lakes, assemblage type followed winter O₂ and refuges: centrarchid–*Esox* where winter O₂ was high or a stream refuge existed, *Umbra*–cyprinid otherwise (Tonn & Magnuson 1982) — [sci, record](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/882030ec-c143-4156-9b2d-8fcf76ffef0c). I did not verify golden shiner's membership in the cyprinid group.

#### Spawning
- Spawning starts above 20.6 °C (Cooper 1935). Eggs stick to filamentous algae and plants. Shiners sometimes spawn over largemouth bass nests — [secondary, Fishes of Texas](https://fishesoftexas.org/taxa/notemigonus-crysoleucas)
- Maine: late spring–early summer, over submerged vegetation in quiet water — [agency, MDIFW Non-sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- A spawning range of 20–27 °C is in the companion notes.

### Inferences
- **Diel structure.** Daytime littoral schooling in weeds (cover from visual predators) alternates with dusk dispersal into open water (zooplankton foraging when large *Daphnia* rise and visual predators are less efficient). This is a predation-risk × light × prey-migration mechanism.
  - In a shallow mixed Maine lake with no deep zooplankton refuge, the dusk offshore pulse should weaken. Shiners would feed more in the littoral and at the weed edge (inferred).
- **Winter location in non-hypoxic lakes is undocumented here.** By analogy with cold-tolerant cyprinids, expect loose aggregations near the bottom of deeper weed-edge water with low activity (inferred).
- **In hypoxic lakes,** the documented behaviour is to rise to the ice-water interface, holes and inlets, and to survive about 0.2–0.5 ppm for a week or more.

### Gaps
- No direct under-ice location or activity data for golden shiner in a normoxic northern lake.
- Hall et al. 1979 numbers (distances, depths, time offsets) are from secondary summaries.
- Faber 1980 (Lac Heney, Quebec), the most northern life-history study, was not retrieved.

## 3. White sucker (*Catostomus commersonii*)

### Takeaway
- **A nearshore benthic forager.**
  - In a Boreal stratified lake, tagged suckers stayed in water under 10 m all year, within 1 m of the bottom 84–88% of the time.
  - Mean depth was 3.8 m in spring, 4.9 m in summer, 5.0 m in fall and 6.1 m in winter.
  - In open water they selected 10–16 °C and avoided water below 6 °C.
  - They used about 15–19 ha (one-third of a 52-ha lake) with strong site fidelity, yet moved 2.1–3.2 km/day.
- **Stratified lakes.** Suckers concentrate in the "wash zone", where the oscillating thermocline meets the bottom.
- **Diel.** More active at night, moving shoreward in the evening and offshore by morning (secondary sources). Connected-lake fish make regular daily commutes timed by time of day.
- **Winter.** Slightly deeper, less movement, no thermal selection.
  - They are hypoxia-sensitive: killed when maximum DO stayed below 1 mg/L for about 12 days.
- **Spring.** Runs up gravel inlet or outlet streams from about 10 °C, soon after ice-out.
  - Entry peaks around sunset; spawning is mostly nocturnal or at dusk.
  - Spawning is in water under 30 cm deep at 14–90 cm/s.
  - Some fish may spawn on lake shoals or skip spawning.

### Cited Findings

#### Lake habitat by season (telemetry)
- **Richter, Smokorowski & Blanchfield 2024** — [sci, Environ. Biol. Fish., *Seasonal habitat use of white sucker in a small Boreal lake*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10641-024-01581-8)
  - **Study design.** Turkey Lake, ON (47°N): 52 ha, max 34 m, mean 12 m, oligotrophic and dimictic. Nine adults carried depth tags, tracked by 29 receivers, December 2021–December 2022.
  - **Depth by season.**
    - Mean depths were 6.10 m in winter, 3.78 m in spring, 4.85 m in summer and 4.95 m in fall. Winter and fall did not differ significantly.
    - Fish generally stayed in the upper 10 m.
  - **Habitat.**
    - Core areas and home ranges were 92–100% habitat under 10 m deep.
    - Fish chose shallow, relatively flat bays and avoided the steep-sided main basin.
  - **Bottom contact.**
    - Positions were within 1 m of the bottom 84.4–87.8% of the time in winter, summer and fall.
    - In spring they were furthest off the bottom (spawning movement).
  - **Temperature.**
    - Spring: preferred 12–16 °C, avoided below 10 °C.
    - Summer: affinity for 14–16 °C, avoided below 6 °C. The lake reached 24.6 °C, yet water above 18 °C was not selected.
    - Fall: 10–14 °C.
    - Winter: no thermal preference.
    - Over the whole open-water season: 10–16 °C.
  - **Space use.**
    - Home range (95% kernel) was 18.4 ha in spring and 14.6 ha in fall; core area (50% kernel) was 4.0 ha in winter and about 2 ha otherwise.
    - Seasonal home ranges overlapped 74–82%, showing high site fidelity. At most, fish used about 35% of the lake.
  - **Movement.**
    - Horizontal movement was 2.1–3.16 km/day (mean 2.6 ± 0.5), higher in spring and summer than in winter and fall.
    - Vertical movement was 25 m/day in winter against about 36–49 m/day in the other seasons.
  - **Spawning.** In late May, some tagged fish disappeared from the array near the inlet; the inlet and outlet are under 0.5 m deep. Others stayed in the lake, suggesting lake spawning or skipped spawning.
  - **Competing preference estimates.** These fish chose cooler water than earlier estimates suggest: a final preferendum of 23.4 °C (Hasnain et al. 2013) and an "optimal" 14.1–21.2 °C (Cincotta & Stauffer 1984).
- **The wash zone in stratified lakes.**
  - White sucker habitat use (top 50% of abundance) lay fully within the wash zone: the band of lake bottom swept by the thermocline during internal seiches, which coincides with a sharp change in sediment roughness and hardness.
  - Longnose sucker used deeper sediment-focusing areas and did not overlap white sucker.
  - The data come from Lakes Manitou and Kioshkokwi, Algonquin, ON, using depth-stratified netting.

  — [sci, Dryad dataset for *The wash zone and habitat use among three benthic fish species in stratified lakes*](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.fj6q5742c)
- **Ontario LHD summary.** Pools and riffles of creeks and rivers, warm shallow lakes and embayments of larger lakes, "usually at depths of 6–9 m". Preferred 17–24 °C; coolwater; benthic — [secondary, Ontario LHD](https://www.ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=19)
  - **Disagreement:** the telemetry above found 10–16 °C selected and 3.8–6.1 m mean depths.
- **Maine.**
  - Very broad habitat use: small streams to large lakes, rocky to muddy bottoms, warm and cold, weedy or bare.
  - Spends most of its time close to the bottom. Longnose sucker uses deeper water in lakes.

  — [agency, MDIFW Non-sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)

#### Diel behaviour
- **Day and night activity** — [secondary, ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Catostomus_commersonii/):
  - Adults and juveniles feed day and night but are more active at night, when they move into shallower water.
  - Suckers tend to be inshore in the evening and offshore by morning.
  - Young-of-year form schools of several hundred.
  - Fry under 51 mm feed in water 15–20 cm deep along lake shores.
- White suckers feed at night — [agency, Newfoundland & Labrador](https://www.gov.nl.ca/fal/wildlife/all-species/animals/inland-fish/white-sucker)
- **Daily commutes in connected Adirondack lakes** (PIT telemetry):
  - A subset of the population regularly moved back and forth each day through a short, shallow channel between two lakes.
  - The direction of movement was strongly tied to time of day.
  - Larger individuals were far more likely to commute.

  — [sci, Marcy-Quay & Kraft 2021, Northeast. Nat. 28:430](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-28-4/11-Marcy-Quay.shtml) (abstract; the time-of-day direction is not stated in it)
- **Counter-evidence from a river.** Juvenile white suckers in the Juniata River, PA, had a *diurnal* feeding peak — [sci, Johnson & Dropkin 1995, J. Freshw. Ecol. 10:11](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1014897). This is a river and juveniles, so it differs from the lake-adult pattern.

#### Winter
- **Behaviour.** Turkey Lake telemetry: slightly deeper (6.1 m), mostly on the bottom, least vertical movement (25 m/day), largest core area (4 ha), no thermal preference — [Richter et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10641-024-01581-8)
- **Hypoxia sensitivity** — [agency, Shroyer 2007, MN DNR IR 547](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2007/other/070599.pdf):
  - White suckers are listed with walleyes and sunfish as "anoxia-intolerant": reduced or eliminated when induced winter hypoxia cut maximum DO by at least 2 mg/L at more than 1 mg/L/day.
  - Mortenson Pond: maximum DO was below 1 mg/L for at least 12 days. Many dead white suckers washed ashore at ice-out; only fathead minnows survived.
  - Tatlie Pond: DO fell only to about 4 mg/L, and suckers survived.

#### Spring spawning run
- **Maine.**
  - All three Maine suckers run into streams in early spring to spawn over gravel in shallow, swift water.
  - Spawning is generally at night, with no nest and no parental care.
  - White suckers may also spawn on lake shoals.
  - 96% of trap-net catch in spring runs was white suckers.

  — [agency, MDIFW Non-sport Plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/nonsportcommercial.pdf)
- **Temperature trigger** (Geen et al. 1966): spring migrations began when stream temperature reached 10 °C. Barton (1980) found discharge also drives the onset and strength of the run — [sci, MSU thesis citing Geen et al. 1966 and Barton 1980](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/25060/FULL_TEXT/view) [snippet]
- **Newfoundland and Labrador.** Adults move from lakes into gravelly streams at 10 °C, early May to early June. Spawning is at dusk and late evening in shallow gravel. Adults return to the same sites, where thousands may gather — [agency, NL](https://www.gov.nl.ca/fal/wildlife/all-species/animals/inland-fish/white-sucker)
- **Timed by ice-out.** Upstream breeding runs usually occur at night, timed shortly after ice leaves the spawning area — [secondary, ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Catostomus_commersonii/)
- **Entry timing (DIDSON sonar, Boardman River, Lake Michigan tributary)** — [sci, Swanson et al. 2021, J. Great Lakes Res. 47:1159](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70223117):
  - The study counted medium fish (30–50 cm, mostly white and longnose suckers).
  - Against midday, entry was 21% more likely at sunset and 25% less likely at midnight.
  - Entry rose about 6% per 1 °C of river temperature and about 4% per 1 m³/s of the previous day's discharge; it fell about 1% per 10 cm of lake level.
  - **Disagreement:** this is a dusk peak, not "nocturnal" in the strict sense.
- **Spawning-site hydraulics** (from the companion notes): under 30 cm deep, 14–90 cm/s, onset near 10 °C; run peaks at about 11–15 °C in the Great Lakes.

### Inferences
- **Depth placement.** Suckers sit at the shallowest bottom that still offers about 10–16 °C (stratified lake) and good benthic food on gently sloping, fine-to-mixed bottom.
  - In a 3–6 m mixed lake there is no cooler bottom refuge in summer. Expect suckers spread over the whole flat basin bottom, with the deepest part used most in midsummer (inferred).
  - The winter shift to slightly deeper water matches seeking the about 4 °C bottom water of inverse stratification.
- **Hypoxia sensitivity** means suckers should be among the first to die or leave in a shallow pond's late-winter anoxia. They lack the surface-crowding tolerance of shiners and bullheads.
- **Spring run.** A threshold-plus-hazard process: eligibility when stream temperature is at least 10 °C after ice-out, an entry hazard modulated by light (peak at sunset, low at midnight), temperature and discharge, a short stay in the stream, then return to the lake.

### Gaps
- No lake study gives day vs night *depth* for adult white suckers. The evening-inshore pattern rests on secondary summaries.
- No direct under-ice O₂ behaviour data (rising to the ice? using inlets?).
- Only one telemetry lake (n = 5–9 fish).

## 4. Brown bullhead (*Ameiurus nebulosus*), "hornpout"

### Takeaway
- **Habitat.** Benthic, in warm, quiet, soft-bottomed, weedy shallows: coves, bays and ponds.
- **Diel.**
  - Inactive by day, resting in weed beds or cover; forages over the bottom after dark using taste and smell.
  - Feeding peaks around dusk and dawn.
  - During the spawning season it is active day and night.
  - Moves between habitats by night.
- **Gregarious.** Adults loosely school; young form tight schools herded by the parents.
- **Summer home range.** Small: about 4.5 ha, within 500 m.
- **Spring.** Home range expands up to about 20 ha, moving toward warming water.
- **Winter.**
  - Stops feeding and becomes sluggish.
  - Often buries in soft, leafy ooze or mud (shoreline or deeper soft bottom) with only mouth and gills exposed.
  - Survives the lowest O₂ of the four (about 0.2 ppm). In winterkill it crowds with golden shiners at surface openings and inlets.
- **Preferred temperature** 25–31 °C.
- **Spawning.** May–June (about 18–25 °C) in nests in sheltered sites, with biparental care.

### Cited Findings

#### Habitat and diel activity
- **Wisconsin DNR** — [agency, Wisconsin DNR PUBL-FM-706 (2008), *Bullheads*](https://dnr.Wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Species_bullheads.pdf):
  - Bullheads are gregarious and prefer to travel in large schools along the bottom of warm lakes and sluggish streams, toward quiet backwaters or vegetated shallows.
  - They generally stay inactive by day, "loafing" in weed beds until night, then prowl after dark.
  - About 100,000 taste receptors over the body help them find food at night.
  - They will bite "at almost any time of day". Anglers fish bait on the bottom of a shallow bay in the evening.
- **Maine.** Hornpout anglers usually fish sheltered muddy coves after dark on calm, warm evenings (Northeast Pond) — [agency, MDIFW lake survey](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/york/northeast_pond.pdf)
- **Lake Opinicon nearshore study.** Two nocturnal feeders, black crappie and brown bullhead, moved significantly between habitats (weedbeds, sand, rock, gravel) between day and night — [sci, Keast, Harker & Turnbull, Env. Biol. Fish. 3:173 (QUBS summary)](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/nearshore-fish-habitat-utilization-and-species-associations-in-lake-opinicon-ontario-canada)
- **Light and spawning** — [sci review, Reebs 2002](https://www.umoncton.ca/sites/prof.prod.umoncton.ca/files/users/user109/Reebs%20fish%20review.pdf):
  - **Light controls activity** (Eriksson 1978). Of 6 captive brown bullheads that swam only at night, 4 became diurnal when daytime light was lowered to 1 lux. All six fed mostly around dawn and dusk whatever the daylight intensity.
  - **Spawning overrides the rhythm** (Helfman 1981a). In the spawning season, brown bullhead, with pumpkinseed, largemouth bass, bluntnose minnow, yellow perch and rock bass, becomes active day and night.
- **Ontario LHD summary.**
  - Habitat: pools and sluggish runs over sand to mud, impoundments, ponds and lake embayments. Benthic, warmwater.
  - Preferred 25–31 °C. Tolerates 0.2 mg/L O₂ and 36 °C.
  - Spawns May–June at 14–25 °C; a nest-guarding cavity spawner.

  — [secondary, Ontario LHD](https://ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=77)
- **ADW summary.** A bottom-dwelling fish of vegetated habitats. Mainly a nocturnal feeder, but seen feeding by day. Juveniles stay in schools — [secondary, ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Ameiurus_nebulosus/)

#### Home range and movement
- **Anacostia River, DC** (40 adults, 250–310 mm TL, ultrasonic telemetry):
  - **Summer:** fish stayed within 500 m of release; 95% minimum-area polygon 4.5 ha, linear range 0.50 km.
  - **Spring:** most moved upstream about 1 km as water warmed; 19.7 ha and 2.1 km.
  - **Fall–winter:** 15.9 ha and 2.1 km.
  - **Lake Kingman** (a tidal impoundment): 5.8 ha.
  - Fish were resident all year.

  — [sci, Sakaris & Jesien 2005, TAFS 134:1262](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1577/T04-086.1)
- **Presque Isle Bay, Lake Erie** (telemetry, n = 49): adult site fidelity, with movement mainly between sheltered embayments and the open bay — [sci, USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70035937)
- **Lake Taupo, NZ.** Introduced population in a warm-temperate, ice-free lake, so use with caution.
  - **Telemetry** — [sci, Dedual 2002, Hydrobiologia 483:129](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1021319310063):
    - Pressure tags on 12 adults (295–345 mm) gave about 61,000 depth records over a year.
    - Depths ranged 0–17 m.
    - Fish were more active at night, using shallow water extensively, especially in winter.
    - In winter they stayed more stationary, moving only within the bay; movement peaked in spring.
  - **Netting** — [sci, Barnes & Hicks, DOC](https://doc.govt.nz/documents/science-and-technical/PF04barnes.pdf):
    - Overnight fyke nets in the under-5 m littoral caught most fish in sheltered, macrophyte-dominated bays.
    - Catch per net-night (late summer / autumn / winter / spring / early summer):
      - weedy sites: 19.6 / 26.9 / 6.0 / 22.6 / 92.1;
      - rocky sites: 5.5 / 39.0 / 44.0 / 23.5 / 34.3;
      - sandy sites: about 0–3.
    - In winter, catches shifted from weed beds to rocky sites.

#### Winter
- **Wisconsin DNR.**
  - Feeding falls off in winter. Fish stop feeding, become sluggish and often bury in soft, leafy ooze along the shore with only mouth and gills exposed.
  - This "hibernation" lets bullheads survive winterkill.
  - Bullheads survive out of water for hours; there are anecdotes of fish surviving weeks in drying mud.

  — [agency, Wisconsin DNR PUBL-FM-706](https://dnr.Wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Species_bullheads.pdf)
- **Oneida Lake, NY.** Bullheads survive winter by burrowing into mud until only their mouths show — [snippet, Cornell Biological Field Station / NY Sea Grant (page now 404)](https://seagrant.sunysb.edu/OLI/brownbullhead.pdf)
- **Michigan habitat table.** Lists muddy bottoms as the brown bullhead's winter refuge — [snippet, Michigan DNR Special Report 16 appendix (now 404)](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/IFR/ifrlibra/Special/Reports/sr16/sr16app_p53-76.pdf)
- **Deeper water in the north.** A species profile says that in northern parts of the range fish likely move to slightly deeper water to hibernate in soft mud — [snippet, govdocs species profile (host unreachable)](https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/255/255220.pdf)
- **Winterkill survival** — [Cooper & Washburn 1946](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1001-RR1100/RR1078.pdf):
  - Bullheads (with golden shiners) had the best survival, at least below 0.3–0.2 ppm.
  - In Green Lake on 1 February, "many bullheads" were in a hole where surface O₂ was 0.8 ppm. On 5 February, dead and live bullheads were in a hole at 0.1 ppm.
  - Bullheads were among the 15,000 fish packed into the Green Lake inlet refuge.
  - At Third Sister Lake they formed a large share of the 10–20 thousand fish at the air hole at 0.07–0.2 ppm.
  - Moore 1942 gave about 0.3 ppm as the black bullhead threshold.
- **Black bullhead (a congener).** Never significantly reduced by induced winter anoxia (maximum DO below 1 mg/L for 6–15 days), whereas walleye, sunfish and white sucker died. Black bullheads may survive 0.2 mg/L or less (Cooper & Washburn 1946; Johnson 1965) — [agency, Shroyer 2007](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2007/other/070599.pdf)

#### Spawning
- **Wisconsin.** Bullheads spawn April–July at 70–77 °F (21–25 °C). Brown bullheads favour rocky and sandy bottoms. The female excavates a saucer nest sheltered by vegetation, logs, rocks or overhanging banks, or in stumps or muskrat burrows.
  - Both parents fan the eggs and herd the fry into compact schools for about 2 weeks.
  - Young keep travelling in large schools.

  — [agency, Wisconsin DNR](https://dnr.Wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Species_bullheads.pdf)
- **Disagreement on onset temperature:** NH says about 18 °C and over, Ontario LHD 14–25 °C, Wisconsin 21–25 °C (see the companion notes).

### Inferences
- **Light-gated activity.** Bullheads are a fish whose activity is set by ambient light (Eriksson's 1-lux result). Daytime cover-seeking (weeds, logs, burrows) plus nocturnal chemosensory bottom foraging produce the "sheltered muddy cove after dark" pattern.
- **Winter refuge.** Burial in soft sediment in winter puts the fish in about 4 °C bottom water with minimal metabolism. When bottom O₂ goes to zero in late winter, bullheads leave the mud and rise to whatever O₂ remains (holes, inlets, under-ice film). Their tolerance about 0.2–0.3 ppm makes them the last fish standing (Cooper & Washburn observations).
- **Shallow ponds.** The pattern fits the user's 4 m pond: bullheads are the expected dominant winter survivor there.

### Gaps
- No telemetry of brown bullheads under ice in a native northern lake.
- Loeb 1964 (burial in sediment, NY) was not retrieved, so burial depth, frequency and season limits are unknown.
- No day vs night depth data from a native lake.

## 5. Cross-species patterns relevant to stratified vs shallow mixed lakes

- **Stratified deep lakes** (Turkey Lake, Algonquin lakes, deep Maine basins):
  - White sucker concentrates at the thermocline–bottom contact (wash zone) and in 3.8–6.1 m nearshore bottom.
  - White perch summer days are at 4–9 m; winter in basins at 12–18 m (to 40 m).
  - These depths come from Richter et al. 2024, the wash-zone dataset and Stanley & Danie 1983, linked above.
- **Shallow mixed lakes and ponds** (3–6 m):
  - Summer offers no thermal or depth refuge. White perch still support a winter fishery in a lake only 6.1 m deep (Horseshoe Lake, ME), so they use the deepest available basin.
  - MDIFW flags winter O₂ in the deep water as the condition for white perch persistence. Lakes about 7.6 m or more and 20 ha or more are typical (MDIFW White Perch Plan).
- **Winter-hypoxia ranking** (most to least tolerant), consistent with the Cooper & Washburn 1946 field data and Shroyer 2007 above:
  1. Brown bullhead ≈ golden shiner (about 0.2–0.3 ppm, a week or more below 0.5 ppm);
  2. white perch (avoids below 35% saturation; growth hit at ≤40%; needs oxygenated deep water);
  3. white sucker (killed by about 12 days at maximum DO below 1 mg/L).
- **Under-ice O₂ refuges are real and used.** Fish pack into inlets with oxygenated flow (15,000 in a 137 m inlet) and crowd at holes and the ice-water interface when bulk-water DO falls below about 0.5–1 ppm (Cooper & Washburn 1946).
  - Refuges also explain assemblage structure in Wisconsin lakes (Tonn & Magnuson 1982).
  - Klinger, Magnuson & Gallepp 1982 (Environ. Biol. Fishes 7:113) studied the survival mechanisms of central mudminnow, fathead minnow and brook stickleback for low winter O₂ — [FishBase reference record](https://fishbase.se/references/FBRefSummary.php?ID=77050). Shroyer 2007 cites it for fish gathering in small higher-O₂ refuges — [MN IR 547](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2007/other/070599.pdf). The paper itself was not seen.
- **Twilight is the shared feeding window:**
  - white perch: dusk catches, last hour of daylight, dusk egg-release peaks;
  - golden shiner: within 1 h after sunset;
  - white sucker: sunset stream entry, dusk spawning, evening inshore;
  - brown bullhead: dawn and dusk feeding peaks, night activity.

  Twilight and darkness reduce visual-predator efficiency, and zooplankton rise then. All sources are above.

## 6. Implications for a simulation

### Mechanisms to build (so the patterns emerge rather than being scripted)
1. **Acclimation-tracking thermal preference.** Each fish holds an acclimation temperature that lags its experienced temperature by days. Its preferred temperature is a species function of that acclimation, capped at the final preferendum.
   - White perch: about 5 °C (winter-acclimated) to 29–31 °C final.
   - White sucker: 10–16 °C selected in the field (17–24 °C in the literature; prefer the field value for a Maine fish).
   - Brown bullhead: 25–31 °C.
   - Under ice, with inverse stratification (0 °C at the ice, about 4 °C at the bottom), this alone sends fish to the bottom and the deepest basin.
2. **O₂ as an overriding constraint.** Avoidance starts at a species threshold and lethality accrues as a dose (concentration × days), so a fish trades the warm, hypoxic bottom for the cold, oxygenated ice interface, holes or inlet plume:
   - white perch: avoid below about 35% saturation, growth penalty at ≤40%;
   - white sucker: lethal dose roughly below 1 mg/L for more than about 10 days;
   - golden shiner and bullhead: survive about 0.2–0.5 mg/L for about a week or more.

   This produces both the "perch under the ice" reports and Cooper & Washburn's crowding at holes and inlets.
3. **Light-gated diel state.** Use a per-species light-intensity switch (bullhead: activity suppressed above about 1 lux; Eriksson 1978) combined with predation risk from visual predators that scales with light:
   - white perch: depth or offshore refuge by day, inshore 1 m flats at night, feeding peak at dusk;
   - golden shiner: weed-bed schooling by day, dispersal offshore at sunset tracking rising *Daphnia*;
   - brown bullhead: in cover by day, roaming over open mud at night;
   - white sucker: more active at night and shoreward in the evening.

   Spawning overrides the gate: bullhead active day and night while spawning (Helfman 1981a).
4. **Food tracking.** The golden shiner's dusk offshore move should depend on the zooplankton field's own vertical and horizontal migration (large *Daphnia* offshore and rising at dusk). Winter white perch follow plankton and baitfish into basins. Suckers and bullheads forage on benthic fields: suckers on gently sloping bottom under 10 m deep, bullheads on soft mud.
5. **Cover and substrate preference:**
   - bullhead: weed, wood or burrow by day; soft mud for winter burial;
   - golden shiner: dense submerged vegetation;
   - white perch: open water over level fine sediment, avoiding muck, gravel and rock;
   - white sucker: flat, fine-to-mixed bottom.
6. **Schooling as a state variable:**
   - white perch: large mobile schools all year (hundreds at spawning), compact near-bottom under ice, roaming on minute-to-hour scales;
   - golden shiner: tight day schools that break up at sunset and re-form at dawn;
   - suckers: young-of-year in schools of hundreds;
   - bullhead: fry herded by parents for about 2 weeks; adults loosely gregarious.
7. **Spawning migrations as hazards, not dates:**
   - **White sucker:** eligible after ice-out once stream temperature is at least 10 °C. Entry hazard is about 1.21× at sunset and 0.75× at midnight relative to midday, ×1.06 per °C and ×1.04 per m³/s of the previous day's discharge. Spawning is over gravel under 30 cm deep at 14–90 cm/s, followed by a return to the lake. Some fish may spawn on lake shoals or skip.
   - **White perch:** at about 15 °C (11–21 °C across sources), mass shallow spawning at 0–1.5 m on any substrate or up inlets; dusk peaks; release spread over 10–21 days.
   - **Golden shiner:** at about 20 °C or above, over submerged vegetation.
   - **Bullhead:** about 18–25 °C, sheltered nest, biparental guarding.
8. **Winter dormancy (bullhead).** At low temperature, a burial state in soft sediment with feeding off and metabolism minimal. It exits when local O₂ falls below a threshold, then seeks refuges.

### Validation checks (headless, per species; use multi-seed medians)
- **White perch, summer (lake):** median daytime depth of adults is at least 2× the median nighttime depth.
  - Daytime ≥60% are over water deeper than 3 m; nighttime ≥50% are over water shallower than 1.5 m.
  - The feeding (consumption) rate peaks within ±1.5 h of sunset.
- **White perch, winter (lake):** under ice and with bottom DO above the avoidance level, ≥70% of adults are in the deepest 25% of lake depth (the hole) and within about 4 m of the bottom.
  - Schools stay cohesive: mean nearest-neighbour distance below about 3 body lengths.
  - If bottom DO falls below the avoidance level, mean depth decreases.
- **White perch, spawning:** ≥80% of spawning events occur at water depth ≤1.5 m (or in the stream) at about 15 °C or above, with a dusk peak.
- **Golden shiner, summer:** at midday ≥70% are in littoral or weed cells. In the hour after sunset the share over open water or near the surface rises by at least 30 points. Gut-fill gain is concentrated at dusk.
- **Golden shiner, hypoxic pond winter:** at DO 0.3–0.5 mg/L survival after 7 days is ≥50%, and fish cluster at the highest-O₂ cells (ice interface or inlet).
- **White sucker, all seasons:** ≥80% of positions are within 1 m of the bottom.
  - Summer mean depth is 4–5 m in the lake when it stratifies; winter mean depth is about 1 m deeper than summer.
  - Home range is about one-third of lake area or less, with high season-to-season overlap.
- **White sucker, spring run:** the first stream entries come after the stream reaches 10 °C. The entry rate at sunset exceeds midday, which exceeds midnight.
- **White sucker, winterkill:** a pond held below 1 mg/L for 12 days or more loses ≥90% of its suckers. At about 4 mg/L, survival exceeds 90%.
- **Brown bullhead, summer:** night activity (distance per hour) is at least 3× day activity, and ≥70% of daytime positions are in cover or weed cells. The summer home range is small (a few ha in sim-scaled units).
- **Brown bullhead, winter:** below about 4–5 °C, ≥50% are in the buried/torpid state in mud cells. In a hypoxic pond, bullheads have the highest survival of all species at 0.2–0.3 mg/L.
- **Cross-species winterkill ordering** in a hypoxic pond: bullhead ≥ golden shiner > white perch > white sucker. Survivors aggregate at the inlet or other O₂ refuges.

### Biggest uncertainties to carry into design
- No direct day vs night *depth* data for white perch, white sucker or golden shiner from a northern lake. The magnitudes above are older compilations, secondary summaries or angler knowledge.
- White perch spawning temperature disagrees: 15 °C (MDIFW, NH) against 18–21 °C held for days (AuClair 1956, Maine).
- White sucker preferred temperature disagrees: 10–16 °C in the field against 17–24 °C and a 23.4 °C final preferendum in the literature.
- Golden shiner and bullhead *normoxic* under-ice positions are undocumented, as is bullhead burial depth and frequency.
