## Bluegill (Lepomis macrochirus): spawning, nesting, limnetic larval phase, YOY growth, density, stunting, mortality, length-at-age

### Takeaway
In Upper-Midwest lakes, bluegill spawn in dense colonies in several bouts from late May to early August. Bouts run every ~1–2 weeks: 4 bouts in a 31-day season and up to 11 in a 112-day season. Spawning starts at roughly 18–21 °C.
- **Nest stage:** eggs hatch in 2–5 days, and fry stay on the nest a further 4–10 days. They leave at ~5 mm.
- **Limnetic stage:** larvae spend ~3–7 weeks feeding on zooplankton offshore, growing ~0.4–0.7 mm/d. They return to littoral vegetation at ~12.5 mm SL to 25 mm TL.
- **Recruitment** is set mostly in the larval/early-juvenile stage and by predators. It is highly variable: fall fingerlings ranged from ~0 to 376,000/ha in predator-free Wisconsin lakes.
- **Size:** Michigan's statewide mean length at age 1 is only ~46 mm TL.

### Cited Findings

**Spawning temperature, season, bouts**
- Spawning onset:
  - may begin when surface water reaches 64 °F (17.8 °C) (Scott & Crossman 1973);
  - in Wisconsin, starts in May at 67 °F (19.4 °C), peaks in June and continues through early August (Snow et al. 1970).

  Both via [Neuswanger et al. 2015, WDNR synoptic review](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Wisconsin DNR species page: spawning late May–early August, peak June, at 67–80 °F (19.4–26.7 °C) — [WDNR bluegill page](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/Fishing/species/bluegill.html).
- Beard (1982), three small winterkill lakes in NW Wisconsin (Camp 4.0 ha, Lamereau 4.2 ha, Nancy 2.7 ha; max depth 1.2–1.5 m), 1971–76:
  - spawning did not begin until water reached 21 °C, and was rare above 80 °F (26.7 °C);
  - multiple bouts ran from late May to early August: 4 bouts in a 31-day season, 11 bouts in a 112-day season;
  - temperature fluctuations stimulated repeated spawning, and longer seasons had more spawning periods (P < 0.01);
  - most females ripened over a 3-week period at the start of the season, giving peak fry/nest on 15 June–6 July;
  - some spawning happened as early as 1 June and as late as mid-August.

  Source: [Beard 1982, WDNR Tech. Bull. 127](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf); temperature and bout figures also via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Lake Opinicon, Ontario: 8 spawning bouts were identified in the 1993 season — [Cargnelli & Gross 1996, CJFAS 53:360–367](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-193).
- Late spawning adds little in N. Wisconsin: "number of fry/nest was low for spawnings after the 1st week in July". Beard concluded that spawning after the first week of July "will not add appreciably to the year class" — [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Ridge Lake, Illinois (central IL, more southern): spawning ran mid-May to mid-August, with several peaks in larval abundance — [Santucci & Wahl 2003, TAFS 132:346–360](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(2003)132<0346:TEOGPA>2.0.CO;2).
- Stunted populations spawn later. In Illinois ponds, peak spawning in a stunted population (all nesting males < 6.3 in / 160 mm) was 3–4 weeks later than in non-stunted ones (Aday et al. 2002) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

**Colonial nesting, nest predation, male tactics**
- Colony structure:
  - colonies hold 4 to 272 nests (Neff et al. 2004), spaced ~2 in (5 cm) apart (Côté & Gross 1993);
  - 4.5% of parental males in Lake Opinicon nested solitarily, ≥ 3 ft (0.9 m) from neighbours;
  - Saprolegnia fungus infected 38% of eggs in solitary nests vs 19% in colonial nests.

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Nest depth: 1.3–8.2 ft (0.4–2.5 m) in mesotrophic Lake Opinicon (Gross & Charnov 1980). In ultra-clear Lake Giles, PA (Secchi ~33 ft), nests reached 19 ft (5.8 m) (Olson et al. 2006). Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Substrate: gravel is generally preferred. Beard found NW Wisconsin bluegill nesting on muck, sand and gravel, with a slight preference for gravel mixed with sticks — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Brood predation in Lake Opinicon (Gross & MacMillan 1981, Behav. Ecol. Sociobiol. 8:163–174):
  - water-column predation on nests was 94% by conspecific bluegill; pumpkinseed also ate eggs and larvae;
  - substrate-level predation was 11% by snails (mostly *Viviparus georgianus*) and 7% by fishes (mostly *Ictalurus* = bullheads);
  - peripheral nests lost ≥ 3× more than interior nests.

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Other nest predators:
  - in Michigan ponds, crayfish at 1.2 /yd² (1.4 /m²) destroyed all nests in small colonies (≤ 15 males) within 4 weeks (Dorn & Mittelbach 2004);
  - in stunted Michigan ponds, recruitment failure was driven mainly by juvenile bluegill eating eggs, larvae and dispersing fry (Breck 1996).

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Male life histories, Lake Opinicon:
  - parental males mature late, at age 7–8;
  - cuckolders mature early, as "sneakers" at ages 2–3 and "satellites" at ages 4–5;
  - cuckolders put 5.6% of body mass into testes vs 1.2% in parentals, and make up ~20% of males (Gross & Charnov 1980; Ehlinger et al. 1997);
  - mature cuckolders had only 15% of the survivorship of future parentals; most died by age 5, while some parentals lived to age 11 (Gross 1982).

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

**Fecundity, eggs and fry per nest, hatch and swim-up**
- Eggs per female increased with size (Beard 1982, gravimetric counts, mean ± SD):

  | Female TL (mm) | Wolf/Loyhead lakes (1972–74) | Reynard Lake (1971) |
  |---|---|---|
  | 102–124 | 6,500 | – |
  | 127–150 | 8,900 | – |
  | 152–175 | 15,100 | 10,900 |
  | 178–201 | 21,000 | 17,900 |
  | 203–226 | – | 23,500 |

  Females deposit eggs over several bouts; nests with up to 67,600 fry imply several females per nest. Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Fry per nest ranged from < 100 to 67,600; nests with 30,000 were "quite common".
  - Mean fry/nest per season: 15,600–31,800 (Camp), 300–21,600 (Lamereau), 1,200–18,400 (Nancy).
  - At the start and end of the season: 1,000–8,500 fry/nest.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Active nests (nests with fry) per season: 52–127 in Camp Lake and 16–134 in Lamereau Lake, i.e. ~3.8–31.9 nests with fry per ha (Table 21) — [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Hatch time: eggs hatched in 2–3 days above 21 °C, and in 4 days (extreme cases 5) below 21 °C.
  - Northern Wisconsin spawning water normally runs 19–26 °C.
  - Mean egg viability on nests was 86.5%, falling to 57.4% by day 4 after deposition.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Swim-up: fry stayed on the nest 4–10 days after hatching, shorter at higher temperature (P < 0.01). Beard took ~4 days as the practical minimum. Eggs are on the nest at least 2 days and fry at least 5 days before dispersal — [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- 90–100% of hatched fry dispersed from the nest (whole-nest failures were rare) — [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).

**Limnetic (pelagic) larval phase**
- Northern Wisconsin (Beard 1982):
  - fry left the nest at 5 mm and migrated to the limnetic zone;
  - they stayed until 15–20 mm, then returned to the littoral;
  - limnetic growth averaged 0.5 mm/d, so they spent 30–40 days offshore;
  - in the first 14 days after dispersal they grew 0.4–0.7 mm/d;
  - Beard cites Werner (1969): 0.4 mm/d for limnetic fry in Crane Lake, Indiana;
  - "the entire year class … may be isolated in the limnetic area of a lake for a particular period".

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Werner (1967), Crane Lake, Indiana (27 acres): fry went pelagic shortly after swim-up and stayed "for several weeks", returning to littoral macrophytes at 21–25 mm. Primary citation: [Werner 1967, TAFS 96:416–420](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1967)96[416:IMOBFI]2.0.CO;2) (no abstract online); numbers via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Werner & Hall (1988) (Ecology 69:1352–1366):
  - in four Michigan lakes, fry returned to the littoral "at a relatively constant size of ~12.5 mm standard length";
  - juveniles then spend several years in littoral vegetation;
  - they shift back to pelagic Daphnia-feeding at 50–83 mm SL, the size varying among five lakes with largemouth bass density;
  - predation risk from bass was 40–80× higher for small bluegill in open water than in vegetation;
  - the pelagic zone was the more profitable habitat for every size class.

  Source: [Werner & Hall 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941633).
- Lake Mendota, WI, and Lake Opinicon, ON: larval sunfish were much denser in shallow limnetic areas near the colonies than far offshore. At the deepest Opinicon site, density was only 8% of the mean inshore-limnetic density (Post et al. 1995; Garvey et al. 2002) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Lake Mendota 1988–90 (purse seine):
  - the pelagic phase lasted 3–7 weeks, depending on species and year;
  - age-0 yellow perch dominated the pelagic age-0 community in June, followed by black crappie, freshwater drum and then Lepomis;
  - by August, Lepomis numerically dominated pelagic age-0 fish.

  Source: [Post, Rudstam & Schael 1995, TAFS 124:84–93](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1995)124<0084:TASDOP>2.3.CO;2).
- Gull Lake, Michigan (MSU thesis): "immature bluegill" were reported in the limnetic epilimnion for ~7 weeks in summer before returning to the littoral — [MSU ETD 41834](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/41834/FULL_TEXT/download). Seen only as a search snippet; the repository was access-restricted.
- Lake Opinicon 1998: 40% of larvae had appeared in open water by 12 June, but most juveniles caught in fall came from later production. Larval survival was often highest above 23.5 °C and was generally unrelated to edible zooplankton at swim-up — [Garvey, Herra & Leggett 2002, Ecol. Appl. 12:194–205](https://doi.org/10.1890/1051-0761(2002)012[0194:PRISTT]2.0.CO;2).

**YOY diet**
- By size (Beard 1982):
  - 5–10 mm: mainly copepod nauplii, *Daphnia* and *Bosmina*; prey size mattered more than species;
  - 11–19 mm: almost entirely Cladocera;
  - from ~20 mm, back in the littoral: Chydorinae, aquatic insects, ostracods and *Gammarus*.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Small bluegill and pumpkinseed (< 75 mm SL) in three Michigan lakes foraged mostly in vegetation. 76–91% of their seasonal diet was vegetation-dwelling (non-gastropod) prey — [Mittelbach 1984, Ecology 65:499–513](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941412).
- Blueberry Pond, MI: bluegill 1–3 in (25–76 mm) ate small midge larvae and *Caenis* mayfly nymphs in dense vegetation (Schneider 1999) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Small bluegill (< 80 mm TL) stayed in vegetation year-round, apparently to avoid predation. Overwinter they aggregated in tall perennial vegetation in the deep littoral and dispersed when water warmed to 20 °C (Lawrence Lake, MI) — [Hall & Werner 1977, TAFS 106:545–555](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1977)106<545:SDAAOF>2.0.CO;2).

  - The full fingerling range was 376,000/ha (Camp 1971) to "only a trace" (Lamereau 1974).
  - Fry 4 days after dispersal: 54,000–205,000/ha.
  - First fry dispersal fell between day 164 and day 200 of the year (13 June–19 July).
  - Fall length distributions were bimodal in 1971 and 1974.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf). The table was read from OCR text; individual cells may carry OCR error.
- Age-0 growth: Beard found it inversely related to fall density. Others report daily growth of 0.1 mm/d (slow ponds) to 0.6 mm/d (fast ponds) for 12–25 mm fry (Krumholz 1949), and 0.2–0.5 mm/d for 11–39 mm fish (Lux 1960); both cited in [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Birth date and size at age 1, Lake Opinicon:
  - yearling size fell steadily across the 8 bouts, a 50% difference in size at age 1 between early- and late-born fry;
  - first-trimester fry increased 231% in representation among yearlings;
  - via the WDNR review: early fry were 17% of production but 40% of spring recruits.

  Sources: [Cargnelli & Gross 1996](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-193); [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Fall age-0 size and experimental densities:
  - Jewett Lake, MI (walleye-perch community): surviving bluegill grew from 1.4 in (36 mm) at fall age-0 to 4.3 in (109 mm) one year later (Schneider 1997);
  - Michigan overwinter pond experiments stocked fall age-0 bluegill at 1.1 in (28 mm) and 4,348–8,502/acre (10,700–21,000/ha) (Schneider & Breck 1997).

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Density dependence:
  - small bluegill (< 55 mm SL) showed significant density-dependent growth across nine SW Michigan lakes;
  - the growth patterns break at ~55 mm SL, coinciding with the ontogenetic diet shift;
  - lake identity explained the variation and year explained none.

  Source: [Osenberg, Werner, Mittelbach & Hall 1988, CJFAS 45:17–26](https://doi.org/10.1139/f88-003).
- Across lakes, adult growth and adult density should be positively correlated, while juvenile growth and density should be negatively correlated. Both are driven by Daphnia productivity, and a long-term Michigan data set supported this — [Mittelbach & Osenberg 1993, Ecology 74:2381–2394](https://doi.org/10.2307/1939589).
- Age-0 bluegill growth dropped when zooplankton fell below a threshold, and the timing was density-dependent (Breck 1997, MI ponds/model) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

**Adult density / standing stock (Midwest)**
- Escanaba Lake, WI (293 acres), 1946–69: bluegill ≥ 4 in averaged only 1–8/acre (2.5–20/ha), under 15–36/acre (37–89/ha) age-2+ walleye (Kempinger et al. 1975) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Murphy Flowage, WI (180 acres), 1955–69 (Snow 1978):
  - harvest averaged 116 bluegill/acre/yr (287/ha/yr), or 18 lb/acre (20 kg/ha/yr), mean 6.0 in;
  - preferred-size fish (≥ 8 in) fell from 29/acre (72/ha) in 1955 to 2.8/acre in 1958.

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Mid Lake, WI (12 acres): bluegill ≥ 6 in fell from 104/acre (257/ha) to 37/acre (91/ha) within 3 years of opening to liberal harvest (Goedde & Coble 1981) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Blueberry Pond, MI (20 acres, dense vegetation, lightly fished):
  - 204/acre (504/ha) bluegill ≥ 8 in (Schneider 1999);
  - only 100–200 nests/yr, and ~2% of adults attempted to spawn.

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf). In a companion lake, piscivores (mostly largemouth bass) ate an estimated 303,300 juvenile bluegill per year — [Schneider 1999, NAJFM 19:97–109](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8675(1999)019<0097:DOQBPI>2.0.CO;2).
- Jewett Lake, MI (13 acres): after bluegill established, total fish standing crop was 63 lb/acre (~71 kg/ha), 52% of it bluegill (~37 kg/ha) (Schneider 1997) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- 38 Midwestern bass–bluegill ponds of 0.5–6 acres: bluegill standing stocks of 100–150 lb/acre (112–168 kg/ha) produced the most fall age-0. Lower and higher stocks produced fewer (Novinger & Legler 1978) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Michigan lakes: the bluegill-to-pumpkinseed ratio ranged from 25:1 to 1:1 across three lakes, tracking the availability of adult resources (snails vs zooplankton) — [Mittelbach 1984](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941412).

**Stunting**
- Becker (1983) called stunting "the biggest fish management problem in Wisconsin": overpopulation, often from excessive recruitment, leading to slow growth and high natural mortality before fish reach angler size — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Causes documented. All are via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf); the bass threshold point uses [Werner & Hall 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941633).
  1. **Weak predation on recruits.** Abundant small largemouth bass or walleye control recruitment. Bass fail where buffer prey (crayfish, young bullheads, gizzard shad) are abundant (Lewis & Helms 1964; Snow 1971; Einfalt & Wahl 1997). Esocids rarely control bluegill.
  2. **Density-dependent food limitation:**
     - Wiener & Hanneman 1982: condition and growth fall with relative density in N. Wisconsin;
     - Osenberg et al. 1988;
     - Beckman 1950: growth rose for only 1 year after winterkill.
  3. **Selective harvest of large parental males**, which triggers early maturation and "sexual stunting" (Drake et al. 1997; Jennings et al. 1997; Aday et al. 2002).
  4. **Bass predation sets a threshold.** Greater predation risk keeps juveniles in vegetation to larger sizes (50–83 mm SL).
  5. **Cannibalism of eggs and fry** by abundant juveniles in stunted ponds (Breck 1996).

**Annual adult mortality**
- Murphy Flowage, WI, bluegill ≥ 4 in, 1955–69 (Snow 1978): total A = 61% (range 49–74%), natural = 46% (32–59%), fishing = 12% (4–24%) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Four west-central Minnesota lakes (420–1,744 acres): mean total annual mortality of quality-size (≥ 6 in) bluegill was 75%, with exploitation 8–32% (Parsons & Reed 1998) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Mid Lake, WI, before exploitation: total annual mortality 30%, with abundant 12–15 in largemouth bass (Goedde & Coble 1981) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Nebraska Sandhill lakes (southern edge; weedy, lightly fished): conditional natural mortality 15–53% (Paukert et al. 2002) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Stocked adults (145–203 mm) in predator-free, unfished NW Wisconsin lakes:
  - lost 47.4% (28.2–64.4%) from June to September, 90% of it by mid-July, which Beard attributes to spawning stress;
  - survival from mid-July to September averaged ~95.7%;
  - adults grew 38 mm (30–43 mm) over the summer.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Calibration envelope for a small mesotrophic lake.**
  - Spawning window: water ≥ ~19–21 °C, from late May to early August.
  - Bouts: one every ~7–14 days (4 bouts in 31 d; 11 in 112 d; 8 in Opinicon).
  - Egg → hatch: 2–3 d (≥ 21 °C) or 4–5 d (< 21 °C). Hatch → swim-up: 4–10 d. Swim-up length ≈ 5 mm.
  - Limnetic stay: ~25–50 d at ~0.4–0.7 mm/d, returning at ~15–25 mm TL (≈ 12.5 mm SL in Werner & Hall).
  - Fall age-0 length: ~25–45 mm TL in northern lakes. Michigan age-1 mean is 46 mm, measured in the following spring/summer before much new growth.
  - Implied mean mass of Beard's October fingerlings, from kg/ha ÷ no./ha: ~0.3–1.2 g.
- **Egg → October survival, predator-free lakes.** From Beard's Table 21 (October fingerlings ÷ eggs potentially hatching):
  - range ≈ 0.02%–27%, median ≈ 9% across the 12 lake-years;
  - these were predator-free, unfished lakes, so it is an upper envelope;
  - with bass or walleye present, expect 1–2 orders of magnitude lower, given 58–93% of littoral fry eaten by young bass (below) and A ≈ 0.92 from fall age-0 to fall age-1 in Jewett Lake.
- **Mortality concentrated at first feeding.** Beard's daily instantaneous mortality was ~0.11–0.39 d⁻¹ in the 4 days after dispersal, vs ~0.001–0.008 d⁻¹ from day 4 to fall. Mortality is heavily front-loaded at the swim-up/first-feeding transition. Values read from OCR'd Table; treat as approximate.
- **Adult density for a mesotrophic lake with bass:** roughly 100–500 adults/ha (≥ 6 in) and ~20–80 kg/ha of bluegill. Very low (≤ 20/ha) under heavy walleye predation; very high (500/ha large fish) in weedy, lightly fished lakes with low recruitment.
- **The pelagic return size is a predation-risk decision**, and this matters for the sim's "schools feed on zooplankton then recruit" design. The second (adult) pelagic shift at 50–83 mm SL depends on bass density, so it could be gated on a predator-risk sensor rather than a fixed size.

### Gaps
- Werner (1969) "Ecology of limnetic bluegill fry in Crane Lake, Indiana" (Am. Midl. Nat. 81:164–181) was not read directly; its numbers come only via Beard 1982.
- Cooke & Philipp (2009) *Centrarchid Fishes* and Carlander's Handbook were not accessible.
- Wisconsin and Minnesota statewide length-at-age means for bluegill were not found; only Michigan's (Schneider et al. 2000) were. The web-search budget ran out before these could be located.
- No direct per-ha YOY density was found for a lake with a normal predator community. The numbers above are from predator-free experimental lakes (Beard) or from ponds.
- Bluegill YOY TL at the end of the first season, measured in October in Michigan/Wisconsin lakes, was not found as a published mean.

## Pumpkinseed (Lepomis gibbosus): spawning, size at the shift to molluscivory, diet, density, growth

### Takeaway
Juvenile pumpkinseed share the bluegill's vegetation niche and soft-bodied littoral prey.
- **Diet shift:** they break away at ~40–50 mm SL (a growth-pattern break) and become strong snail specialists by ~75 mm SL. In snail-rich Michigan lakes, snails are > 80% of their prey mass.
- **Abundance:** set by adult resources (snails). The bluegill:pumpkinseed ratio ranged 25:1 to 1:1 among Michigan lakes.
- **Spawning:** protracted, like bluegill (Lake Opinicon).
- **Missing:** numeric pumpkinseed spawning temperatures, nest/egg counts and per-ha densities were not obtained.

### Cited Findings
- Small vs large pumpkinseed in three small Michigan lakes:
  - small pumpkinseed (< 75 mm SL) are "unable to feed effectively on snails" and eat vegetation-dwelling non-gastropod prey (76–91% of diet, shared with bluegill);
  - large pumpkinseed (> 75 mm SL) specialise on vegetation-dwelling gastropods, while large bluegill take open-water Daphnia;
  - pumpkinseed had significantly lower snail-handling times than bluegill.

  Source: [Mittelbach 1984, Ecology 65:499–513](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941412).
- Growth-pattern break, nine SW Michigan lakes, 1978–85:
  - small pumpkinseed (< 40 mm SL) and small bluegill responded alike to environment;
  - large pumpkinseed (> 50 mm SL) diverged, and the break coincides with the size of the ontogenetic diet shift;
  - large fish were food-limited, and small fish showed density-dependent growth.

  Source: [Osenberg et al. 1988, CJFAS 45:17–26](https://doi.org/10.1139/f88-003).
- In three Michigan study lakes, gastropods were consistently > 80% of the prey mass in pumpkinseed diets. Selection among snail sizes was explained by size-specific encounter rates (71% of variance), plus size refuges from crushing resistance and gape limits (46% of variance where snails were relatively invulnerable) — [Osenberg & Mittelbach 1989, Ecol. Monogr. 59:405–432](https://doi.org/10.2307/1943074).
- Pumpkinseed from a snail-rich lake had larger levator posterior (shell-crushing) muscles than those from a snail-poor lake, together with:
  - 71% greater crushing strength;
  - ~100% higher foraging rates and fraction of thick-shelled snails in the diet.

  Source: [Osenberg, Huckins, Kaltenberg & Martínez 2004, Oecologia 141:57–65](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00442-004-1650-z). Numbers taken from a search summary of the abstract; the full text was not read.
- Where redear sunfish co-occur (Michigan), pumpkinseed are less molluscivorous: snails were ~30% of their prey biomass, and the diet was dominated by soft-bodied invertebrates — [MSU dissertation, ETD 23550](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/23550). Seen as a search snippet only.
- Lake Opinicon, ON, 1998–99: bluegill and pumpkinseed both reproduce over a protracted season. Fall length of age-0 sunfish increased with age (days post swim-up), and size-at-age shifted upward between October and May (growth plus possible selective loss of small fish) — [Garvey et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1890/1051-0761(2002)012[0194:PRISTT]2.0.CO;2).
- Pumpkinseed eat bluegill eggs and larvae from the water column at bluegill colonies (Gross & MacMillan 1981, Lake Opinicon) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Maturity and longevity: tabulated as maturity at age 2 and 59 mm SL (Portt et al. 1988) and maximum known age 8 (inferred from Carlander 1969 and Smith 1985) — Coker, Portt & Minns 2001, *Morphological and ecological characteristics of Canadian freshwater fishes*, Can. Manuscr. Rep. Fish. Aquat. Sci. 2554. Read from a local copy; no URL verified.

### Inferences
- **Timing of the diet shift.** For the sim, pumpkinseed fry/YOY can use the bluegill early-life parameters, with the "snail-crushing" diet switch ramping from ~45 mm SL to fully molluscivorous by ~75 mm SL. Under ~40 mm SL the two species are near-equal competitors in vegetation (Mittelbach 1984; Osenberg et al. 1988).
- **Abundance from adult food.** Pumpkinseed abundance relative to bluegill should be driven by the snail standing stock. A 25:1 to 1:1 bluegill:pumpkinseed ratio is the plausible realised range.

### Gaps
- No primary numeric data were retrieved on pumpkinseed:
  - spawning temperature or season;
  - nest density or eggs per nest;
  - hatch time;
  - YOY autumn length;
  - per-ha density or biomass;
  - adult annual mortality.
- Fox (1994, Ecology 75:1157) and Danylchuk & Fox (1994) on Ontario pumpkinseed life histories were identified but not retrieved. The web-search budget was exhausted.
- Michigan statewide pumpkinseed length-at-age means were not obtained. One search snippet showed a DNR table (A4-1) with values such as 4.4 in at age II and 5.9 in at age IV, but the file returned 404 and could not be verified.

## Yellow perch (Perca flavescens): spawning, egg ribbons, hatch, pelagic larvae, first feeding, survival vs zooplankton, shifts to benthos and piscivory, cannibalism, year-class variability, YOY size, adult density

### Takeaway
Yellow perch spawn earliest of these species. Eggs are laid as gelatinous ribbons at ~6–12 °C in April–May, and hatch after ~2–3 weeks (about 100 °C-days above 5 °C).
- **Larvae:** pelagic and offshore. They start on copepod nauplii, move to adult copepods by ~7 mm, then to cladocerans. They migrate inshore with diel movements and are fully nearshore by ~30 mm TL.
- **Juveniles:** take macroinvertebrates from ~50 mm.
- **Early survival:** low and variable. Egg → 8 mm larva survival was 1.6–18.4% in Oneida Lake. Larval Z was 0.15–0.52 d⁻¹ in a Nebraska lake, inversely related to zooplankton biomass and temperature.
- **Feedback on zooplankton:** strong YOY cohorts above ~20–40 kg/ha eliminate *Daphnia pulex* in late summer, depressing their own growth.
- **Year classes:** hugely variable. Mechanisms include walleye predation (depensatory in the 1960s), size-selective overwinter starvation, and intraspecific cannibalism and competition, which can produce ~5-year cohort cycles.

### Cited Findings

**Spawning, eggs, hatch**
- Spawning temperature and timing (Ontario): 6–12 °C in April–May. The species is a non-guarding, open-substratum phyto-lithophil spawner.
  - Fecundity: 950–210,000 eggs per female.
  - Maturity: males at 2–3 yr, females at 3–4 yr.
  - Lifespan: 7–12 yr. Preferred temperature 18–24 °C.

  Source: [Ontario Freshwater Fishes Life History Database, yellow perch](https://www.ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=149).
- Eggs are released as a gelatinous, accordion-folded strand ("ribbon") that tangles on vegetation or debris or rests on the bottom — [The Fish Site species profile](https://thefishsite.com/articles/species-profile-yellow-perch-perca-flavescens). Secondary aquaculture source.
- Incubation is ~95–108 degree-days (°C) above 5 °C, about 12 days at 13–15 °C — SRAC aquaculture fact sheet ([srac.tamu.edu/fact-sheets/serve/188](https://srac.tamu.edu/fact-sheets/serve/188)). HATCHERY value, seen in a search summary only; the site later failed DNS, so it is unverified.
- Larvae hatch near shore over a 4–6 week period and are quickly carried offshore, where pelagic durations are prolonged (southwestern Lake Michigan) — [Weber, Dettmers & Wahl 2011, TAFS 140:1172–1185](https://doi.org/10.1080/00028487.2011.608605). Great Lakes system; pelagic durations are longer than in small lakes.
- Pelican Lake, Nebraska (332 ha; 2004–12; ~42°N, southern edge of the region): hatch cohorts ran 14 April–23 May — [Kaemingk, Graeb & Willis 2014, TAFS 143:845–855](https://doi.org/10.1080/00028487.2014.886622).

**Pelagic larval stage, first feeding, diet shifts**
- Lake St. George, Ontario (Post & McQueen 1988), distribution changed with development:
  - early larvae were exclusively offshore, day and night;
  - larger larvae and juveniles moved nearshore to offshore at dusk and back at dawn;
  - this diel migration weakened with growth;
  - by ~30 mm TL, juveniles were exclusively nearshore day and night;
  - the authors argue the pattern is typical of both *Perca* species, not a proximate response to prey or predators.

  Source: [Post & McQueen 1988, CJFAS 45:1820–1826](https://doi.org/10.1139/f88-214).
- Lake Mendota: age-0 yellow perch had a pelagic phase within the 3–7-week range observed across taxa, and numerically dominated pelagic age-0 fish through most of June. Densities were aggregated, with CV 48–154% among replicate sites — [Post, Rudstam & Schael 1995](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1995)124<0084:TASDOP>2.3.CO;2).
- Oneida Lake staging used for analysis:
  - egg deposition;
  - 18 mm TL, taken as the end of the larval stage;
  - 1 August, ending the "limnetic stage" from 18 mm;
  - 15 October, ending the "demersal stage".

  Source: [Irwin et al. 2009, TAFS 138:99–110](https://doi.org/10.1577/T07-256.1).
- First foods, two South Dakota glacial lakes (Fisher & Willis 1997):
  - copepod nauplii first, switching to adult copepods by 7 mm TL (earlier when nauplii were scarce);
  - then increasing cladocerans;
  - in Pickerel Lake, juveniles ate cladocerans and some adult copepods until ~50 mm TL, then increasingly macroinvertebrates;
  - in Pelican Lake, macroinvertebrates stayed a small diet fraction even after 50 mm.

  Source: [Fisher & Willis 1997, J. Freshw. Ecol. 12:421–429](https://doi.org/10.1080/02705060.1997.9663552).
- Prey selection across 14 Michigan lakes of varied trophic status ran nauplii (smallest larvae) → cyclopoid copepods → *Bosmina* and calanoids → large *Daphnia*. The shifts "occur quite rapidly" with growth, and did not differ by trophic status — [McCullough & Hayes 2026, Environ. Biol. Fish. 109(3)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10641-026-01863-3).

**Larval and YOY growth and survival; dependence on zooplankton**
- Oneida Lake, NY (Clady 1976), 1969–75 year-classes:
  - egg → larva survival (to a mean of 8 mm) was 1.6–18.4%, with a base mortality of 80+% in all years;
  - mean air temperature in the 3 weeks before 8 mm (positive) and mean wind in the 4 weeks before (negative) explained 87% of survival variance.

  Source: [Clady 1976, JFRBC 33:1887–1893](https://doi.org/10.1139/f76-241).
- Pelican Lake, NE, 2004–12 (Kaemingk et al. 2014):
  - young larvae (5–14 d) grew 0.09–0.50 mm/d, with growth increasing with temperature and later hatch date;
  - old larvae (15–24 d) grew up to 0.39 mm/d;
  - larval mortality was Z = 0.15–0.52 (daily instantaneous), inversely related to total zooplankton biomass (strongest predictor) and temperature;
  - the authors describe two bottlenecks: post-hatch (temperature and hatch date) and old-larva (temperature and zooplankton).

  Source: [Kaemingk et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00028487.2014.886622).
- Oneida Lake, 1975–79 (Mills & Forney 1983):
  - *Daphnia pulex* tolerated predation by ~10 kg/ha of age-0 yellow perch;
  - reproduction could not compensate when age-0 biomass exceeded 20–40 kg/ha;
  - young-perch consumption exceeded *D. pulex* production in 1975 and 1977, when daphnids disappeared;
  - maximum daily consumption was < 27% of production in 1976, when daphnids stayed abundant.

  Source: [Mills & Forney 1983, TAFS 112:154–161](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1983)112<154:IODPOP>2.0.CO;2).
- Large YOY perch year-classes in Oneida Lake can reduce large-bodied daphnids and thereby their own growth; early larval mortality is influenced by temperature and high winds — [Hall & Rudstam 1999, Hydrobiologia](https://halllab2.sitehost.iu.edu/Publications/Hall%20and%20Rudstam%201999%20Hydrobiologia.pdf). Seen as a search snippet; the PDF host's TLS certificate was invalid, so the full text was not read.
- South Dakota: August juvenile catch was 8× higher in 1996 than 1995 in Pickerel Lake, and correlated with peak abundance of ≤ 10 mm larvae (r = 0.996). Year-class strength "may have been set at an early life stage" — [Fisher & Willis 1997](https://doi.org/10.1080/02705060.1997.9663552).
- Oneida Lake, > 40 years of data (Irwin et al. 2009):
  - age-0 densities at all four stages were lower recently than in the 1960s–70s;
  - egg → 18 mm mortality showed no trend; 18 mm → 1 Aug mortality increased; 1 Aug → 15 Oct mortality decreased;
  - mortality is "no longer depensatory";
  - age-0 growth increased and became density-dependent, so end-of-year size increased ("delayed compensation" via lower winter mortality).

  Source: [Irwin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1577/T07-256.1).
- Oneida Lake long-term survey charts (Cornell Biological Field Station; Brooking, Rudstam et al., 2026 presentation):
  - y-axis scales reach 160,000 per acre for 18-mm yellow perch and 3,000 per acre for October age-0 perch;
  - adult population estimates run to the millions of fish (axis to 6,000,000);
  - "YP populations still above average" as of 2025.

  Source: [Oneida Lake Association 2026 fisheries presentation](https://oneidalakeassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/OLA-Annual-Meeting-Oneida-Lake-Fisheries-2025-04-29-2026.pdf). Values read only from chart axes; exact yearly values were not extractable.
- A Lake Winnebago, WI study reported yellow perch year-class strength set by midsummer of the first year. Over 13 years it was not correlated with May–August water temperature — [WDNR / UW digital collection PDF](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/Q7ZAK5G4ZJ4MW87/E/file-b9526.pdf). Seen as a search snippet; the PDF exceeded fetch size limits and was not read.

**Overwinter survival, size selection, cannibalism, predation, cycles**
- Lake St. George, ON (Post & Evans 1989):
  - in the lab, under a simulated winter, fed and starved age-0 perch suffered 1% and 46% mortality respectively;
  - small fish lost proportionally more mass, and winter duration drove both total and size-selective mortality;
  - size-selective overwinter mortality was confirmed in an in-situ enclosure and in five natural cohorts;
  - simulation suggested overwinter starvation can cause substantial year-class variability, independent of adult stock.

  Source: [Post & Evans 1989, CJFAS 46:1958–1968](https://doi.org/10.1139/f89-246).
- Otolith evidence of strong size-selective mortality within the first growing season in a slow-growing population, and minimal selection in a faster-growing one; size-selective predation favours fast growers — [Post & Prankevicius 1987, CJFAS 44:1840–1847](https://doi.org/10.1139/f87-228).
- Oneida Lake, 1959–68 (Forney 1971):
  - dominant year-classes were 1960, 1962, 1964 and 1968;
  - year-class divergence grew from age 0 to age 1;
  - weak year-classes had higher August–October mortality, i.e. depensatory mortality consistent with walleye predation.

  Source: [Forney 1971, TAFS 100:739–749](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1971)100<739:DODYCI>2.0.CO;2).
- Crystal Lake, WI (oligotrophic; Sanderson et al. 1999):
  - three episodes since 1981 in which two age-classes dominated for ~5 years;
  - YOY appeared only in 1981–82, 1986–87 and 1990–91;
  - YOY presence was negatively related to juvenile abundance and positively to adult abundance, consistent with cannibalism and juvenile–YOY competition generating cycles.

  Source: [Sanderson, Hrabik, Magnuson & Post 1999, CJFAS 56:1534–1542](https://doi.org/10.1139/f99-077).
- Theory: in size-structured cannibalistic perch, the lower limit of the cannibal's prey:predator length window controls whether cannibalism regulates recruitment (many year-classes) or not (single-cohort cycles) — [Claessen et al. 2002, Ecology 83:1660](https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658(2002)083[1660:TIOSDP]2.0.CO;2).
- Eurasian perch ponds (related species, Europe): YOY perch became piscivorous within a few weeks of hatch when prey fish hatched ~12 days later, producing bimodal cohorts and intra-cohort cannibalism — [Heermann & Borcherding 2012, J. Appl. Ichthyol. 29:549–554](https://doi.org/10.1111/jai.12044).
- Adult yellow perch also eat age-0 bluegill in winter. In Cedar Lake, MI, age-0 bluegill were 84% of winter food volume in 185 perch stomachs (Moffett & Hunt 1945) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- An individual-based model of Oneida walleye–perch predicted that compensation is driven by density-dependent growth (earlier maturation, higher fecundity) — [Rose et al. 1999, Ecol. Monogr. 69:127](https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9615(1999)069[0127:IBMOYP]2.0.CO;2).

  | Round Lake | 6.2 | 7.6 | 9.0 | 9.6 | 11.0 | 11.7 | 12.2 |

### Inferences
- **Stage durations and growth for calibration.**
  - Larvae are offshore and planktivorous from hatch (~5–6 mm; hatch length NOT verified here) to ~18 mm.
  - From 18 mm to ~30 mm TL they move inshore with diel migrations, settling demersally by about August.
  - Larval growth runs ~0.1–0.5 mm/d.
  - At Z = 0.15–0.52 d⁻¹ over a ~20-day larval period, larval survival is roughly e⁻³ to e⁻¹⁰ (≈ 5% down to 0.005%). This is my arithmetic, not a published survival figure.
- **Zooplankton coupling.** Because Daphnia collapses at age-0 perch biomass > ~20–40 kg/ha (Oneida), a sim YOY-perch school should be able to graze Daphnia to near zero in strong years. Its growth should then drop, creating the observed self-limitation.
- **First-winter rule.** A size-dependent first-winter starvation rule is supported for perch: small fish lose more mass, and starved fish suffer 46% mortality vs 1% when fed.
- **Year-class variability.** Oneida chart axes imply 18-mm densities up to ~10⁵ per acre (~2.5×10⁵/ha) falling to October densities of ≤ ~3×10³ per acre (≤ ~7×10³/ha). That is ~1–2% survival from 18 mm to October in high years, an order-of-magnitude inference from axes only.

## Fathead minnow (Pimephales promelas): repeated spawning, male guarding, maturity, lifespan, densities with and without piscivores

### Takeaway
Fathead minnows are fractional spawners.
- **Female output:** 16–26 clutches per season at ~4-day intervals, ~400–480 eggs per clutch, ~7,000–10,000 eggs per season.
- **Male nests:** males guard nests on the undersides of objects in < 1 m of water. Nests last ~1–2 weeks, and eggs hatch in ~4–6 days.
- **Season:** starts at ~16–18 °C (late May/June) and runs into August.
- **Life history varies with latitude and predators:**
  - North Dakota fatheads mostly mature in their second summer and then die (max ~3 yr);
  - Alberta populations reach 5+ yr, with 50% maturity at age 3 where pike are absent;
  - with northern pike present, > 90% mature by age 2.
- **Density:** enormous in predator-free shallow waters — 52,000–356,000/ha in early June in prairie wetlands, dropping to 5,400–19,700/ha by late August.
- **Disturbance:** winterkill cuts populations 47–94% in about a quarter of lake-winters in boreal Alberta.

### Cited Findings
- Pool experiment, 20 May–20 Oct 1980 (Gale & Buynak 1982):
  - each of 5 pairs produced 16–26 clutches between 22 May and 22 August;
  - total eggs per female ranged 6,803–10,164 (mean 8,604);
  - eggs per clutch ranged 9–1,136, with per-female means of 391–480;
  - spawning intervals were 2–16 days (mean 3.9, mode 3);
  - no post-spawning mortality occurred.

  Source: [Gale & Buynak 1982, TAFS 111:35–40](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1982)111<35:FASFOT>2.0.CO;2). Outdoor pool experiment, not a lake.
- Northern Alberta (Silver 2008):
  - spawning begins in late May or early June at 15.6–17.8 °C and extends to August (Dobie et al. 1956; Nelson & Paetz 1992);
  - incubation is temperature-dependent, with 4.5–6 d "typical" (Scott & Crossman);
  - females release ~400 eggs per session and up to 10,000 per summer (Gale & Buynak 1982);
  - males hold nest sites on the undersurface of vegetation, wood or rocks in < 1 m of water, and more than one female spawns per nest.

  Source: [Silver 2008, MSc thesis, Univ. Alberta](https://doi.org/10.7939/r3-jrj8-xs83).
- Common-garden ponds, Meanook, Alberta, 2006 (Silver 2008, Table 3-3), comparing pike-experienced (Duck Lake) vs pike-naïve (Sara Lake) stock:

  | Measure | Duck (pike-experienced) | Sara (pike-naïve) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Nesting season (d) | 23 ± 3 | 30 ± 3 |
  | Nest duration (d) | 7.8 ± 0.7 | 13.9 ± 1.3 |
  | Batch size (eggs) | 428 ± 35 | 362 ± 33 |
  | Maximum nest load (eggs) | 927 | 1,299 |
  | Nest-days with male defender present | 32.4% | 56.3% |
  | Incubation (d) | 3.8 ± 0.2 | 4.9 ± 0.2 |
  | Nest failure (0% hatch) | 31.8% | 0% |
  | Egg survival to hatch | 37.2% | 75.5% |
  | Hatch → fall survival | 46.5% | 24.3% |
  | YOY per pond half in fall | ~1,215 | ~1,781 |

  - Season dates: 29 Jun–29 Jul (31 d) for Duck and 2 Jul–17 Aug (47 d) for Sara.
  - Duck YOY were larger in fall.

  Source: [Silver 2008](https://doi.org/10.7939/r3-jrj8-xs83).
- Natural Alberta lakes, with vs without pike (Silver 2008):
  - maximum age was 5+ yr in all three lakes (4+ for Sara Lake males);
  - in the pike lake (Duck), > 90% were mature by age 2;
  - in pike-free lakes, 50% maturity was reached at age 3 (Armstrong, Sara);
  - fathead CPUE was significantly lower in the pike lake during most of summer;
  - back-calculated TL at ages 1 and 2 was 52 and 56 mm when coexisting with pike, vs 48 and 51 mm after pike were extirpated (Armstrong Lake);
  - fatheads are "extremely susceptible" to pike predation (Robinson & Tonn 1989; Jones & Paszkowski 1997).

  Source: [Silver 2008](https://doi.org/10.7939/r3-jrj8-xs83).
- 150 monitored nests:
  - 29% failed to produce any hatchlings, and egg mortality was mostly from conspecific nest predation;
  - nests with observed paternal care were larger, lasted longer and were more likely to hatch;
  - over one-third of males were aggressive toward intruders;
  - larger males nested earlier.

  Source: [Divino & Tonn 2008, Copeia 2008(4):920–930](https://doi.org/10.1643/CE-06-245).
- Males remain with the eggs until they hatch, and females may spawn several times a summer — [New Hampshire Fish & Game fathead minnow page](https://www.wildlife.nh.gov/fishing-new-hampshire/fish-species-nh/fathead-minnow). Seen via search summary.
- North Dakota saline prairie lakes (Held & Peterka 1974):
  - fry appeared in early June and were present through summer (1 July–1 September);
  - mean TL was 41 mm at the end of year 1 and 58 mm at the end of year 2;
  - only 1 of 2,100 fish aged had 3 annuli;
  - "most fathead minnows … matured and spawned during their second summer and then died";
  - the largest fish was 79 mm and 6.7 g;
  - diet was dominated by Cladocera, then copepods and amphipods;
  - partial winterkills stimulate fast growth of survivors.

  Source: [Held & Peterka 1974, TAFS 103:743–756](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1974)103<743:AGAFHO>2.0.CO;2).
- Maturity at age 1 at 50 mm SL (Portt et al. 1988) and maximum age 3 (Portt et al. 1988) are tabulated in Coker, Portt & Minns 2001 (Can. Manuscr. Rep. Fish. Aquat. Sci. 2554). Read from a local copy; no URL verified.
- Prairie wetlands, predator-dominated differences (Duffy 1998):
  - density was 52,000–356,000/ha in early June and 5,400–19,700/ha in late August;
  - biomass was 144–482 kg/ha in early June and 1–33 kg/ha in late August;
  - net production was 71.5–202.7 kg/ha over the study period;
  - daily net production was 2.6–13.5 kg/ha/d in early June and 0.1–1.2 kg/ha/d in July–August;
  - differences in standing biomass were attributed to predation pressure rather than harvest.

  Source: [Duffy 1998, CJFAS 55:15–27 (USGS record)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/population-dynamics-production-and-prey-consumption-fathead-minnows-pimephales).
- Four small boreal Alberta lakes over 5 years (Danylchuk & Tonn 2003):
  - densities fell 47–94% year-to-year after extremely low winter O₂;
  - presumed winterkills followed 25% of lake-winters;
  - smaller fish were more strongly affected than larger ones.

  Source: [Danylchuk & Tonn 2003, TAFS 132:289–298](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(2003)132<0289:NDAFLA>2.0.CO;2).
- No size-related first-winter mortality was detected in a fathead population — [Toneys & Coble 1979, TAFS 108:415–419](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1979)108<415:SFWMOF>2.0.CO;2).
- Management stocking guidance (Missouri): 300–500 fatheads per surface acre (740–1,240/ha) as forage, stocked a season before bass — [Missouri Dept. of Conservation fathead minnow sheet](https://mdc.mo.gov/sites/default/files/2020-05/fathead_minnow.pdf). Management target, not a natural density; seen via search summary.
- Esocids capture fathead minnows far more efficiently than bluegill: 0.67 vs 0.14 captures per strike in the lab (Wahl & Stein 1988) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Spawning parameters for the sim.**
  - Season: from ~16–18 °C to mid/late August.
  - Clutch every ~3–4 days, ~400 eggs.
  - Incubation ~4–6 d (3.8–4.9 d measured at Alberta pond temperatures).
  - A male's nest lasts ~1–2 weeks.
- **Egg → fall survival, predator-free ponds:** ≈ egg survival (37–76%) × hatch-to-fall survival (24–47%) ≈ 9–35%. This is my product of Silver's means.
- **Lifespan should depend on growing season and predation.** A "mostly semelparous at age 1–2" setting matches North Dakota/prairie populations. Note the conflict: Gale & Buynak saw no post-spawning mortality in pools.
- **Lake densities.** Lake densities with piscivores are probably 1–3 orders of magnitude below the 10⁴–10⁵/ha of predator-free wetlands. No direct per-ha lake value with pike or bass was found.

### Gaps
- No direct per-ha density was found for fatheads in a lake with bass or pike; only relative CPUE (Silver 2008) and distribution statements.
- The duration of male guarding per brood, and male post-season mortality in natural north-temperate lakes, were not quantified beyond the pond nest durations.
- Divino & Tonn 2007 (Ecol. Freshw. Fish 16:165–176, hatch-date effects on recruitment) and Danylchuk & Tonn 2006 (J. Fish Biol. 68:681–694, winterkill life-history effects) were identified but not read.

## Brown bullhead (Ameiurus nebulosus): spawning, guarding of fry balls, fry diet, first-year growth

### Takeaway
In Michigan, brown bullhead pair monogamously once a year.
- **Spawning:** late May–early June (Munro Lake), with most egg-laying in a 10–15-day window.
- **Care:** both parents tend nests, males most; care extends to schooling juveniles for up to ~20 days after oviposition.
- **Development:** eggs hatch in ~6–9 days (reported mean 5.6 d, max 13 d).
- **Juvenile diet:** a mixed diet of Crustacea (~60%: ostracods and cladocerans) and chironomid larvae (~25%) from June onward. They are partly benthic from early life, not purely planktivorous.
- **First-year size:** mean ≤ 76 mm TL by October in Cayuga Lake, NY, with some fish near 100 mm.
- **Maturity:** first breeding at age 3.

### Cited Findings
- Munro Lake, Michigan (Blumer 1985):
  - reproductive activity begins in May, and spawning occurs in late May and early June;
  - "most oviposition occurs during a 10- to 15-day period each year";
  - adults care for eggs, larvae and juveniles "as long as 20 days after oviposition";
  - care includes nest building, guarding, fanning and manipulating eggs and larvae;
  - each sex has one mate per season;
  - males alone give care more often than females alone, and males are the principal care-givers.

  Source: [Blumer 1985, Am. Midl. Nat. 114:318–330](https://doi.org/10.2307/2425607).
- Blumer's thesis: bullheads bred once a year with monogamous matings. Parental presence improved offspring survival, mainly by reducing predation, and two parents improved survival more than one. Lone males guarded more than paired males. Females fed during care while males fed little or not at all. Reproduction cost females more weight and condition. Source: [Blumer, Univ. Michigan PhD (abstract)](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/8a87c33f-8b48-4022-a2ea-78541cc47786), via search summary.
- Secondary compilation (Animal Diversity Web):
  - spawning peaked "within the first 16 days of June" in a 4-year Michigan study (Blumer 1985);
  - hatch takes up to 13 days, mean 5.6 d (Blumer 1985);
  - the larval stage lasts 4–9 d, mean 4.4 d (Blumer 1985);
  - brood care was by both sexes in 56.2% of broods, males only in 39.3% and females only in 4.5% (Blumer 1985, 1986a).

  Uncited on the page:
  - mean daily water temperature during reproduction 14–29 °C;
  - egg clusters 50–10,000 eggs;
  - egg diameter 2.2–2.7 mm;
  - hatchlings 4–8 mm;
  - average time to independence 9.4 d, maximum care 29 d;
  - maturity at 3 yr;
  - lifespan 6–8 yr (max 9).

  Source: [ADW, Ameiurus nebulosus](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Ameiurus_nebulosus).
- Eggs hatch in 6–9 days and adults usually first breed in their third year (Becker 1983; Moyle 1976) — [Encyclopedia of Puget Sound species profile](https://www.eopugetsound.org/node/15407).
- Michigan DNR: parents accompany the young "until they reach about two inches in length" (~50 mm) — [Michigan DNR bullheads page](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/bullheads). Seen via search summary; direct fetch returned HTTP 403.
- Fry diet and first-season growth, Cayuga Lake, NY (Raney & Webster 1940):
  - 170 young were sampled 21 June–10 October 1938 in quiet coves;
  - Crustacea were ~60% of the diet, 80% of it ostracods and cladocerans;
  - Diptera were ~25%, almost all chironomid larvae;
  - sizes diverged after mid-July, and by 10 October the largest was > 2× the length of the smallest;
  - "mean total length does not exceed 3 inches [76 mm] at the end of the first growing season", but some reach "nearly 4 inches" (~100 mm).

  Source: [Raney & Webster 1940, TAFS 69:205–209](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1939)69[205:TFAGOT]2.0.CO;2).
- Black bullhead (congener, not brown): free-swimming fry stay near the male about 2 weeks, reaching ~25 mm TL while with him, and ~100 mm by the end of year 1 — [BioKIDS, Ameiurus melas](https://www.biokids.umich.edu/critters/Ameiurus_melas/).
- Bullheads prey on bluegill nests. In Lake Opinicon, fishes (mostly *Ictalurus*) caused 7% of substrate-level brood predation (Gross & MacMillan 1981). Young bullheads are a preferred "buffer" prey that can blunt bass control of bluegill (Lewis & Helms 1964; Snow 1971). Both via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

### Inferences
- **Brood timeline for the sim.**
  1. Spawn when water reaches the high teens °C, in late May–early June (Michigan).
  2. Hatch ~6 d later.
  3. Yolk-sac larvae stay on the nest ~4–5 d.
  4. A guarded, schooling "fry ball" stays near the parents to ~20 days after laying.
- At typical early-summer growth, young would leave care at ~20–30 mm. Michigan DNR's "about two inches" is probably an upper bound or a loose figure. This conflicts with Blumer's 20-day maximum care, which is the primary source.
- **Feeding:** from swim-up, fry can be modelled as feeding on both zooplankton (cladocerans, ostracods) and bottom food (chironomids), at roughly a 60:25 mix.
- **End of first summer:** ~50–76 mm TL mean in NY/MI-type lakes, with a wide size spread (~2×).

### Gaps
- Not found:
  - brown bullhead fry survival on and off the nest;
  - YOY density;
  - overwinter survival;
  - adult natural mortality.
- Primary spawning-temperature data for northern populations were not found. The 14–29 °C figure is uncited on ADW.
- Eggs per clutch (50–10,000, ADW) lacks a primary citation. Scott & Crossman 1973 and Becker 1983 were not read directly.

## All species: egg-to-autumn and first-winter survival, and adult natural annual mortality

### Takeaway
Most early mortality falls in the first days after swim-up or first feeding.
- Bluegill fry: Z ≈ 0.1–0.4 d⁻¹ in the first 4 days after dispersal.
- Perch: 80+% egg-to-larva loss; larval Z 0.15–0.52 d⁻¹.

Survival from egg (or hatch) to autumn spans ~0–30% even in predator-free systems, and is far lower with piscivores. First-winter loss ranges from ~10% (no predators) to 75–92% (with walleye or bass). Evidence that it is size-selective is mixed: strong for perch and in northern bluegill populations, weak in southern bluegill and in fathead.

Adult annual natural mortality is ~30–60% in Upper-Midwest bluegill. Fathead minnows are effectively annual to short-lived (2–5 yr). Brown bullhead and yellow perch live 7–12 yr; no rate found.

### Cited Findings

**Bluegill**
- Predator-free NW Wisconsin lakes (Beard 1982):
  - 90–100% of hatched fry left the nest;
  - mortality from dispersal to day 4 was much higher than in later stages, and "has a great influence on year class strength";
  - October fingerlings were 0.1–376,000/ha against 433,000–1,491,000 potentially hatching eggs/ha.

  Source: [Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf).
- Ridge Lake, IL (14 acres; central Illinois):
  - young largemouth bass consumed 58–93% of littoral bluegill fry. The bass averaged 179/acre (442/ha) "[≤?] age 3"; the comparison symbol is garbled in the review's text, but the fish are described as young bass (via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf));
  - first-winter losses were 75–88%, with little size-specific mortality ([Santucci & Wahl 2003](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(2003)132<0346:TEOGPA>2.0.CO;2)).
- Michigan 0.5-acre ponds, 4 winters (Schneider & Breck 1997): overwinter mortality of age-0 bluegill (28 mm) averaged:
  - 10% with no predators;
  - 39% with some predator;
  - 55% (range 25–89%) with walleye as the sole predator.

  Via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Jewett Lake, MI: total mortality from fall age-0 to fall age-1 was A = 0.92 under walleye and perch predation (Schneider 1997) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Lake Opinicon: early-born fry had "strikingly higher survivorship to age 1", attributed probably to over-winter survival and size-selective predation ([Cargnelli & Gross 1996](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-193)). In 1998 the reverse held: early larvae suffered high mortality, and late-hatched larvae had higher survival probabilities ([Garvey et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1890/1051-0761(2002)012[0194:PRISTT]2.0.CO;2)).
- Size-related first-winter mortality was detected in only 1 of 3 bluegill populations, and not in fathead minnow. It is "probably not a common phenomenon for bluegill and largemouth bass" — [Toneys & Coble 1979](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1979)108<415:SFWMOF>2.0.CO;2).
- Copper sulphate poisoning of nest sites in 4 Michigan lakes (80–200 acres), from the onset of spawning (18 May) to its end (9 August), did not reduce fall age-0 catch. This implies strong compensatory mortality later in the first year (Beyerle & Williams 1967) — via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).
- Adults, Murphy Flowage, WI: natural mortality 46% (32–59%) and total 61% (49–74%) (Snow 1978). In Alexandria-chain lakes, MN, total mortality of quality fish was 75% (Parsons & Reed 1998). Both via [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf).

**Yellow perch**
- Oneida Lake: egg → 8 mm larva survival 1.6–18.4% (1969–75) — [Clady 1976](https://doi.org/10.1139/f76-241).
- Pelican Lake, NE: larval Z 0.15–0.52 — [Kaemingk et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00028487.2014.886622).
- Lab overwinter: starved 46% vs fed 1% mortality, size-selective — [Post & Evans 1989](https://doi.org/10.1139/f89-246).
- Oneida Lake: mortality from 18 mm to 1 August has increased over 40 years, while mortality from 1 August to 15 October has decreased — [Irwin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1577/T07-256.1).

**Fathead minnow**
- Alberta ponds: egg survival to hatch 37–76% and hatch → fall survival 24–47% — [Silver 2008](https://doi.org/10.7939/r3-jrj8-xs83).
- Winterkill: 47–94% declines in 25% of lake-winters — [Danylchuk & Tonn 2003](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(2003)132<0289:NDAFLA>2.0.CO;2).
- North Dakota: most adults die after spawning in their second summer — [Held & Peterka 1974](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1974)103<743:AGAFHO>2.0.CO;2).

**Brown bullhead**
- Parental guarding raises brood survival mainly by reducing predation — [Blumer thesis abstract](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/8a87c33f-8b48-4022-a2ea-78541cc47786). No numeric survival values were found.

### Inferences
Suggested calibration targets for a small mesotrophic lake with bass and pike present. These are my synthesis of the ranges above, not published composites.

| Species | Egg → autumn | First winter | Adult natural mortality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bluegill | ~0.1–2% (vs ~9% median with no predators) | 30–75% loss | ~35–50% /yr |
| Yellow perch | ~0.01–1% | 20–50% loss, higher for small or late fish | not sourced; generic short-lived-percid prior (unverified) ~30–50% /yr |
| Fathead minnow | ~5–30% (no predators); much lower with pike | Major loss mainly in winterkill years (50–90%) | Most die at age 1–2 in short-lived populations |
| Brown bullhead | not sourced | not sourced | not sourced |

- **Bluegill:** egg → yearling is ~0.05–1%.
- **Brown bullhead:** guarded broods suggest higher egg/fry survival than for non-guarders, but this is unquantified.
- **Recruitment variability:** for every species it should be multiplicative and front-loaded. Simple density dependence acting on YOY growth (via zooplankton depletion), plus size-dependent winter loss, reproduces the "delayed compensation" seen in Oneida perch and the stunting dynamics of bluegill.

  - field per-ha YOY densities for any of these species in a small mesotrophic lake with a full predator community. Beard's lakes were predator-free; Oneida is large and eutrophic.
