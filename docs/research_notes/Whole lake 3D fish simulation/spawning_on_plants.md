# Spawning on plants and wood: how the Hot Brook fish use aquatic plants and woody debris as spawning substrate, and how egg and larval survival depend on it

Scope: Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake (Danforth/Weston, Maine). They are soft, tea-coloured, shallow (2.5–5.5 m) and unstratified, and ice goes out in late April. The sim already holds 13 plant species as biomass per bed cell; it currently places spawning grounds by depth only.

How each source was read:
- **[full text]**: I read the paper or report itself (PDF text or HTML).
- **[abstract]**: I read only the abstract or catalogue record.
- **[snippet]**: the number comes from a search-engine summary of the page; treat it as unverified.
- **[secondary]**: the primary is cited inside another work I read.

Regions are given with each finding. A surrogate species is labelled **SURROGATE**.

Blocked or unavailable pages (none bypassed):
- maine.gov MDIFW plans returned 403.
- dr.lib.iastate.edu (the Iowa thesis) returned 403.
- deepblue.lib.umich.edu returned 403.
- kmae-journal.org returned 403.
- The int-res.com MEPS PDF (Snickars 2010) returned 429 (rate-limited).

A cross-cutting timing point used throughout: perch (≈7–13 °C) and pickerel (≈6–11 °C) spawn in the first 1–3 weeks after ice-out. So their substrate is last year's standing dead stems, wood, and plants that overwinter green. It is not the current year's plant growth.

## 1. Yellow perch egg ribbons: substrate, depth, temperature, survival on structure vs bare bed, ribbon density

### Takeaway
Perch drape their single annual ribbon on rigid, erect, branching structure:
- dead emergent and submerged stems;
- brush and woody debris;
- rigid plants such as charophytes;
- live soft submerged plants only when nothing better exists.

They avoid bare bottom and periphyton-coated surfaces.

Ribbons lying on the bare bed survive markedly worse:
- In the lab: 46% vs 99% hatch from the eyed stage, so about 0.47× (yellow perch).
- In the field (Eurasian perch): significantly more dead embryos on the bed, though I could not get the number.

Removing wood from a lake with sparse plants coincided with near-collapse of perch recruitment. In tea-coloured (higher-DOC) lakes perch spawn very shallow (most eggs < 1 m), unless the shore is wave-exposed.

### Cited Findings
**What the ribbons are laid on**
- USFWS: yellow perch eggs are deposited in long ribbons along submerged vegetation, dead branches and trees, and hatch in about 12 days depending on temperature — [USFWS](https://www.fws.gov/fisheries/freshwater-fish-of-america/yellow_perch.html) [snippet]
- NH Fish & Game: eggs are strewn in long ribbons over dead vegetation and the branches of fallen trees (northeast US) — [NH Fish & Game](https://www.wildlife.nh.gov/fishing-new-hampshire/fish-species-nh/yellow-perch) [snippet]
- The ribbon is the whole year's clutch. After water-hardening it can be over 2 m long (Hardy 1978, cited in Ward & Barnes 2020) — [Ward & Barnes 2020](https://www.arcjournals.org/pdfs/ijisabf/v6-i2/1.pdf) [full text; secondary for the 2 m]
- Perch (both species) put all of a year's eggs into one clutch, so a bad site choice "may easily result in complete failure" of a female's reproduction — [Čech et al. 2012, J. Limnol. 71:170–179](https://www.jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/jlimnol.2012.e18/468/935) [full text]
- Pickerel Lake, South Dakota (1995):
  - perch began spawning at 7 °C;
  - they preferred **periphyton-free woody structure** along shorelines of the main lake;
  - all 18 egg masses examined were developing normally;
  - no egg masses were found in Pelican Lake (SD) — [Fisher 1996, SDSU MS thesis](https://openprairie.sdstate.edu/etd/354) [abstract]
- Eastern South Dakota semi-permanent wetlands (2002–03), with conifer "tree reefs" added:
  - perch chose inshore reefs over offshore ones;
  - more egg masses were found on short-needle spruce than on long-needle fir or pine;
  - reefs aged 1–3 years were used as much as new ones — [Mangan 2004, SDSU MS thesis](https://openprairie.sdstate.edu/etd/530) [abstract]
- Iowa (2020 snorkel survey):
  - 30 eastern red cedars at 5 branch-complexity levels, from all branches removed to all retained;
  - 35 skeins in total;
  - deposition was non-random toward the two most complex levels and random among the three least complex;
  - a nest-survival model gave a 27-day skein survival of 0.07 for some levels, lower than the other skeins (the comparison was truncated in what I could see) — [Iowa State thesis, "Importance of Coarse Woody Habitat Complexity to Yellow Perch Egg Skein Deposition and Survival"](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/handle/20.500.12876/0zEy6Z9z) [snippet; page 403]
- Southeastern Lake Michigan (1980–81, divers):
  - egg masses lay **exclusively on rough substrate**, at up to 184 per 1000 m² but usually fewer than 10 per 1000 m²; the authors inferred spawning was not substrate-limited there;
  - egg masses planted at 6 and 9 m were carried off smooth bottom by currents within 24 h, but not off rough bottom — [U. Michigan Deep Blue record](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/48b4e2f8-66c2-4716-b37e-18f7f3753371) [snippet of abstract; page 403; author not confirmed]
- Green Can Reef, Lake Michigan, WDNR diver surveys:
  - 1997: 0.50 egg masses per 1000 m²;
  - 2002, 2005–2007: more than 11 per 1000 m²;
  - 2017: 0.37 per 1000 m² (4 masses in 10,944 m²) — [WDNR yellow perch research](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/Fishing/lakemichigan/Yellowperch.html) [snippet]

**SURROGATE: Eurasian perch, *Perca fluviatilis***
- Chabařovice Lake, Czech Republic (2007, SCUBA): 7 substrates were used. The most important were dead *Potamogeton crispus*, *Artemisia* (wormwood) and common reed. **Live submerged vegetation was common but mostly avoided; dead submerged vegetation was strongly preferred.** The authors' explanation is that rigid 3-D structure keeps the eggs oxygenated around the clock. Ranked factors: depth > substrate > date > temperature — [Čech et al. 2009, Hydrobiologia 630:105–114, catalogue](https://asep.lib.cas.cz/arl-cav/en/detail-cav_un_epca-0328068-Distribution-of-egg-strands-of-perch-iPerca-fluviatilisi-L-with-respect-to-depth-and-spawnin/) [abstract via search]
- The same lake in later years:
  - In 2007–2009 only **6.0–28.9 %** of strands were on live submerged vegetation; dead reed and wormwood beds were the preferred substrate.
  - By 2010 those dead beds had degraded. **77.3 %** of the few strands outside the artificial substrate were then on live Eurasian milfoil and stonewort.
  - When 96 trees were sunk as artificial substrate, **two thirds** of all strands went onto them.
  - The authors judge live submerged vegetation "not a very suitable" substrate, probably because it depletes O₂ at night around the eggs.

  — [Čech et al. 2012](https://www.jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/jlimnol.2012.e18/468/935) [full text]
- Northern Baltic lagoons (18 bays, 2020–21 snorkelling, 1,453 strands):
  - strands were mostly on vegetation, far less on submerged branches or trunks, and on bare bottom in only a few lagoons;
  - they were mainly on **clean reed stems without epiphytes** (always the algae-free ones where both occurred) and on the charophyte *Chara tomentosa*;
  - they lay at 0.1–2 m, most at 0.5–0.7 m;
  - *Chara* cover alone explained 59 % of between-bay variation in strand counts (adding salinity raised R² to 0.69, adding reed cover to 0.73);
  - reed cover alone explained 43 %;
  - **total macrophyte cover explained < 1 %** (P = 0.95);
  - the authors call rigid erect structures "a precondition" for breeding success (citing Snickars et al. 2010) — [Westerbom et al. 2023, Front. Mar. Sci.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2023.1167038/full) [full text, first 100k characters]
- Snickars et al. 2010 (Baltic, MEPS 398:235–243): **substrate type was the most important predictor** of spawning sites. Rigid, structurally complex vegetation was favoured even where other substrates were common. Sheltered, shallow areas were preferred, and selection for temperature was stronger at the local scale — [Snickars et al. 2010](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v398/p235-243) [abstract via search; PDF 429]

**Survival on structure vs bare bed**
- **Yellow perch, hatchery experiment** (West Oakwood Lake stock, South Dakota; n = 4 per arm, 1.89-L buckets at 16.2–16.6 °C):
  - eyed eggs held on artificial vegetation hatched **98.6 % (SE 0.6)**;
  - eggs on the bare bucket bottom hatched **46.2 % (SE 23.7)** (P < 0.05);
  - mortality diverged from day 4, as hatching began;
  - DO did not differ (≥ 6 mg/L in both arms);
  - the authors attribute the loss to the strand folding on itself (suffocation), siltation and bacterial degradation of the egg membrane;
  - they cite earlier no-structure hatch rates of 37–55 % (Jansen et al. 2009) and a minimum of 35 % on floating screens (West & Leonard 1978) — [Ward & Barnes 2020](https://www.arcjournals.org/pdfs/ijisabf/v6-i2/1.pdf) [full text]
- **SURROGATE (Eurasian perch, field):**
  - strands at lake margins were significantly associated with submerged vegetation;
  - the proportion of dead embryos was **significantly higher in strands lying on the lake bottom** than in strands on submerged vegetation — [Smith, Douglas & Jurajda 2001, J. Fish Biol. 58:880–882](https://asep.lib.cas.cz/arl-cav/en/detail-cav_un_epca-0164163-Oviposition-site-selection-and-embryo-mortality-in-perch/) [abstract via search; percentages not obtained]
- Treasurer (1983, Env. Biol. Fish. 8:3–16) is cited for higher survival on vegetation through "increased ventilation and reduced siltation" — [Ward & Barnes 2020](https://www.arcjournals.org/pdfs/ijisabf/v6-i2/1.pdf) [secondary]

**Wood removal (Little Rock Lake, Wisconsin, whole-basin experiment)**
- Large coarse woody habitat (CWH) was cut from 475 to 128 logs per km of shoreline (a 73 % cut) in 2002. The reference basin had 344 per km. Littoral plants were only sparse burreed at 4.8 stems/m².
- In the treatment basin perch fell from 141/ha before the removal to as low as ~5/ha.
- YOY catch was 20 per 10 ha in the treatment basin vs 256 per 8 ha in the reference (up to 16× more there).
- The authors attribute the collapse jointly to more bass predation and loss of spawning substrate; the two causes are confounded.

  — [Sass et al. 2006, Fisheries 31(7):321–330](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Outreach_WholeLakeWoodRemovalSass.pdf) [full text]
- Contrast: across 13 Wisconsin and Michigan lakes with 3–1,520 wood pieces per km, YOY *largemouth bass* open-water mortality (instantaneous rate 0.04–0.19) was **not** related to CWH. Wood-as-refuge is not universal — [Ziegler et al. 2019, CJFAS](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10111626) [full text]

**Depth, water colour, waves and temperature**
- Two Pennsylvania lakes:
  - in the low-DOC lake ~**92 % of eggs were deeper than 3 m**;
  - in the higher-DOC lake **76 % were shallower than 1 m**;
  - surface UV killed **100 %** of eggs before hatch in the low-DOC lake, but had little effect in the higher-DOC lake or at depth;
  - warm surface water in the higher-DOC lake shortened hatching by 10–26 d;
  - the optimal spawning depth therefore depends on DOC — [Huff, Grad & Williamson 2004, TAFS 133:718–726](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1577/T03-048.1) [abstract via search]
- SURROGATE, Lake Constance: with artificial substrata at 0.5–15 m, the preferred depth was **2 m at the sheltered site vs 5 m at the ship-wave-exposed site**, with similar total ribbon numbers. Large females may choose deeper sites where waves are attenuated — [Probst et al. 2009, Ecol. Freshw. Fish 18:1–7](https://epic2-clone.awi.de/id/eprint/19428) [abstract via search]
- SURROGATE, Chabařovice Lake (wind-exposed, deforested shores):
  - perch avoided the 0–1 m layer around the whole shore, and the 1–3 m layer on the windward shore;
  - they preferred the calm south shore (50 vs 30 strands on artificial substrate; 34 vs 10 outside it);
  - 88–93.5 % of strands lay deeper than 3 m in 2007–2010;
  - mean strand depth followed the 10–12 °C layer (4.9 m early, 12.3 m late);
  - wind and waves damage strands mechanically, and the internal seiches they drive can thermally shock embryos (Aalto & Newsome 1989; Newsome & Aalto 1987);
  - **temperatures > 14 °C raise egg mortality** (Guma'a 1978) — [Čech et al. 2012](https://www.jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/jlimnol.2012.e18/468/935) [full text; secondary for Guma'a and Newsome & Aalto]
- Clady & Hutchinson (1975, TAFS 104:524–525) report high winds acting on yellow perch eggs in Oneida Lake, NY — citation confirmed by [Čech et al. 2012](https://www.jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/jlimnol.2012.e18/468/935) [title only; findings not obtained]

**Predation on ribbons**
- Lab tests (Purdue, Almeida et al.) with calico crayfish and round goby:
  - both predators preferred perch eggs stripped from the skein, and fathead minnow eggs, over eggs in the skein;
  - gobies spat skeins out;
  - crayfish rarely ate skein eggs, though their pulling damaged them;
  - the skein may contain noxious compounds (preliminary);
  - no consumption numbers were given — [The Fisheries Blog, 2017](https://thefisheriesblog.com/2017/02/06/predators-reject-yellow-perch-egg-skeins/) [full text; a blog summary of the study]

### Inferences
- **What substrate the sim should offer at perch spawning time.** It should be a *structure index*, not total plant biomass:
  - CWH;
  - standing dead stems of last year's emergents (pickerelweed and bulrush remnants);
  - rigid or overwintering submerged plants: *Nitella* as the charophyte analogue of *Chara*, and probably *Fontinalis* moss and large-leaf pondweed's overwintering shoots.

  Weight soft live submerged plants low (Čech: 6–29 % use when dead structure exists), and weight bare bed lowest. In the Baltic, total macrophyte cover explained < 1 % of strand variation while rigid *Chara* and reeds explained 43–73 %.
- **A usable survival multiplier.** Egg survival on bare or soft organic bed ≈ 0.45–0.5 × that on suspended structure. The 46.2/98.6 = 0.47 is a lab value; its direction is confirmed in the field for Eurasian perch (Smith 2001). Add a siltation or organic-bed penalty where the sim's `sedOM` is high.
- **When structure is scarce,** perch still spawn: the share placed on live plants rises (to ~77 % in Čech 2012), then on bare bed. So spawning should not fail outright; survival falls via the bare-bed multiplier. If the sim's plant beds collapse and there is little wood, perch recruitment should drop sharply, as in Little Rock Lake.
- **Depth in the Hot Brook lakes.** Tea-coloured (higher-DOC) water removes the UV constraint, so the Huff pattern predicts most ribbons < 1 m, in the warmest water. That should be offset by wave exposure: shift ribbons deeper (≈2 m vs 5 m in the Lake Constance sheltered-vs-exposed contrast) on long-fetch shores, and apply stranding or mechanical loss to ribbons in < 1 m during wind events. In a lake only 2.5–5.5 m deep, the whole 0.5–2.5 m littoral band is plausible.
- **Egg predation on perch ribbons should be low** relative to other eggs, because the skein deters crayfish and fish.

### Gaps
- No ribbon density per m² against stem density or plant biomass was found for any lake. The Baltic regressions use % cover and their coefficients are not in what I read.
- The numbers in Smith et al. 2001 (% dead on bottom vs on vegetation) and in Clady & Hutchinson 1975 (wind losses) were not obtained.
- The full survival comparison in the Iowa thesis is blocked (403).
- No study from Maine or another soft, brown-water New England lake on perch spawning substrate was found.

## 2. Chain pickerel: spawning over flooded and submerged vegetation; egg adhesion; survival; vegetation vs recruitment (northern pike as SURROGATE)

### Takeaway
Chain pickerel spawn right after ice-out:
- range 6–11 °C, peak 8.3–11.1 °C;
- in shallow, sluggish, densely vegetated water, to ~3 m but mostly very shallow;
- as broadcast spawners with no nest, scattering adhesive ~2–3 mm eggs on plants;
- hatch in 6–12 days.

I found no chain-pickerel-specific egg-survival study. The pike surrogate gives the mechanism:
- egg densities are highest in **dense, short mats of dead sedge, grass or wild-celery remains**, and lower among bulrush stems, lily-pad fragments and broken rushes;
- larvae hang on vegetation for about the first 10 days;
- egg-to-fingerling survival is ~0.15–2.7 %;
- the stock of small pike is capped per hectare of vegetated area.

### Cited Findings
**Chain pickerel (species-specific)**
- Maine: spawning occurs "shortly after ice-out when adults move in to swampy areas containing flooded vegetation" (MDIFW pickerel assessment). MDIFW says pickerel do well "as long as a lake contains relatively shallow, weedy habitat". These were read in earlier notes (`plant_species_traits.md`, `macrophytes.md` §6) — [MDIFW pickerel assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/pickerel.pdf) [read in earlier notes; 403 this session]
- Spawning runs at 2–22 °C with peak activity at 8.3–11.1 °C, over a 7–10-day window per population. Adults leave deeper wintering water for sluggish vegetated water up to 3 m deep. Eggs are released into submerged vegetation. Fertilized eggs are 2–3 mm. Fecundity is up to 50,000 but commonly 6,000–7,000. Eggs hatch in 6–12 days, and the yolk sac is absorbed 6–8 days later at ~10 mm. Young hide in mud or vegetation — [Animal Diversity Web, *Esox niger*](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Esox_niger/) [full text; secondary compilation]
- Ontario: spawning at 6–11 °C, April–May. The species is a nonguarding open-substratum *phytophil*, with fecundity 6,102–29,928 and maturity at 3–5 years. Adults favour shallow lakes and ponds (< 3 m) with abundant aquatic vegetation — [Ontario Freshwater Fishes Life History Database](https://ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=89) [full text; compilation]
- The eggs are numerous, small, demersal and adhesive, "scattered over vegetation or detritus". Usual spawning temperature is 8.3–11.1 °C — [Fishes of Texas](https://txstate.fishesoftexas.org/esox%20niger.htm) [snippet]
- Spawning is in heavy weed growth or flooded grasses, from a few inches to several feet deep — [NJ Scuba, Eastern Pickerel](https://njscuba.net/?p=994) [snippet]
- "Large number of adhesive eggs are scattered over vegetation" — [Florida FWC](https://myfwc.com/wildlifehabitats/profiles/freshwater/chain-pickerel/) [snippet]
- **Last year's dead vegetation:** the only explicit statement I found is an angler-forum post ("flooded marshland (last year's vegetation)"), which is unverified. Agency sources say only "flooded vegetation" — [BassResource forum](https://www.bassresource.com/bass-fishing-forums/topic/59702-spawning-pickerel/) [snippet; low quality]

**SURROGATE: northern pike, *Esox lucius***
- **Gilbert Lake, Wisconsin** (1968–69):
  - **egg densities were greatest where dense sedge mats and wild-celery (*Vallisneria*) remains were present**;
  - at similar depth and temperature, sites whose bottom held **bulrush stems, lily-pad fragments, broken rushes and debris had lower egg densities**;
  - spawning ran 25 March to early April, peaking at 10–17.8 °C surface temperature;
  - incubation took 13–16 days at 2.2–16.6 °C;
  - egg survival was poor that year, with no evidence that O₂ or temperature caused it;
  - the bulletin recommends protecting marshes with wild celery, grasses and sedges "that break down to form a dense mat";
  - it also relays Lake George, Minnesota (Franklin & Smith 1963): **highest egg densities in dense mats of sterile *Eleocharis* clumps, next in *Carex***;
  - and Fabricius & Gustafson (1958): eggs deposited **only over dense but relatively short vegetation such as *Carex***.

  — [Priegel & Krohn 1975, WDNR Tech. Bull. 86](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/VTGW7JVEZOPJJ8N/E/file-32a6a.pdf) [full text (OCR)]
- **Wisconsin managed spawning marshes** (1969–73):
  - spawning concentrated at the marsh edge **in dense mats of dead sedges and cattails**;
  - **survival from eggs deposited to fingerlings at draining was 0.15–2.69 %**;
  - production averaged 539 fingerlings/ha;
  - Franklin & Smith (1963) estimated egg survival at **77 %** from a live:dead egg ratio, or **18 %** from egg and fry densities per ft²;
  - other cited spawning substrates: marsh grass *Calamagrostis canadensis* (Carbine 1941, Houghton Lake MI); *Spartina* grasses, *Carex* and *Alisma* (Forney 1968); mowed hay (McCarraher & Thomas 1972).

  — [Fago 1977, WDNR Tech. Bull. 96](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/HS735DO56ZUQI9C/E/file-bdd62.pdf) [full text (OCR)]
- Pike spawn in shallow water with decomposing vegetation (Carbine & Applegate 1948; Miller 1948; Franklin & Smith 1963; Bregazzi & Kennedy 1980). Females scatter eggs in batches of fewer than 100 (5–60 per spawning act) on submerged vegetation over 2–5 days, often in < 18 cm of water. Eggs hatch in about 2 weeks.
  - **Contradiction:** the same review states that "hatching success is higher in eggs deposited on sand or silt, compared with those laid on aquatic plants" (Wright & Shoesmith 1988, UK gravel pits).

  — [UK Environment Agency pike literature review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/770796/Appendix_3c_Review_of_available_literature_-_pike.pdf) [full text]
- Wright & Shoesmith 1988 (two English gravel-pit lakes):
  - egg densities on sand or silt were 14–671 per m²;
  - survival from stage 1 to hatched fry was **3–6 %**, with egg losses of **9–10 % per day**;
  - silt deposition affected hatching (plant, settled-silt and disturbed-silt treatments were tested);
  - 0-group survival was higher in the densely vegetated 2-ha lake than in the 17-ha lake with little vegetation, where variable recruitment was blamed on lack of vegetation for spawning and cover — [Wright & Shoesmith 1988, J. Fish Biol. 33:623–636 (GWCT)](https://gwct.org.uk/research/scientific-publications/1980-89/1988/wright1988/) [abstract]
- Newly hatched pike attach vertically to plant stems, leaves or decaying vegetation by cement glands on the head, for about the first 10 days (Irish account). Suitable vegetation is dense, short, upright and shallow (Romanian study) — [Marine Institute Ireland record](https://oar.marine.ie/entities/publication/a19c0a90-fb63-4fe7-af0e-738ea84c38fa); [spasb.ro](https://spasb.ro/index.php/public_html/article/download/880/831/1606) [snippets]
- **Vegetation cap on pike stock** (four Dutch shallow waters, 1974–82):
  - the standing stock of pike < 54 cm is "tied to a maximum per unit of vegetated area": ~110 kg/ha of vegetated surface at site JV while the vegetated area grew from ~1 to 1.5 ha;
  - pike < 41 cm stayed inside vegetation;
  - 0+ biomass was strongly negatively correlated with larger pike (r = −0.87 to −0.96), consistent with cannibalism;
  - 45 % of native 0+ pike (12–22 cm) survived the 1978 growing season at site PS.

  — [Grimm 1984, FAO symposium document](https://www.fao.org/4/ae996b/AE996B02.htm) [full text]
- A summary of Grimm's work reports standing pike stocks of 50–75 kg/ha in lakes with 5–15 % emergent and 30–50 % submerged cover. It says the area of inundated emergent vegetation sets recruitment of 0–12 cm pike — FAO-hosted summary, page not pinned down ([fao.org U5736E](https://www.fao.org/4/U5736E/U5736E04.htm) or [fao.org AC858E](https://www.fao.org/4/ac858e/AC858E08.htm)) [snippet; verify]

### Inferences
- **Spawning-site rule for pickerel.** Choose cells < ~1 m (rarely to 3 m) at 6–11 °C in the first weeks after ice-out. Weight them by *dense, short, fine-stemmed mats*, ideally last year's dead sedge or grass flattened into a mat. Coarse emergent stems (bulrush) and lily-pad debris score lower.
- **Mapping to the 13 sim plants:**
  - pipewort and water lobelia (dense short isoetid turf) and bladderwort or moss mats are the nearest analogues of the "dense short mat";
  - dead pickerelweed and bulrush stems score medium;
  - fragrant water-lily, yellow pond-lily and watershield debris score low.
- **Missing habitat.** The lakes' real pickerel spawning habitat probably includes flooded shoreline sedge and grass at spring high water above the 0.25-m waterline. The sim has no field for it. A cheap proxy is a "flooded margin" bonus in the shallowest wet cells during the ice-out high-water weeks.
- **Survival mechanism.** Eggs (and the ~10 days when larvae hang on plants) laid onto anoxic soft bed or into silt should suffer extra loss. The UK gravel-pit result is the exception: hatching was higher on clean sand or silt there, but recruitment was still worse without vegetation. So model vegetation mainly through larval attachment and cover.
- **Overall magnitude.** Egg-to-fall-fingerling survival is ~0.2–3 % (managed marshes; 3–6 % to hatch in the UK). A Grimm-style carrying-capacity cap, of the form "pike or pickerel < X cm biomass ≤ K × vegetated area", gives a density-dependent ceiling tied to plant cover. The pike value is ~110 kg/ha of vegetated area; pickerel are smaller, so scale it down. This fits the sim's super-individual YOY schools.

### Gaps
- No chain pickerel egg or larval survival data by substrate were found.
- No primary source confirms that chain pickerel use last year's dead stems specifically (inferred from pike).
- No pickerel spawning depth or egg density values from a northeastern lake were found.
- I could not reach the MDIFW pickerel plan this session (403).
- I did not obtain Franklin & Smith 1963 (TAFS 92:91–110) or Casselman & Lewis 1996 (CJFAS 53 Suppl. 1) directly.

## 3. Banded killifish: eggs on plants

### Takeaway
Banded killifish are summer spawners: about 19–23 °C in Newfoundland, and ≥ ~21 °C in mainland reports. Territorial males spawn with females in shallow, densely vegetated water. Each adhesive egg (~2.0–2.2 mm) hangs on a thread attached to plants, with *Potamogeton* used most in the one study that recorded it. Fecundity is low, about 32–450 eggs per female per season. Their Newfoundland habitat plants overlap the Hot Brook plant list almost exactly. I found no survival-by-substrate data.

### Cited Findings
- Mainland populations spawn in spring. Eggs are "bound to aquatic plants by an adhesive thread" (Richardson 1939). Mainland spawning is reported in April–May at water temperatures of about 21 °C or higher (Carlander 1969; McAllister & Coad 1974) — [COSEWIC 2014, Banded Killifish (Newfoundland)](https://wildlife-species.canada.ca/species-risk-registry/virtual_sara/files/cosewic/sr_Banded%20Killifish_2014_e.pdf) [full text]
- Newfoundland (Indian Bay watershed) results, from the same report:
  - spawning runs late June to mid-August at **19–23 °C**;
  - ***Potamogeton* is the plant most often used as spawning substrate** (Chippett 2004);
  - fish are not active until water reaches 12 °C;
  - there is no parental care;
  - mean egg diameter is 2.2 mm with a maximum fecundity of 450 (Newfoundland), vs 2.0 mm (Cooper 1936) and a maximum of 250 (Carlander 1969) on the mainland;
  - fish mature at 1 year;
  - YOY reach 40–50 mm by October (49.5 mm mid-October in Lake Opinicon, Ontario; Keast & Eadie 1984) — [COSEWIC 2014](https://wildlife-species.canada.ca/species-risk-registry/virtual_sara/files/cosewic/sr_Banded%20Killifish_2014_e.pdf) [full text]
- **The commonest plants where the fish occur** (Indian Bay quadrats) were water lobelia (*Lobelia dortmanna*), quillwort (*Isoetes*) and pipewort (*Eriocaulon*). Others were purple bladderwort (*Utricularia purpurea*), **ribbon-leaf pondweed (*Potamogeton epihydrus*)** and *Myriophyllum tenellum*. The habitat is shallow, slow water with soft bottom and abundant vegetation. Patches are separated by deeper water, rocky substrate and little vegetation. The main threat named is sedimentation smothering vegetation — [COSEWIC 2014](https://wildlife-species.canada.ca/species-risk-registry/virtual_sara/files/cosewic/sr_Banded%20Killifish_2014_e.pdf) [full text]
- Females lay eggs with adhesive threads that stick to aquatic vegetation, and spawn among plants — [DFO Management Plan 2011](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/management-plans/banded-killifish-newfoundland-final-2011-part1.html) [snippet]
- Eggs are laid in adhesive strands within dense aquatic vegetation (New Hampshire) — [NH Fish & Game](https://www.wildlife.nh.gov/fishing-new-hampshire/fish-species-nh/banded-killifish) [snippet]
- Virginia: spawning April–September at 21–32 °C; eggs adhere to objects by filaments; fecundity is 32–252 eggs per female (Jenkins & Burkhead 1993) — [Virginia Tech eFish](https://efish.fishwild.vt.edu/families/bandkill.html) [full text; compilation]

### Inferences
- In Maine, spawning would fall around **late June–August**, when littoral water is ≥ 19–21 °C. The current year's submerged plants are then near peak, so the substrate is *live* plant biomass. The best match is ribbon-leaf pondweed, then the other pondweeds, bladderwort, and the isoetid turf of lobelia and pipewort, in < ~1 m.
- A sim rule: killifish spawning-site weight ∝ live submerged biomass of pondweeds + bladderwort + isoetids in wet cells < 1 m. Small clutches mean recruitment is limited by adult numbers and fry survival rather than by substrate, unless plants are absent.
- Siltation smothering plants is the documented threat, so a high-turbidity or sediment event should lower suitability.

### Gaps
- No egg-survival or hatching data by substrate were found.
- No incubation time was confirmed in what I read. It is widely given as ~11–12 days, but I did not verify that here.
- No clutch size per spawning act was found. Richardson 1939 (the behaviour description) was not obtained.

## 4. White perch: broadcast spawning substrate in lakes

### Takeaway
White perch broadcast adhesive eggs in shallow water over almost any firm bottom (sand, gravel, rock, sometimes sparse vegetation), in lake shallows or tributaries. In Maine this happens at about 15 °C (59–60 °F) in May–June, and eggs hatch in 4–5 days. Vegetation is not a requirement; no plant-dependence of survival was found.

### Cited Findings
- Maine: spawning is mainly in spring at ~59–60 °F (15 °C), in shallow water over almost any bottom type. Fecundity is 20,000–300,000 eggs depending on size. Eggs hatch in 4–5 days at 59–60 °F — [MDIFW white perch plan](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/strategic-management-plans/whiteperch.pdf) [snippet]
- Massachusetts: fish make short migrations into freshwater stream reaches and spawn on sand and gravel bars, where adhesive eggs are scattered on the sand and gravel. There is no nest and no care — [Mass.gov white perch](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/white-perch) [snippet]
- Michigan habitat summary: spawning is in shallow water over firm substrate — [Michigan DNR Special Report 39, Appendix 1](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/pdfs/IFR/ifrlibra/Special/Reports/sr39/sr39_App_1_71-80.pdf) [snippet]

### Inferences
- Treat white perch as **plant-independent** broadcast spawners over firm or sandy shallows (lake shoals, or the brook's riffle and run reaches) at ~11–16 °C. Egg survival should depend on bed type (firm vs flocculent organic) and siltation, not plant biomass.
- Brief incubation (4–5 d) means exposure to substrate hazards is short.

### Gaps
- No lake study gives white perch egg survival by substrate, or egg densities.
- The MDIFW plan was not readable this session (403), so its numbers are from the search snippet only.

## 5. Nest builders (pumpkinseed, redbreast, smallmouth bass, brown bullhead): plants, cover and nest success

### Takeaway
All four need a cleared patch of firm bed (sand or gravel; mud for bullhead). They do not spawn *on* plants:
- Redbreast in New Brunswick lakes **dig through dense plant mats** to coarse sand or fine gravel. They often (85 % in one lake) nest within 1 m of a rock or log.
- Smallmouth nest at 0.5–3 m on coarse substrate near wood or rock cover. No macrophyte relation is established.
- Pumpkinseed prefer cleared areas over cluttered (woody-debris) ones until nest space runs out. Nests in clutter are smaller and closer to cover.
- Brown bullhead nest in mud or sand near or under logs, roots, rocks or vegetation.

Evidence linking nest *success* to nearby cover is thin and mixed.

### Cited Findings
**Redbreast sunfish (Yoho and Oromocto lakes, New Brunswick)**
- Spawning was late June to early July near 20 °C (literature range 16.7–27.8 °C).
- Nests averaged **50.9 ± 10.1 cm** across and 7.1 ± 2.5 cm deep, in **43.2 ± 8.4 cm** of water (n = 43 + 20); the literature gives nests of 61–102 cm in 15–40 cm of water.
- **Most nests were dug through a dense vegetative mat** down to coarse sand or fine gravel.
- In Yoho Lake, 72 % of nests had sparse vegetation and no cover structure.
- In Oromocto Lake, **85 % were within 1 m of a rock or log**.
- 58 % of nests were more than 5 m from another nest (not colonial).
- Males guard and fan the nest; larvae stay in it 1–2 weeks.

— [COSEWIC redbreast sunfish, ch. 6 (Gautreau & Curry 2006 and others)](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/redbreast-sunfish/chapter-6.html) [full text]

**Smallmouth bass**
- In two northern Wisconsin lakes (Big Crooked and Sanford), nests were at **0.5–3.0 m**, "generally near wood or rock cover", on substrate with ≥ 40 % of particles 6.4–149 mm. Model variables were sand, gravel, cobble, embeddedness, rock cover and wood cover; vegetation is not mentioned — [Bozek et al. 2002, AFS Symp. 31](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/14064) [abstract]
- Same lakes:
  - egg-to-fry survival differed between the lakes;
  - in Big Crooked (coarser bed), **greater spacing between nests** raised survival;
  - in Sanford (less coarse), survival rose with **cobble, cover diameter, nest depth and the guarding male's length** — [Short 2001, UW-Stevens Point MS thesis](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/80807) [abstract]
- Lake Opeongo, Ontario: nest density was related to littoral temperature and shoreline reticulation (sheltered, convoluted shores). Vegetation was not among the variables reported — [Rejwan et al. 1999](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%201990s/Rejwan%20et%20al%201999.pdf) [snippet]
- North-temperate lakes: YOY smallmouth used woody structure just after leaving the nest, but moved away from complex wood by August, which the authors link to predation risk — [Brown & Bozek 2010, USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70169302) [snippet]

**Pumpkinseed (Lake Opinicon, Ontario)**
- Males nested more in areas **cleared of woody debris** than in cluttered areas at the start and end of the season. During peak nesting there was no difference, because of density-dependent limits on nest space. Nests in clutter were **smaller and closer to cover**. Substrate type affected nest density. Vegetation was not reported — [Colgan & Ealey 1973, JFRBC 30:853–856 (QUBS summary)](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/role-of-woody-debris-in-nest-site-selection-by-pumpkinseed-sunfish-lepomis-gibbosus) [abstract-level]
- Nests are in shallow water, from 6 inches to several feet, on sand or gravel — [WDNR pumpkinseed sheet](https://dnr.Wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Species_pumpkinseed.pdf) [snippet]

**Brown bullhead**
- Nests are shallow saucers excavated in mud or sand near logs, rocks or vegetation, or among the roots of aquatic plants near stumps or downed trees, in water from 6 inches to a few feet. Both parents guard, fan and mouth the eggs, and herd the larval "ball" — [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Ameiurus_nebulosus); [Missouri MDC field guide](https://mdc12.mdc.mo.gov/applications/mofwis/Home/Details?Id=0100015); [Michigan DNR](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/bullheads) [snippets]
- Michigan (Blumer 1985, Am. Midl. Nat. 114:318–330, via ADW): eggs hatched on average at day 5.6, and the larval stage lasted 4.4 days. Spawning was concentrated in the first 16 days of June — [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Ameiurus_nebulosus) [secondary]

**Context (not Hot Brook species).** In Minnesota lakes, black crappie nested in emergent hardstem bulrush; largemouth bass depended less on it and more on undeveloped shoreline — [UWSP restoration resource PDF](https://www3.uwsp.edu:443/cnr-ap/UWEXLakes/PublishingImages/resources/restoration-project/Relationships%20Between%20Shoreline%20Development%20and%20Nest%20Site%20Selection%20by%20Black%20Crappie%20and%20Largemouth%20Bass.pdf) [snippet]

### Inferences
- **Nest suitability for the sim:**

  `suit = f(bed: coarse sand/gravel high; organic muck low, except bullhead) × g(cover within ~1 m: wood or rock bonus) × h(plant density)`

  where h is near 1 at sparse-to-moderate cover and declines for dense mats. Redbreast and pumpkinseed can clear mats, so for them use a mild penalty, i.e. an excavation cost, not exclusion. Smallmouth: coarse bed at 0.5–3 m, no plant term. Bullhead: tolerates mud, wants overhead or side cover (logs, roots, plants).
- **Nest success.** Male size and nest spacing appear in smallmouth survival models. Cover diameter helped in one lake only. A cover term on nest success should be weak or optional; male guarding is the main mechanism already in nest-guarder models.
- **Plants matter more after hatch.** Sunfish and bass fry leave the nest into nearby cover (wood, plants), so plant and wood fields enter through YOY refuge rather than egg survival.

### Gaps
- No quantitative nest-success vs macrophyte-density relation was found for any of the four species.
- Pumpkinseed nest-site numbers from Lake Opinicon were not obtained.
- Blumer 1985's cover and depth details for bullhead were not obtained.
- No Maine data.

## 7. Quantitative relations usable as mechanisms

### Takeaway
No source gives egg survival as a continuous function of stem density or plant biomass. What exists:
- step contrasts: structure vs bare bed at ~0.47×, dead or rigid vs live soft plants;
- choice shares when the substrate is scarce or plentiful;
- depth shifts with wave exposure and water colour;
- egg-to-fingerling survival ranges;
- a vegetation-area carrying capacity for pike.

### Inferences
Proposed mechanisms, built from the findings above:

1. **Spawning-substrate index per bed cell, evaluated at each species' spawning date** (not peak biomass):

   `S = a·CWH + b·deadStanding + c·rigidLive + d·softLive`

   - For perch, use b ≈ c > d, with a high. Čech's 6–29 % use of live soft plants when dead structure exists implies a selection ratio on live soft plants of about 0.1–0.3 relative to dead structure.
   - For pickerel, use *dense short mats* (isoetid turf, flattened dead sedge, bladderwort or moss) above coarse stems.
   - For killifish, use live pondweeds, bladderwort and isoetids in summer.

   This needs a "standing dead" or overwinter pool per plant species. The plant-traits note gives overwinter fractions, e.g. large-leaf pondweed 0.3 (`plant_species_traits.md`).
2. **Site choice:** probability ∝ S^k (k ≈ 1–2) within the species' depth and temperature window. When S is low everywhere, spawning still happens; it falls back to live plants and then bare bed (Čech 2012). There should be no hard failure.
3. **Egg survival multiplier by placement:**
   - on structure 1.0;
   - on bare or organic bed ≈ 0.47 (perch, Ward & Barnes);
   - extra loss in < 1 m on wave-exposed cells during wind events (perch: Čech, Probst; Clady & Hutchinson by title);
   - extra loss when egg-stage temperature exceeds ~14 °C (perch).
4. **Esocid larval stage:** the first ~10 days need vegetation to hang on. Larvae hatched in cells with low S suffer extra loss, through sinking to anoxic bed or predation (inferred mechanism).
5. **Carrying capacity:** cap pickerel YOY plus juvenile biomass by K × the vegetated area of the lake (pike ~110 kg/ha vegetated for < 54 cm; scale down for pickerel), with cannibalism as the regulating process (Grimm).
6. **Nest builders:** substrate and cover suitability with a weak plant penalty (§5). Plants act on their fry via cover, not on eggs.

### Gaps
- No continuous dose–response (egg survival vs stem density or g DW/m²) exists in what I found. The parameters above are step values and shares, to be tuned against the sim's young-of-year fate audit.
- No Hot Brook or Maine field data on where perch ribbons or pickerel eggs actually lie. A spring snorkel survey (ice-out to mid-May) of the two lakes would settle this. Per the field observations in memory, the human fishes these lakes.
