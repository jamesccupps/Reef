# Aquatic plant species of soft, tea-coloured, shallow Maine lakes: traits and ecological roles, per species (for replacing the 3-D sim's five growth forms)

Compiled 2026-10-16 for the Hot Brook 3-D sim (`src/w3/plants3.js`, `FORMS3`: EM, FL, SC, IS, DM). The goal is species-level parameters for plants as "places for the fish to be, food and things for the water, places for insects and other things to be on top of" (the lake owner).

**Builds on, does not repeat.** Form-level light, depth limits, biomass, growth, nutrients, litter and effects are in `macrophytes.md` ("the macrophytes note"). Per-gram epiphytic fauna, snails, emergence and crayfish are in `littoral_invertebrates.md` ("the invertebrates note"). Muskrat, beaver and loons are in `Maine lake shores and catchment/shore_birds_and_mammals.md`; turtles and frogs in `turtles_and_frogs.md`; MNAP shore communities in `shore_geometry_and_vegetation.md`; fish spawning seasons in `Maine lakes ponds and streams/maine_fish_community_hot_brook.md` and `habitat3d_new_species.md`. Numbers re-used from those notes are cited to their original source and marked "(… note)".

**Tags.** [read] seen in the source text or its abstract; [snippet] only in a search summary; [2°] in a review citing the original; (inf) my arithmetic or judgement. Region flags: [ME]/[NE] New England, [NA-N] northern North America, [EU] Europe, [S] southern US (warm water; use with care).

**Bottom line.**
1. The soft-water species split cleanly by **carbon source and rooting**: isoetids (*Lobelia*, *Isoetes*, *Eriocaulon*, *Littorella*) draw CO₂ from the sediment and push >97% of their O₂ into it; floating-leaved plants take air CO₂ and vent air to their rhizomes; Nitella and mosses need free CO₂; bicarbonate users (*Elodea*, many pondweeds) are at a disadvantage in water this soft.
2. **Watershield is a clonal annual** in a perennial disguise: every ramet lives one season, the clone carries over on winter buds that stay attached, and free buds almost never found new plants (Adams 1969, a New Hampshire pond).
3. **Lily pads are a food web of their own**: *Donacia* reed beetles (larvae breathe from the roots), *Galerucella* leaf beetles, *Bagous* weevils, pyralid moth caterpillars, leaf-mining midges, aphids. In soft, acid water their area loss is small and leaves live 5–7 weeks; under heavy *Galerucella* a *Nuphar* leaf lasts 17 days.
4. **Moose** remove *Nuphar* and pondweeds from shallow, mineral-bottomed sites they favour (13–27% of an Isle Royale moose's summer diet is aquatic). Beaver and muskrat eat lily rhizomes; ducks eat pondweed, watershield and pickerelweed seeds.
5. **Per gram**, dissected and broad-leaved submerged plants carry the most invertebrates and epiphyton; ribbon leaves (*Vallisneria*) and floating leaves the least; isoetid, *Nitella*, moss and bladderwort fauna are unmeasured in North America.

## 1. Growth: depth, light, carbon, rates, phenology, overwintering, biomass, height, substrate and waves, per species

### Takeaway
The candidate list holds three physiologies that matter more than leaf shape: sediment-CO₂ isoetid rosettes (slow, evergreen, ~0–2 m on sand and gravel, killed by organic mud), floating-leaved rhizome plants (depth window set by petiole reach, ~0.3–2.5 m on muck, leaves in full light, need shelter from waves) and submerged canopy and meadow plants (light-limited depth, 1–6 m). Within each, species differ chiefly in overwintering (evergreen shoots, attached winter buds, turions, rhizomes, tubers) and in start date: *Lobelia*, *Isoetes*, *Elodea*, *P. robbinsii*, *Nitella* and mosses are green at ice-out; *Brasenia* starts on Apr 24–25; *Vallisneria* in mid-June.

### Cited Findings
**Soft-water community and species frequencies [ME/NE]**
- New England pondweed medians (mg HCO₃⁻/L; Hellquist 1980, 321 sites): *P. spirillus* 13.4, *P. epihydrus* 13.4, *P. natans* 20.7, *P. robbinsii* 25.6, *P. amplifolius* 28.1, against 93 for curly-leaf and 113 for sago. Hot Brook's drainage analogue sits at ~6.7 (macrophytes note) — [Hellquist 1980, Rhodora 82:331](https://archive.org/download/biostor-138628/biostor-138628_djvu.txt) [read]
- Highland Lake [ME] frequencies: *P. epihydrus* 81%, *Eriocaulon* 77%, *Nymphoides* 47%, *Brasenia* 31%, *Utricularia intermedia* 26%, *Lobelia* 24%, *Nuphar* 20%, *Nymphaea* 13%, *Isoetes* 13% (macrophytes note) — [MNAP aquatic ch. 6](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_6_6.1to6.4.pdf)
- MNAP's open-water marsh: white or yellow water-lily or (rarer) watershield dominate; floating-leaved pondweed variants (bigleaf pondweed); bladderworts and pipewort common underneath. Pickerelweed beds: pickerelweed, yellow water-lily and bladderworts almost always present, water bulrush, white water-lily and pipewort characteristic (shore geometry note) — [MNAP Open-water Marsh](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/openwatermarsh.htm)

**Carbon source**
- *Lobelia dortmanna* takes "the majority" of its CO₂ from the sediment; >97% of its photosynthetic O₂ leaves through the roots (split chambers); root O₂ permeability per area is 2–3× the leaf's — [Lobelia vs *Zostera* O₂ dynamics, PMC4247029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4247029) [read]. *Lobelia* takes 46–98% of its CO₂ from the sediment (macrophytes note) — [Winkel & Borum 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2707917/) [read]
- Sediment CO₂ supplies most of the inorganic carbon isoetids fix; only at very high water CO₂ do the leaves take up much. Small elodeids with a high root:shoot area also use sediment CO₂ in low-alkalinity lakes — [Winkel & Borum 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2707917/) [snippet]
- *Isoetes* runs CAM: in *I. howellii* night CO₂ uptake was one-third to one-half of daily gross carbon gain, and emergent leaf tips lost CAM while submerged bases kept it — [Keeley & Busch 1984 (USGS record)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1007700) [read abstract]. CAM in aquatics is a carbon-conserving supplement; daytime uptake is not inhibited — [CAM in freshwater plants review](https://exa.unne.edu.ar/biologia/fisiologia.vegetal/CAMfreshwaterplants.pdf) [snippet]
- *Elodea canadensis* uses bicarbonate via a polar leaf pH shift (Elzenga & Prins 1988) — [Funct. Plant Biol. PP9880727](https://www.publish.csiro.au/fp/PP9880727) [snippet]. Bicarbonate use is a continuum, not two classes — [St Andrews repository 10023/14420](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/14420) [snippet]. Its frequency in a flora rises with water HCO₃⁻ — [Iversen et al. 2019, Science](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/catchment-properties-and-the-photosynthetic-trait-composition-of-/) [snippet]
- *Utricularia purpurea* tested as a non-user, but only below 0.07 meq/L alkalinity — [Faroese lakes study, Fróðskaparrit 1999](https://timarit.is/page/931509) [snippet]
- *Fontinalis antipyretica* "requires water below pH 8.4 where dissolved carbon dioxide is available"; prefers slightly acidic, shaded sites — [Washington Ecology, *Fontinalis*](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/fonant.html) [read]

**Depth and light, by species**
- *Lobelia*: median Zc 0.8 m (0.1–2.2) in 25 Polish soft-water lakes, Zc ≈ SD/3, at ~43% of subsurface light (macrophytes note) — [Borowiak et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1515/limre-2017-0016) [read]. It grows "on hard, firm sandy or gravely sediments in shallow to moderately deep slightly acidic still waters" and flowers in water to 2 m — [Washington Ecology, *Lobelia*](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/lobdor.html) [read]
- *Eriocaulon aquaticum* [NA-N, Wisconsin]: low-conductivity, low-alkalinity lakes; forms dense turfs; scapes to 40 cm; prefers hard substrate; not turbidity tolerant; found to 2.6 m; companions *Brasenia*, *Nymphaea* — [UW–Madison digital collection, Wisconsin lake plants](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/ABZNFJCOGKGIVV8D/text/AYHDUJYWQNIXVU8G) [snippet]. [EU] An indicator of clear, nutrient-poor lakes on acid stony bottoms, with *Lobelia* — [Biodiversity Ireland](https://biodiversityireland.ie/?p=24513) [snippet]
- *Isoetes* [EU, Pyrenees]: a shallow population (0.3–1.3 m) and a deep one (1.8–2.3 m); density falls with depth while biomass, plant size and fertility rise, attributed to winter ice scour and less organic matter in the shallows — [Isoetes Page, node 145](https://isoetes.myspecies.info/node/145) [snippet]. *I. lacustris* forms extensive turf in deeper water; *I. echinospora* is shallower and patchier; North American "*I. lacustris*" is now *I. macrospora* — [BSBI plant crib, *Isoetes*](https://bsbi.org/learn/resources/plant-crib/isoetes) [snippet]. Nova Scotia: *I. lacustris* on stony bottoms, often in deep water of oligotrophic lakes — [Proc. N.S. Inst. Sci. flora](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/NSM/article/view/5325/4810) [snippet]
- *Brasenia*: "clear, soft water to depths of 10 feet" (3 m) — [MN DNR watershield](https://dnr.state.mn.us/aquatic_plants/floatingleaf_plants/watershield.html) [read]; 0.5–3 m — [Washington Ecology, *Brasenia*](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/brasch.html) [read]. Winter-bud germination failed beyond a compensation depth of 6–7 ft (1.8–2.1 m) in a New Hampshire pond — [Adams 1969, Rhodora 71 (Wheelwright Pond, Lee NH)](https://archive.org/download/biostor-180721/biostor-180721_djvu.txt) [read]
- *P. amplifolius*: "will grow in clear water as deep as 6 m" — [Washington Ecology, *P. amplifolius*](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/potamp.html) [read]; reaches the surface from 0–3 m but only 1–3 m tall at 3–5 m in Lake George (macrophytes note) — [Lake George memos](https://dspace.rpi.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e742a406-372f-4003-b2da-24e78f70a891/content) [read]
- *P. robbinsii*: large colonies in deep water, maximum densities ~1,000 plants/m² at 5–7 m — [Go Botany, *P. robbinsii*](https://gobotany.nativeplanttrust.org/species/potamogeton/robbinsii/) [read]; "shallow to deep water, usually with low alkalinity" — [Washington Ecology, *P. robbinsii*](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/potrob.html) [read]
- Lake George shoot compensation / K½ at 20 °C (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹): *Vallisneria* 10/26, *Elodea* 12/26, *P. amplifolius* 12/31, *P. robbinsii* 20/38 (macrophytes note) — [Madsen et al., Lake George](https://dspace.rpi.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a7a8bb06-0c45-44b0-94c1-2eb34b701a2e/content) [read]
- *Nitella flexilis* grows at 2.2% of surface light (macrophytes note) — [1981, Int. Rev. Hydrobiol.](https://doi.org/10.1002/iroh.19810660506) [read]
- *Vallisneria* winter-bud output is light-limited: over 1%-light depths of 111.2 down to 23.8 cm, total winter-bud biomass produced ranged from 0.63 to 0.01 g, bud numbers from 3.5 to 0.1 and single-bud mass from 0.18 to 0.04 g (Upper Mississippi; the pairing of the ends is my reading of the snippet: less light, fewer and smaller buds, as the authors conclude) — [Korschgen et al., USGS UMESC](https://www.umesc.usgs.gov/documents/reports/1998/98r005.txt) [snippet]
- Coloured water: isoetid population area shrank sharply with humic matter (25 Polish soft-water lakes); settlement index 85.4 → < 0.001 g DW/m² for *Isoetes*, 8.4 → 6.2 for *Lobelia* (invertebrates note) — [Acta Soc. **Growth rates**
- *Lobelia*: maximum specific growth ~4× slower than *Zostera marina*; leaves and roots live more than a year; maximum biomass ~90 g DW/m²; light-saturated photosynthesis 1–2 mg O₂ g⁻¹ DW h⁻¹ — [PMC4247029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4247029) [read]. It makes and loses leaves all year (macrophytes note) — [Farmer 1987](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.1987.tb00144.x) [read]
- Isoetids produce 5–425 g m⁻² yr⁻¹; *I. echinospora* leaves turn over 1.5–1.6×/yr; 30–50% of production below ground (macrophytes note) — [Chappuis et al. 2016](https://limnetica.com/documentos/limnetica/limnetica-35-2-p-373.pdf) [read]
- *Elodea*: growth Q10 2.3–3.5 over 5–15 °C, "nearly arrested" at 5 °C (macrophytes note) — [Madsen & Brix 1997](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s004420050165) [snippet]
- *Utricularia*: aquatic carnivorous plants grow apically fast, 1–3 leaf whorls/day; *U. vulgaris* 1.4–2.8 whorls/day over a season — [Adamec, apical growth](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL11_Apices.pdf) [snippet]
- *P. amplifolius*: "plants over 3 m tall observed in early May" (Washington, a milder climate) — [Washington Ecology](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/potamp.html) [read]
- The per-species RGR table of Nielsen & Sand-Jensen 1991 (Aquat. Bot. 39:109) was not readable; only its record was found — [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/da/publications/variation-in-growth-rates-of-submerged-rooted-macrophytes)

**Phenology and overwintering**
- *Brasenia* [NE, New Hampshire, 1966] — [Adams 1969](https://archive.org/download/biostor-180721/biostor-180721_djvu.txt) [read]:
  - Spring growth (a meristem elongating one inch) began in 9 of 10 tagged plants on **Apr 24–25**.
  - The first overwintering winter buds were collected **Jul 30**; development was followed Jul 23–Sep 28. Buds reach ~20 cm with up to ten segments; early plants carry 1–3 buds.
  - Fate: ~60% of buds were freed before Oct 1, floated, sank and died; ~40% stayed attached, of which most (≈35% of all buds) overwintered and made next year's plants; < 0.25% of production had any chance as a new independent plant. Of thousands of free buds that germinated in 1966, eight made mature plants. Frozen buds never germinated.
  - "No individual plant lasts more than one season"; no rhizome segment more than two. Never seen established in swift water; bottom muck at both sites.
  - Wind piled free buds into the shallows, where they dried out as water fell.
- *Brasenia* propagates by "rhizomes, seeds, winter buds"; *P. robbinsii* by "seeds, rhizomes, winter buds"; *P. amplifolius* by "seeds, fragments, rhizomes" — [Washington Ecology pages](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/brasch.html) [read]; contradicted for *P. robbinsii* by Go Botany ("no turions"; perennial, lives more than two years; rarely flowers) — [Go Botany](https://gobotany.nativeplanttrust.org/species/potamogeton/robbinsii/) [read]
- Lake George: *P. amplifolius* grows right after ice-out (mid-April); *Vallisneria* starts mid-June and dies back in September; *P. amplifolius* deeper than 3 m and *P. robbinsii* stay productive under ice, *Vallisneria* does not; ten of 26 species held density and photosynthesis (10–20% of summer) through winter (macrophytes note) — [Boylen & Sheldon 1976](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.194.4267.841); [Lake George memos](https://dspace.rpi.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e742a406-372f-4003-b2da-24e78f70a891/content) [read]
- *Vallisneria* [NA-N]: leaves dominate the biomass from mid-June through August; the plant then senesces to winter buds alone, which held **11% of total dry weight** at season's end — [Titus & Stephens-type seasonal study, doi 10.1007/BF00378213](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/BF00378213) [snippet]
- *Elodea* [EU, three Swedish lakes]: grew through autumn, died back in winter, and regrew early in spring while the water was still under 5 °C — often the only species active soon after ice melt; fragments started in autumn converged in form with summer ones by the next summer — [Tattersdill thesis, UiT](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/12357) [snippet]. Other accounts have it green and slowly growing under ice — [Lake Champlain Committee note](https://www.lakechamplaincommittee.org/learn/news/january-2026-e-news/nature-note-common-waterweed-uncommon-adaptations) [snippet]
- *Utricularia* turions: dormancy ends around late October; 9–70% starch (macrophytes note) — [Adamec 2011](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL11Turions.pdf) [read]. *U. vulgaris* and *U. australis* overwinter as turions — [Adamec apical growth](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL11_Apices.pdf) [snippet]
- *Nuphar* leaves [EU]: live 38–48 d on average; floating-leaf P/Bmax 1.35–2.25 yr⁻¹; leaves produced during 53–73% of a 135–199-day season — [Initial decomposition of floating leaf blades of waterlilies, PMC6590448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590448/) [read]. Leaves appear Apr 28–Jun 7, limited by temperature (macrophytes note) — [Klok & van der Velde 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5410161/) [read]
- Flowering [NA-N, Nova Scotia]: *Nuphar* subsp. *variegata* July–August; *Nymphaea odorata* June–September — [Proc. N.S. Inst. Sci., flora of Nova Scotia, Nymphaeaceae](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/NSM/article/view/5340/4819) [read]. *Brasenia* flowers in June (MN) — [MN DNR](https://dnr.state.mn.us/aquatic_plants/floatingleaf_plants/watershield.html) [read]
- *Nymphoides cordata*: present in all six New England states; lakes, ponds, streams; perennial — [Go Botany](https://gobotany.nativeplanttrust.org/species/nymphoides/cordata/) [read]; "long-lived perennial that goes dormant in winter" — [Florida Native Plant Society](https://www.fnps.org/plant/nymphoides-cordata) [snippet]

**Biomass, density, height**

- *Eriocaulon* rosette leaves ~1–10 cm; much longer when deep — [Wikipedia, *E. aquaticum*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eriocaulon_aquaticum) [snippet; low-quality source]

**Substrate and waves**
- *Lobelia* dies or loses anchorage (roots shortened from 10 to 2 cm) at only ~2.4% degradable organic matter in the sediment; iron plaques on roots in enriched sediment reduce O₂ loss and protect root tips — [Møller & Sand-Jensen, Plant Signal. Behav.](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.4161/psb.3.10.6500) [snippet]; [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/iron-plaques-improve-the-oxygen-supply-to-root-meristems-of-the-f) [snippet]
- Higher alkalinity plus sulfate stimulates sediment mineralisation and anoxia; together they raised *Lobelia* mortality and cut biomass; photosynthetic efficiency correlated with sediment redox — [Aquatic Ecology 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10452-012-9399-7) [snippet]
- Floating leaves and waves [EU]: mechanical (wind and wave) damage hit 60–80% of lily leaves at exposed sites (*N. lutea* 90% in spring); none at a sheltered acid pool — [PMC6590448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590448/) [read]. *Galerucella* damage was lower at the wind- and wave-exposed site of a Polish lake — [Polish J. Nat. Sci.](https://czasopisma.uwm.edu.pl/index.php/pjns/article/view/7489) [snippet]
- *P. amplifolius* grows "in soft sediments" and is easily damaged by boats — [Lake Restoration, large-leaf pondweed](https://www.lakerestoration.com/aquatic_plant_id/large-leaf-pondweed/) [snippet; commercial]
- Bulrush grows on silt, clay, sand, gravel, marl and peat, and "buffers wind and wave action" — [FEIS](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/graminoid/schacu/all.html) [read]

### Inferences
- **Map to the sim's forms** (inf):
  - IS: *Lobelia*, *Eriocaulon*, *Isoetes* (and *Eleocharis acicularis* turf, rosette *Sagittaria graminea*, rare *Littorella americana*).
  - DM: *Nitella*, *Fontinalis* and other mosses.
  - SC: *P. amplifolius*, *P. robbinsii*, *P. spirillus*, *Utricularia* (rootless), *Elodea*, *Vallisneria* (circumneutral; likely sparse here), *Najas flexilis*, *Schoenoplectus subterminalis*.
  - FL: *Nuphar variegata*, *Nymphaea odorata*, *Brasenia*, *P. epihydrus*, *P. natans*, *Nymphoides cordata*, floating-leaved bur-reeds.
  - EM: *Pontederia*, emergent bulrushes and spikerushes, *Sagittaria latifolia*; *Typha* only in marsh coves.
- **Calendar for Hot Brook** (ice-out ~Apr 23; inf): evergreens (IS, DM, *P. robbinsii*, deep *P. amplifolius*, *Elodea*) photosynthesise at ice-out; *Brasenia* sprouts at ice-out (NH Apr 24–25) and reaches the surface in late May–June; lilies leaf out late April–early June; *Vallisneria* from mid-June; winter-bud and turion formation from late July (*Brasenia*) to September; lily and pickerelweed leaves die back Sep–Oct.
- **Winter carry-over by species** (inf from the above): isoetids and mosses ~0.9; *P. robbinsii* and *Elodea* 0.5–0.8 (green shoots, partly lost); lilies 0.7–0.8 (rhizome mass ~75%); *Brasenia* ~0.3–0.4 (attached buds only; ramets die); *Vallisneria* ~0.1 (buds 11% of DW); *Utricularia* ~0.1–0.2 (turions).
- **Brasenia spread is local** (inf from Adams): new beds come from attached buds next to old ones; wind-driven free buds strand and die, so a lake's watershield pattern should be persistent, cove-bound and depth-limited at ~2 m.
- **Isoetid mud threshold** (inf): give IS a hard penalty where the bed's organic content (`sedOM`) passes ~2.4% labile matter, and a soft one with rising alkalinity or sulfate. This also makes isoetids a tracer of the shore's sand and gravel.

## 2. Structure as habitat: architecture, invertebrates and periphyton per plant, fish cover, spawning and nurseries

### Takeaway
Per gram of plant, invertebrates and epiphyton follow surface area and leaf form: dissected and whorled plants carry the most, broad-leaved pondweeds next, ribbon leaves and floating leaves the least, with snails favouring broad leaves. Per m² of bed, biomass matters more than form. For fish, the soft-water species give three kinds of structure: lily and watershield canopies (overhead shade for bass, pickerel and panfish), tall pondweeds such as *P. amplifolius* ("bass weed", "musky weed"), and low meadows (isoetids, *Nitella*, moss) with little cover. Spawning uses the plants mainly as substrate (perch ribbons, pickerel on flooded vegetation, golden shiner and killifish eggs), and sunfish nests are dug through plants to sand.

### Cited Findings
**Architecture and surface area**
- Surface area per g DW [EU, Lake Geneva]: *Elodea canadensis* 1,255 cm² (highest measured), *P. perfoliatus* 762, *P. lucens* 653, *P. pectinatus* 500 (whole plants; Sher-Kaul et al. 1995) — [Univ. Geneva archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:72904) [snippet]

- Plant architecture (number, shape and arrangement of stems, branches and leaves) was the frame of Cheruvelil et al. 2002 (J. N. Am. Benthol. ### Inferences
- **Invertebrate factor per species** (individuals per g DW relative to a broad-leaved pondweed = 1; inf from the ranks above and the invertebrates note's form factors): *Utricularia* and native milfoils 1.2–1.5 (dissected; but *Utricularia* also eats some); *P. amplifolius*, *P. natans* submerged parts, *Elodea* 1.0; *P. robbinsii* 0.8 (stiff leaves, but snails like it); *Vallisneria*, *Sparganium*, *S. subterminalis*, *Eriocaulon* (ribbon/rosette) 0.5–0.6; *Nitella* and moss 1.0 (uncertain: *Chara* meadows carry 78–184/g); lily and watershield blades 0.3–0.4 (most fauna on petioles and leaf undersides); emergents 0.25.
- **Epiphyton factor** (inf from Lalonde & Downing): broad and whorled 1.0, ribbon 0.6–0.7, floating-leaf undersides 0.5; in an oligotrophic-to-mesotrophic coloured lake, let epiphyton per area rise slightly with depth (less grazing and UV, inf), but it shades the host in proportion.
- **Fish cover classes** (inf): (a) overhead canopy — lilies, watershield, floating pondweeds: shade, ambush edge for pickerel and bass, weak zooplankton refuge; (b) tall stems — *P. amplifolius*, *P. robbinsii* beds, bulrush: stem cover by the existing S50 rule; (c) low turf — isoetids, *Nitella*, moss, *Eleocharis acicularis*: little cover, foraging ground for sunfish and perch (snails, chironomids), bass and sunfish nest sites on the sand next to it.
- **Spawning hooks**: pickerel and perch read last year's dead emergent and floating-leaved stems (EM/FL storage or litter in the cell) at ice-out; shiners and killifish read live SC biomass in June–August; sunfish and bass nests read sand or gravel cells next to plants (IS cells qualify).

## 3. Food: who eats which plant, and the lily-pad food web

### Takeaway
Few animals eat living soft-water plants in bulk, but the ones that do are visible and seasonal: moose wade into shallow, mineral-bottomed coves in summer and can strip *Nuphar* and pondweeds; beaver and muskrat dig lily, arrowhead and bulrush rhizomes and tubers; ducks take pondweed, watershield, pickerelweed and bulrush seeds and arrowhead tubers; crayfish graze soft, finely divided plants. The floating leaves carry a specialist insect community (*Donacia*, *Galerucella*, *Bagous*, pyralid caterpillars, leaf-mining midges, aphids, planthoppers) that shortens leaf life and feeds birds and fish.

### Cited Findings
**Moose**
- Isle Royale [NA-N]: stable-isotope mixing models put **13–27% of the summer moose diet as aquatic** — [Tischler et al. 2019, Diversity 11:209 (MTU record)](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/1363) [read abstract]. Aquatic macrophytes have higher crude protein and lower C:N than preferred terrestrial forage — [Tischler et al. 2022, Alces (search summary)](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1889) [snippet]
- Submerged and floating-leaved plants are sodium-rich where terrestrial vegetation is poor; summer consumption could cover the need if stored — [Botkin et al. 1973, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC427100) [read abstract]. Moose feed "extensively" on aquatic plants for about 8 weeks in summer; browse, 90% of annual intake, supplies only 7–14% of the annual sodium requirement — [1973 Nature letter on Isle Royale moose sodium (Nature 246:248)](https://www.nature.com/articles/246248a0.pdf) [snippet; paywalled, authors not verified]
- [ON] Cafeteria trials: moose consistently ate 7 aquatic species, rejected 5, intermediate on 7; preferred species had more sodium. Of 24 lakes, 6 were heavily used; these had **shallower water, more mineral-rich sediment and more preferred plants**, and their plants held more Na, P and Mn than the same species elsewhere — [Fraser, Chavez & Palohelmo 1984, Can. J. Zool. 62:80](https://www.wellbeingintlstudiesrepository.org/feebeh/6) [read abstract]
- [ON, Sibley Provincial Park] Exclosures in preferred lakes grew dense, palatable vegetation. In a large preferred lake ***Nuphar variegatum* and *Potamogeton filiformis* "largely disappeared"** between the 1960s and 1980, leaving sparse vegetation or annuals; *Nuphar* re-established as moose declined. Across 23 lakes *Nuphar* was absent or scarce where moose use was heavy, and the annual *P. foliosus* commonest — [Fraser & Hristienko 1983](https://wellbeingintlstudiesrepository.org/feebeh/9) [read abstract]
- Sodium's primacy is disputed: mineral licks may supply more — [Springer, Oecologia](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00377330) [snippet]

**Beaver, muskrat, deer, turtles**
- *Nuphar* (subsp. *variegata* and others) rhizomes "are valuable food source for muskrat and beaver" [NS] — [N.S. flora, Nymphaeaceae](https://ojs.library.dal.ca/NSM/article/view/5340/4819) [read]. "Birds and mammals feed upon the seeds and rhizomes" of the family — same source [read]
- *Nuphar*: turtles, birds, deer, moose, porcupines and muskrats eat parts — [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/nuphar-luteum/) [snippet]; seeds eaten by waterfowl, leaves important for muskrats — [Lauderdale Lakes 1967](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AJWL4ILJABBJPA9E/text/AS2IMQCAHGGV4Q9A) [snippet]
- *Pontederia*: muskrats and white-tailed deer occasionally browse the foliage — [Illinois Wildflowers](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/pickerelweed.htm) [read]
- *Sagittaria latifolia*: muskrats eat stalk bases, crowns and tubers; snapping and painted turtles reportedly feed on it — [Illinois Wildflowers, arrowhead](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/cm_arrowhead.htm) [read]
- Bulrush is a muskrat "staple food" — [FEIS *S. acutus*](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/graminoid/schacu/all.html) [read]. [ME] Muskrats need cattail, bulrush, sweetflag, arrowhead, wild rice or sedge over ~¾ of a wetland and can "eat out" their food (shore birds and mammals note) — [Maine IF&W muskrat assessment](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/muskrat-speciesassessment.pdf)

**Waterfowl**
- [ME, east-central Maine wetlands, 1983–85] Ring-necked ducklings ≤ 24 days ate 70% invertebrates by dry weight (32% at ≥ 25 days); freshwater sponges were 23% of all duckling food; **pondweed (*Potamogeton*) seeds were the most important plant food**; seeds or fibres of 23 plant taxa were eaten — [USGS: foods of juvenile ring-necked ducks and wetland pH](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/5220770) [read abstract]
- *Brasenia* "is eaten by waterfowl" — [MN DNR](https://dnr.state.mn.us/aquatic_plants/floatingleaf_plants/watershield.html) [read]; "seeds and vegetation are eaten by waterfowl" — [Washington Ecology](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/brasch.html) [read]. [S, Florida, winter] waterlily, spatterdock and watershield seeds were major ring-necked duck foods — [SEAFWA, Jeske](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/JESKE-130-137.pdf) [snippet]
- *P. amplifolius* seeds and plant are "good wildlife food"; *P. robbinsii* seeds and vegetation food and cover for waterfowl — [Washington Ecology](https://fortress.wa.gov/ecy/gisresources/lakes/AquaticPlantGuide/descriptions/potamp.html) [read]
- *Pontederia*: mallard, black duck, green-winged teal and wood duck occasionally eat the large seeds — [Illinois Wildflowers](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/pickerelweed.htm) [read]
- *Vallisneria*: canvasbacks need at least **125 g DW of winter buds per bird per day**; on Pool 7 they fed on wild-celery buds and stiff-arrowhead tubers — [Korschgen 1988, USFWS/UMESC](https://www.umesc.usgs.gov/documents/publications/1988/korschgen_a_1988.txt) [snippet]
- Bulrush: coots 26% of diet (California); seeds eaten by waterfowl and passerines; hens nest in it — [FEIS](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/graminoid/schacu/all.html) [read]

**Crayfish**
- Virile crayfish (*O. virilis*) "alter and reduce macrophyte biomass and diversity" (Chambers et al. 1990, Freshwater Biol. 24:81) — [IUCN GISD](https://www.iucngisd.org/gisd/species.php?sc=218) [2°]
- In enclosures, *O. virilis* significantly reduced the size of *Najas flexilis* (with *Myriophyllum heterophyllum*) — [Stafford 1991, U. Michigan](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/e1f20a86-7d8c-4b55-9680-781eb621303b) [snippet]. *O. virilis* and *O. rusticus* adults grazed caged *Chara* at similar rates — [MSU thesis](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/32011) [snippet]
- *O. immunis* above ~140–150 g/m² cut macrophyte biomass; *Procambarus clarkii* preferred delicate fresh plants with filamentous or finely branched form — [search summaries of crayfish grazing studies](https://rlp-forschung.de/public/publications/62348) [snippet] (invertebrates note gives the same thresholds)

**The lily-pad food web**
- **Specialist herbivores of *Nymphaea odorata*** [NA, compiled by Harms & Grodowitz 2009]: *Donacia* reed beetles (*D. cincticornis*, *pubescens* on roots and/or leaves; several prefer it), *Galerucella nymphaeae* (adults and larvae on floating leaves), *Bagous* weevils (larvae mine leaves and bore petioles), leaf-mining midges *Polypedilum braseniae* and *P. illinoense*, the stalk-boring midge *Tribelos quadripunctatus*, the waterlily aphid *Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae*, the planthopper *Megamelus davisi*, pyralid caterpillars (*Munroessa gyralis*, *Parapoynx* spp.) and caddis larvae (*Triaenodes*, *Mystacides*) on submerged parts; lady beetles hunt the herbivores on the pads — [Illinois Wildflowers, insects of *N. odorata*](https://w.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nymphaea_odorata.html) [read]
- **Pickerelweed's** specialists: *Donacia rugosa*; *Bellura* borer moths in stalks and petioles; the pickerelweed weevil *Onychylis nigrirostris* — [Illinois Wildflowers, pickerelweed](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/pickerelweed.htm); [*N. odorata* list](https://w.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nymphaea_odorata.html) [read]. **Arrowhead**: *Donacia*, *Listronotus* weevils, waterlily aphid, caddis *Triaenodes* — [Illinois Wildflowers, arrowhead](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/cm_arrowhead.htm) [read]
- ***Donacia* life history**: adults on the pads lay eggs (some chew a hole and glue egg rows beneath); eggs hatch in ~10 days; larvae drop to the roots and rhizomes in the muck and tap the plant's air channels with two spined terminal spiracles, so they breathe plant air 1 m or more down — [Vermont Center for Ecostudies, "A water lily's world"](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) [read; popular, citing Varley 1937, 1939]
- ***Galerucella* on *Nuphar*** [S, Ogeechee River, GA]: larvae pupate ~9 days after hatching; overlapping generations from late spring to mid-autumn; larval standing stock 283.6 mg AFDM per m² of leaf; production 27.14 g m⁻² yr⁻¹; grazing removes only the upper 25% of the leaf; **infested leaves lasted ~17 days against > 6 weeks** at a beetle-free pond; grazing releases nutrients and opens leaves to microbes — [Wallace & O'Hop 1985, Ecology 66:1534](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfd4047c7033e84bef196e) [read abstract]. [EU, Poland, shallow eutrophic lake] larger *Galerucella* populations caused more leaf damage and, through it, more microbial damage; the wind- and wave-exposed site had fewer beetles — [Polish J. Nat. Sci.](https://czasopisma.uwm.edu.pl/index.php/pjns/article/view/7489) [snippet]
- **Damage by cause on lily leaves** [EU, three Dutch waters; the oligotrophic acid pool VG, pH 4.7–5.5, is the closest analogue] — [PMC6590448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590448/) [read]:
  - *Donacia crassipes* touched 54–73% of leaves but removed ≤ 2% of area.
  - *Galerucella nymphaeae* (only on *N. alba* at VG): 30–40% of leaves Aug–Oct, 60% by mid-October; little area lost.
  - *Bagous* weevil up to 30% of leaves at VG; *Hydromyza livens* (dung-fly larvae, *Nuphar* only) < 8%; brown china-mark *Elophila* ≤ 5% of area; chironomid miners minimal.
  - Pond snails took up to 20% of leaf area **only in the two hard-water lakes** (snails need calcium); coot scratches up to 100% of leaves at the eutrophic sites, none at VG.
  - Leaf loss at the eutrophic sites stayed < 20% until mid-September, then > 50%; at VG it was minimal and leaves did not disintegrate; acidity slowed decay.
  - Fungi: *Colletotrichum nymphaeae* up to 55% of *Nymphaea* area late in the season; *Pythium* ~15% on *Nuphar*.
- ***Brasenia*'s** mucilage is widely assumed to deter snails and insects, unproven; 12 insect species reportedly feed on it; pyralid larvae chew large holes — [Missouri Dept. of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/species/water-shield); [Bill Hubick BioFiles](https://thebiofiles.com/info/2915) [snippet]. [Asia] *Galerucella birmanica* is drawn to chewed watershield by an induced volatile — [Insects 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4450/16/4/371) [snippet]

**Moss and fish herbivory**
- [Siberia, Yenisei River] Acetylenic fatty acids specific to *Fontinalis antipyretica* occurred in all dominant benthic animals (gammarids, mayflies, chironomids, caddis), so the moss enters the food web — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df56b60f3e94b9e5d7863d) [read abstract]

### Inferences
- **Moose as a sim visitor** (inf): a summer (≈ June–August, ~8 weeks) wader in coves ≤ ~1.5 m deep with mineral or firm beds, taking FL and SC shoots, preferring *Nuphar* and pondweeds. A heavily used cove loses its *Nuphar* over years and recovers when use stops. A rate needs data (see Gaps); until then a removal of order 1–5% of a cell's FL/SC shoot mass per moose-day is a placeholder to tune for visible but not total loss.
- **Beaver and muskrat** (inf): autumn–winter rhizome digging in lily and arrowhead beds (FL storage pool), local and patchy; muskrat in marsh coves (EM pool), already sketched in the shore note.
- **Duck foods** (inf): a seed and turion pool from P/FL/EM species in late summer and autumn feeds migrating ducks (ring-necked, black duck, wood duck); Hot Brook's soft water favours pondweed and watershield seeds over wild-celery buds.
- **Lily-pad herbivory in soft, coloured water** (inf): no snail grazing on pads (calcium-poor), modest *Donacia*/*Galerucella* area loss, so leaf life ~40–48 d (the European mean), shortened to ~2–3 weeks where *Galerucella* outbreaks; wave tearing dominates leaf loss on exposed shores. Beetles and their larvae are prey for fish at the pad edge and for birds (not quantified).
- **Crayfish** graze *Najas*, *Elodea*, *Nitella* and young pondweed shoots (soft, fine) but rarely lilies, isoetids or bulrush (inf from the preference studies).

### Gaps
- No moose feeding rate (kg per day) or removal per lake, and no Maine study of moose aquatic feeding; the Belovsky & Jordan 1978 PDF is a scan with no text, the Nature 1973 letter is paywalled and the Diversity 2019 paper (MDPI) returned 403. The 7 preferred species of Fraser et al. 1984 were not visible (the post-print returned 403).
- No beaver or muskrat consumption rate per area of lily or arrowhead bed.
- No source on plant eating by pumpkinseed, redbreast, golden shiner or bullhead in the wild.

## 4. Effects on the water: O₂, sediment oxidation and P, nutrient uptake, litter, shade, waves, refuge, allelopathy, carnivory

### Takeaway
Species matter most for the bed's chemistry: isoetid lawns pump oxygen into sand, keep the rhizosphere oxic, bind P on iron and oxidise methane, while lily beds pump air down to rhizomes in anoxic muck and shade and still the water above it. Floating leaves cut up to ~99% of light below them. Lily and watershield tissues are strongly allelopathic in bioassays. Bladderworts trap mostly littoral ostracods and cladocerans, and *U. purpurea* hardly feeds at all.

### Cited Findings
**Isoetids and the sediment**
- *Lobelia*: >97% of photosynthetic O₂ leaves via the roots; root respiration uses ~14× less O₂ than the roots release; pore water at 4 cm stayed oxic day and night, 21 → 3 kPa O₂ near a root and 10 → 3 kPa farther away (Lake Skånes Värsjö) — [PMC4247029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4247029) [read]
- Isoetids' large root O₂ loss usually keeps the rhizosphere oxic and prevents reduced phytotoxins — [Aquatic Ecology 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10452-012-9399-7) [snippet]
- *Littorella* [EU]: redox in vegetated sediment significantly higher than bare; cumulative P, Fe and Mn retention 2–5× that of sediment with microalgae; at steady state 43% (light) and 47% (dark) of added P retained, 63% of recovered P held in the sediment (mostly adsorbed and iron-bound, below 5 cm) against 21% in bare sediment. In Lake Kvie, SRP release at the vegetated station was far below the diffusive flux, attributed to isoetid uptake and Fe binding — [Christensen & Andersen 1996 / Andersen et al. 1997](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/2083595) [snippet]
- Iron and manganese plaques on isoetid roots restrict their own P uptake (macrophytes note) — [Christensen & Sand-Jensen 1998](https://doi.org/10.1139/b98-181) [read]
- *Lobelia* lawns had lower benthic carbon fluxes than bare sediment over two years, attributed to primary production and **methane oxidation** in the oxic rhizosphere — [HAL INRAE, *Lobelia* lawns](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02603665) [read abstract]
- Isoetid roots supply 87% of the plant's P (macrophytes note) [read].

**Floating-leaved plants**
- *Nuphar* ventilates through its rhizomes: young leaves warmed up to 5 K hold 79–100 Pa over atmospheric and drive air down; older leaves vent it — [CCLM portal, pressurised ventilation](https://www.cclmportal.ca/resource/pressurised-ventilation-floating-leaved-aquatic-macrophytes) [snippet]; pressures < 0.002 atm suffice (Dacey's thesis on *Nuphar luteum*) — [Dacey 1979, MSU](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/42061) [snippet]
- Floating-leaved plants can block up to 99% of incident light — [Ribaudo et al. 2012, J. Limnol.](https://jlimnol.it/jlimnol/article/download/mnol.2012.e21/470/939) [snippet]; they usually make too much shade for most plants beneath — [MN DNR, floating-leaved communities](https://www-dev.dnr.state.mn.us/rys/pg/floatingleaved.html) [snippet]. *Nymphaea odorata* produced aerial leaves only above ~30–40% leaf cover, not under shade cloth alone — [Etnier et al. 2017, Northeastern Naturalist](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-24-2/14-Etnier.shtml) [snippet]
- Floating-leaved *Trapa* beds fell below 2.5 mg/L DO up to 40% of the time while *Vallisneria* beds never fell below 5 (macrophytes note, Caraco & Cole 2002) [read].
- *Nuphar* pumps P root → leaf ~4× the reverse and secretes it in summer (macrophytes note) — [Twilley et al. 1977](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1977.22.6.1022) [read]
- Litter: *Nuphar variegata* 72% labile (k 0.13 d⁻¹), 28% refractory (k 0.0034); *Scirpus subterminalis* 52% labile at only k 0.02 (macrophytes note) — [Cunha-Santino & Bianchini 2006](https://www.revistas.ufrj.br/index.php/oa/article/view/5655) [read]. Lily leaf mass and chemistry change fast in the first 10–30 days; acidity slowed decomposition at the Dutch acid pool — [PMC6590448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590448/) [read]

**Allelopathy**
- Of 16 southeastern US hydrophytes tested on lettuce and *Lemna*, ***Nymphaea odorata* and *Brasenia* were highly inhibitory** — [Elakovich & Wooten, USACE TR A-89-2](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA217441.pdf) [read abstract]
- *Brasenia* extracts inhibited 2 algae and 9 bacteria and were allelopathic to lettuce — [Elakovich & Wooten 1987, J. Chem. Ecol.](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/16443) [read abstract]
- *Nuphar lutea* leaf extract at 25 ppt cut *Lemna* fronds to 34% of control; "many times more inhibitory" than 16 other hydrophytes — [Elakovich & Wooten 1991, J. Chem. Ecol.](https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/7011) [read abstract]

**Carnivory (bladderworts)**
- [MI, two northern lakes] Bladderwort prey were mostly littoral cladocerans and ostracods: ostracods dominated early in the season, cladocerans later; ostracods were more common in traps than in the water, cladocerans the reverse; trap size set prey size, not prey number — [Diversity 2023, 15:171](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-2818/15/2/171) [snippet; page returned 403]
- [EU, Austria] *U. vulgaris* (Seetaler Lake): ~31 objects per trap, ~28 of them animals, ~1.9 algae (a snapshot, no time base) — [Ann. Bot. 2015, table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551088/table/mcu236-T2) [snippet]
- *U. purpurea* [S, Everglades]: ~26% of biomass in bladders, yet few microinvertebrates captured — [Richards 2001](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub80237) [read abstract]. Trap fluid is anoxic in several species, so anoxia-tolerant organisms live there as commensals — [Płachno et al. 2012](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Plachno2012.pdf) [snippet]. In nutrient-poor humic waters the value of carnivory for growth is disputed — [Sirová et al.](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf) [snippet]
- Prey supplementation did not raise *Utricularia* shoot biomass in an 11-day trial but lengthened shoots — [Adamec, contrasting growth effects](https://asep.lib.cas.cz/arl-cav/en/detail-cav_un_epca-0347399-Contrasting-growth-effects-of-prey-capture-in-two-aquatic-carnivorous-plant-species) [snippet]; prey capture feeds trap production more than shoot growth under good light and CO₂ — [Adamec 2015, Phyton](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf) [snippet]

**Waves, currents, temperature, refuge** — the form-level effects (current −90% inside beds, resuspension −53–66%, canopy stratification when plants fill > ½ the depth, the PVI refuge) are in the macrophytes note §5.1, §5.4 and are not repeated. Bulrush buffers wind and wave action (qualitative) — [FEIS](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/graminoid/schacu/all.html) [read].

### Inferences
- **Bed oxidation by species** (inf): IS cells (and *Littorella* if present) cut the bed's P release in proportion to their biomass, e.g. a multiplier on `sedOM`-driven P flux of 1 − 0.5·cover, and run net methane oxidation; this flips off when sediment organic matter passes ~2.4% labile (the *Lobelia* threshold) because the plants die.
- **Under lilies** (inf): light to the bed × (1 − 0.9·cover) is already in `PL3.SHADE_FL`; add reaeration blocking and a modest phyto/periphyton suppression term under FL cover for *Nuphar*, *Nymphaea* and *Brasenia* (allelopathy), weak and flagged as speculative since the evidence is bioassay-only.
- ***Utricularia*** (inf): a small, size-selective sink on littoral cladocerans and ostracods inside SC cells, taking nutrients from them; in soft, coloured water its growth should not depend on prey (Sirová, Richards). Its turions sink in autumn; as a rootless plant it takes all its N and P from the water.
- **Lily litter** is labile and decays fast in summer but slowly in acid water (inf from the Dutch pool), so a soft-water lily bed builds an organic muck that excludes isoetids — consistent with MNAP's split (lilies on organic soil, pipewort–lobelia on mineral soil).

### Gaps
- No radial O₂ loss rate per m² of isoetid lawn, no oxidised-layer depth, and no Maine or New England isoetid sediment study.
- No measured light transmission under *Nuphar*, *Nymphaea* or *Brasenia* canopies (only the ≤ 99% upper bound).
- No daily *Utricularia* capture rate per bladder or per gram (the Michigan study's numbers were behind a 403).
- No evidence that allelopathy matters in the field in these lakes.
- Dacey 1980 (Science) flow rates and *Nuphar* methane transport were not read.

## 5. Insects and others "on top of" plants: emergence, perches, oviposition, frogs, turtles, nesting birds

### Takeaway
Plants that break the surface are the lake's climbing frames and platforms. Dragonfly and damselfly larvae climb emergent stems and leaves to emerge, mostly 5–50 cm above the water, and emergence sites can be limiting; damselflies lay eggs into floating-leaf petioles and leaf bases; adult odonates perch on lily pads and stems. Painted turtles bask on floating lily-tuber and root mats; mink frogs float among lily pads; loons nest on the shoreline (vegetation and mud, often on islands); herons and egrets nest in dead bulrush stands.

### Cited Findings
**Odonate emergence**
- A *Cordulia* population emerged mostly 5–50 cm above the water (mean ~20 cm), using reed stalks and the undersides of bogbean leaves — [Hokkaido Univ. repository](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/27560/19(1)_P251-269.pdf) [snippet]. Most species rarely climb above 50 cm; some climb trees up to 14.6 m, perhaps when emergence sites are scarce — [Great Lakes Entomologist 51(1)](https://scholar.valpo.edu/tgle/vol51/iss1/5) [snippet]
- *Pachydiplax longipennis* in cattail mesocosms: at low cattail density emergence began ~5 days earlier and larvae fixed at 18 cm, against 13 cm (medium) and 10 cm (high density) — [International Journal of Odonatology 2018](https://worlddragonfly.org/article/13887890-2018-1520652/) [snippet]
- In a walled pond, every exuvia was on emergent vegetation (*Digitaria*, *Cyperus*, *Typha*), and species emerged at characteristic heights tied to larval size — [Notulae Odonatologicae 1995](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/593562/NOIOS1995004006004.pdf) [snippet]

**Oviposition and perching**
- [EU] *Pyrrhosoma nymphula* laid eggs into *P. natans* almost only at the leaf base and petiole, where tandems land; ovipositions lasted 9.9 min on average at 5.56 eggs/min; reflections from the plant edges help site choice — [Odonatologica (natuurtijdschriften)](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/591938/OJIOS1991020001011.pdf) [snippet]
- [EU] Territorial *Erythromma najas* males perch on floating Nymphaeaceae leaves; females oviposit into hornwort and broad-leaved pondweed, partly or fully submerging — [J. Br. Dragonfly Soc. 20(2)](https://british-dragonflies.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/JBDS_Vol20no2.pdf) [snippet]
- [Asia] *Ceriagrion* chose floating *Nymphaea* leaves for 69% of ovipositions (vs *Lemna* 23%, submerged *Hydrilla* 8%), one bout putting 283 eggs in 16 rows under a leaf — [J. Threatened Taxa](https://threatenedtaxa.org/index.php/JoTT/article/view/3537) [snippet]
- Most damselflies and aeshnid dragonflies insert eggs into plant tissue, which reduces drying and winter mortality — same source [snippet]
- *Donacia* adults stand and lay eggs on the pads (see §3) — [VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) [read]

**Vertebrates on plants** (from the shore notes)
- Painted turtles: highest NB abundance where basking sites such as lily tubers, fallen trees and logs abound; floating lily-tuber and root mats used for basking; MDIFW habitat "permanent water with submerged vegetation and plentiful basking areas" — [COSEWIC 2018 painted turtle](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/species-risk-public-registry/cosewic-assessments-status-reports/midland-eastern-painted-turtle-2018.html) [read] (turtles and frogs note)
- Mink frogs "float among lily pads and emergents in shallow water, rarely on land" (turtles and frogs note §frogs).
- Loons [ME]: nests of dead vegetation and mud "right on the shoreline", often on small islands; boat wakes flood shoreline nests — [Maine IF&W, Living with loons](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/livingwithloons.pdf) [read] (shore birds note)
- Herons and egrets nest in dead bulrush stands of 35–80 dead stems/m²; redhead and canvasback hens prefer bulrush for nesting cover (prairies) — [FEIS](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/graminoid/schacu/all.html) [read]
- Turtles reportedly feed on arrowhead — [Illinois Wildflowers](https://www.illinoiswildflowers.info/wetland/plants/cm_arrowhead.htm) [read]

### Inferences
- **Emergence sites** (inf): give each EM and FL cell an emergence capacity (stems or leaf edges breaking the surface per m²); odonate larvae ready to emerge in that cell's column climb there (render at 5–50 cm, mean 20 cm above the water; lower in dense stands). Where a littoral cell has no emergent or floating structure, larvae walk to the nearest one or to the shore, at some cost in predation.
- **Oviposition sites**: damselflies need FL petioles and leaf bases (*P. natans*, *P. epihydrus*, lilies) or SC stems near the surface in June–August.
- **Perches**: lily and watershield pads and emergent tips are the odonate perching layer; lily pads also hold *Donacia* adults, frogs (mink and green frogs) and, in coves, basking painted turtles on rhizome mats.
- **Loon nest** placement can read shoreline cells with EM vegetation or bog mat on islands or sheltered shores (inf; the shore note has the calendar).

### Gaps
- No North American data on odonate emergence or oviposition by plant species (pickerelweed, lilies, watershield, bulrush), and no emergence-site density per m².
- No data on frogs' use of lily pads by species beyond the mink-frog statement, or on loon nest substrate by plant species in Maine.

**Notes on the table** (inf):
- `lightLim` for *Lobelia* is from Borowiak's 43%; the others are scaled from the macrophytes note's form values by the Lake George compensation ranks (*Vallisneria* ≈ *Elodea* < *P. amplifolius* < *P. robbinsii* — note *P. robbinsii* has the higher compensation point yet grows deeper, presumably by being evergreen and low).
- `invert` multiplies the invertebrates note's SC per-gram value (30–100 per g, central 60).
- `carry` for watershield is low because ramets die; its persistence comes from the buds staying put, not from mass.
- For the sim, species can share one growth engine (the existing form physiology) with per-species overrides of these columns; competition can stay as now (shared light, shade under FL, mud vs sand), with isoetids losing on organic mud and lilies needing shelter.

**What species add that forms cannot** (inf):
1. Lily pads as an above-water habitat layer (beetles, frogs, perching odonates, turtles on rhizome mats) and a moose/beaver food.
2. Watershield clones that are stable in place and capped near 2 m.
3. A green under-ice carpet (*P. robbinsii*, *Elodea*, isoetids, *Nitella*) for winter O₂ production and invertebrates.
4. Tall bass/pickerel cover (*P. amplifolius*) distinct from low carpets.
5. Isoetid lawns as a bed-chemistry engine on sand (O₂, P, methane).
6. Bladderworts as a plant predator of littoral zooplankton.
7. Species-specific spawning: dead bulrush and lily stems (pickerel, perch), live pondweed (shiner, killifish), sand beside isoetids (sunfish and bass nests).

### Gaps
- No Hot Brook plant survey exists (macrophytes note §6): the species set is inferred from Maine soft-water communities (MNAP, Highland Lake) and Hellquist's chemistry, plus the owner's report of weeds and lilies.
- Per-species `Bmax`, `mu`, `tStart` and depth for most rows are inferences from form ranges; they need calibration in the model against the depth limits and the owner's observations.
- The carbon-source column is unverified for *Vallisneria*, bladderworts other than *U. purpurea*, *P. epihydrus*, *S. subterminalis* and *P. robbinsii*.
- The invert column for isoetids, *Nitella*, moss, bladderworts and floating leaves has no North American soft-water measurement.
