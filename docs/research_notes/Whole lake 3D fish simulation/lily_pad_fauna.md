Compiled 2026-10-16 for the Hot Brook 3-D sim. ## 1. The water-lily leaf beetle *Galerucella nymphaeae*: life cycle and generations in the Northeast, densities, leaf damage, effect on the lilies, and fish predation

### Takeaway
At northeastern-US latitudes *Galerucella nymphaeae* is **essentially univoltine with a small partial second generation**:
- Adults overwinter on shore and lay clutches of **9–19 eggs** at **3–7 clutches a week** on the upper surface of new pads in June.
- Larvae and pupae live on the leaf surface.
- Most of the summer generation's females enter diapause instead of breeding.
- Further south (Georgia) it is multivoltine, and a heavy infestation cuts a *Nuphar* leaf's life to 17 days.
- In Finnish ponds (the closest climate analogue) it ate only **3–6% of the annual floating-leaf production**, and grazing even raised leaf production.
- Larval survival is low (5–11% over 4 weeks on a poor host; half of first instars die in the first week). Water striders and a ladybird eat larvae and pupae, and predatory ladybird larvae cut damage to *Nuphar* by **35–43%** in a Michigan lake.
- **No fish-diet record** of *Galerucella* was found. The likely routes into fish are drowning (sinking leaves) and dislodgement.

### Cited Findings
**Voltinism, fecundity and phenology (Northeast and north)**
- [NY, probably the Tauber lab at Cornell (inf); site not given in the record]:
  - Females laid **3 to 7 egg masses per week**, with **9 to 19 eggs per mass**, and each female kept a consistent clutch size.
  - "Most summer-generation females entered reproductive diapause without ovipositing". Only a small share of females that emerged early, **by mid-June**, laid eggs before diapause.
  - The overwintering population is mainly first-generation adults plus a few from the second.
  - Short days and cold (LD 10:14, 5 °C) gave diapause that ended on transfer to long days and warmth (LD 18:6, 21 °C).
  - Laboratory heat-degree models predicted field development of eggs and pupae, but not larvae.
  - — [Tauber, Tauber & Nechols 1996, Physiol. Entomol. 21:317–324, AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df8b847c7033e84bee10cf) (abstract)
- [VT] Adults lay eggs on leaf surfaces **in June**. At peak numbers the larvae make leaves "last just a third as long as uninhabited leaves" — [McFarland 2016, Vermont Center for Ecostudies, "A Water Lily's World"](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) (popular)
- [WI field station; general North America]:
  - "Probably two generations per year, with the second overwintering as adults".
  - Eggs are laid above the water, "a few per leaf", preferring young leaves, and hatch about a week later.
  - The average leaf lives less than a month, which is shorter than a larva needs, so larvae move between leaves when young.
  - Adults "waterski" at about 0.5 m/s.
  - Coots were seen "picking the beetles/pupae/larvae off of the leaves" (an uncited European study).
  - — [UWM Field Station, "Water Lily Leaf Beetle II"](https://uwm.edu/field-station/?p=6695) (popular)
- [EU-UK] Univoltine. The egg-to-adult cycle takes about 8 months, counting the overwintering adult. Overwintered adults stay active into summer, new adults appear from July, and pupae are found from July to late September — [UK beetles site, via search summary](https://www.ukbeetles.co.uk/galerucella-nymphaea-sagittariae) (snippet; the page returned 403)
- [EU-HU, secondary]:
  - Clutches of 10–15 eggs; 80–120 eggs per female (thermal lakes).
  - The larval stage lasts "5 to 10 days" with three moults; pupae stay on the leaf about a week.
  - Two generations a year in central Europe, four in thermal lakes.
  - — [Wikipedia, *Galerucella nymphaeae*, citing György & Viktor 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galerucella_nymphaeae) (secondary)
- [EU-FI] In northern Europe, *G. nymphaeae* sensu stricto lives on *Nuphar* and *G. sagittariae* on semi-aquatic and terrestrial plants. Old Finnish "nymphaeae" data should be checked for which taxon is meant — [search summary of Eur. J. Entomol. 1998](https://eje.cz/pdfs/eje/1998/02/10.pdf) (snippet)
- Conflicting North American statements: one source calls the species "largely univoltine" in its area but says five or more generations are possible depending on latitude. The UWM page says "probably two" — [search summary; the page behind the quote was not pinned down, BugGuide is the likeliest](https://www.bugguide.net/node/view/83801) (snippet)

**Densities and survival**
- [NY, central; on introduced water chestnut]:
  - Larvae were released at 10–50 per rosette, densities "we frequently observe in the field".
  - Survival after 4 weeks was **5.1% (50 larvae) to 11.3% (10 larvae)**, and first-instar mortality was **50% within 1 week**, not caused by lack of food.
  - No effect on plant growth, biomass or reproduction.
  - — [Ding & Blossey 2005, Environ. Entomol. 34:683–689, BioOne](https://bioone.org/journals/Environmental-Entomology/volume-34/issue-3/0046-225X-34.3.683/Impact-of-the-Native-Water-Lily-Leaf-Beetle-Galerucella-nymphaeae/10.1603/0046-225X-34.3.683.full) (snippet; the page loaded empty)
- [NY] In a common garden, survival was **about 54% on *Nuphar*** against **24% on *Trapa***, with larvae drowning as they moved between small leaves. Adults strongly preferred the native host — [Environ. Entomol. 38:1653–1660 (2009)](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/environmental-entomology/volume-38/issue-6/022.038.0618/Differences-in-Preference-and-Performance-of-the-Water-Lily-Leaf/10.1603/022.038.0618.full) (snippet)
- Leaf quality drops quickly after larvae hatch. Leaves eventually sink, "whereupon the eggs, 1st-instar larvae and pupae are killed by drowning" — [abstract text shown in search results, probably a Kouki abstract indexed at fitforthem.unipa.it](https://fitforthem.unipa.it/subject:Galerucella%20nymphaeae) (snippet; host page not confirmed)
- [GA, Ogeechee River] Larval production alone exceeded the *Nuphar* standing crop, so the beetle depends on fast leaf turnover — [Wallace & O'Hop 1985, AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfd4047c7033e84bef196e) (abstract). (The standing stock and production values are in `plant_species_traits.md`.)
- [EU-PL, shallow eutrophic lake] Larval and adult abundance, density and biomass differed between two sites. Larger populations at the sheltered site caused more leaf damage and, through it, more microbial damage. Wind and waves "impeded the development of the G. nymphaeae population" — [Ścibior et al. 2022, Polish J. Nat. Sci. 37(4):517–537](https://czasopisma.uwm.edu.pl/index.php/pjns/article/view/7489) (snippet; site unreachable)
- No per-leaf or per-m² density of eggs, larvae or adults on *Nuphar*, *Nymphaea* or *Brasenia* in a northeastern lake was found. Kouki's oviposition paper ([Oikos 61:243–249, 1991, record](https://marineinfo.org/doc/publication/37147)) likely has per-leaf clutch data (record only).

**Damage and effect on the lilies**
- [EU-FI, three ponds in central Finland]:
  - Adults and larvae consumed **3.0–6.1% of the net annual floating-leaf production** during the growing period.
  - The paper's title reports that grazing *increases* *Nuphar* leaf production and alters C and N dynamics, including detritus input.
  - — [Oecologia, "Galerucella nymphaeae grazing increases Nuphar leaf production…"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00317528) (snippet; the authors were not confirmed)
- [EU-FI] The following came from a search summary listing Kouki's Freshwater Biology paper. That paper is [Kouki 1991, Freshw. Biol. 26:347–354, RG listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230128206_The_effect_of_the_water-lily_beetle_Galerucella_nymphaeae_on_leaf_production_and_leaf_longevity_of_the_yellow_water-lily_Nuphar_lutea) (snippet; 403). Which paper each number comes from is not verified:
  - Leaf longevity was cut by up to **60%**.
  - Leaves that emerged during peak larval growth lived **one-third** as long as controls.
  - In a single-summer experiment, leaf density rose **×1.23 with beetles present and ×1.61 with herbivory removed**.
- [MI, littoral of a Michigan lake, *Nuphar advena*]:
  - Caged predatory ladybird larvae (*Coleomegilla maculata*) cut grazing damage by *G. nymphaeae* larvae **by 35–43%**, mainly by reducing grazer numbers, with signs of trait-mediated effects as well.
  - The authors frame this as a trophic cascade at the land–water ecotone.
  - — [Schlacher & Cronin 2007, Ecol. Res. 22:749–755](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-tropic-cascade-in-a-macrophyte-based/99449955402621) (abstract)
- [WI] "A leaf that's just one-quarter eaten can become unusable", and the beetles can "eat their island out from under themselves" — [UWM Field Station](https://uwm.edu/field-station/?p=6695) (popular)
- A comparative feeding-selectivity study of semi-terrestrial vs aquatic insects on water lilies exists, but only its title was seen: [Cronin, Wissing & Lodge 1998, Freshw. Biol. 39:243–257](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11284-006-0312-5) (record only, cited in Schlacher & Cronin).

**Predators**
- [NY] In experiments:
  - The water strider *Gerris insperatus* and the ladybird *C. maculata* "significantly reduced larval and pupal survival".
  - Backswimmers (*Notonecta*) "always rejected" the larvae.
  - Host plant (*Trapa* or *Nuphar*) did not change the outcome.
  - — [Ding & Blossey predator study, search summary; the record behind it is probably this ISC inventory entry, not confirmed](https://search.isc.ac/Inventory/10/26522.htm) (snippet)
- Spotted lady beetle *Coleomegilla maculata* and thirteen-spotted lady beetle *Hippodamia tredecimpunctata* are recorded hunting herbivores on *Nuphar*'s floating and aerial leaves — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar lutea* insects (Harms & Grodowitz 2009)](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)
- Fish: no stomach-content record naming *Galerucella* (or *Pyrrhalta*) was found in searches of pumpkinseed, bluegill, perch, rudd or roach diets — [search summary](https://uwm.edu/field-station/?p=6695) (gap). One low-reliability web page lists bullfrog and mallard as predators of adults — [Snappit, via search](https://www.snappit.app/explore/waterlily-leaf-beetle) (popular; not used).

### Inferences
- **Calendar for Hot Brook** (inf). Ice-out is about Apr 23. *Nuphar* and *Nymphaea* pads reach the surface late May–early June (`plant_species_traits.md`), so:
  - Overwintered adults fly to the pads late May–early June.
  - Egg clutches are laid through June (VT says June).
  - Larvae feed mid-June to late July (about 2–4 weeks at 18–24 °C), and pupae sit on the leaves in July.
  - New adults appear from July. Most enter diapause; a small early fraction (eclosing by mid-June to early July) lays a partial second brood in late July–August.
  - Adults leave the pads for shore litter in September.
  - The Wikipedia "5–10 day larval stage" and the Georgia "pupate 9 days after hatching" are warm-water values. In a 15–24 °C Maine summer, larval development of 2–3 weeks is more plausible. Tauber's degree-day models (in the full text) would pin this down.
- **Damage in a soft, tea-coloured Maine lake** (inf). Take the Finnish figure: 3–6% of annual floating-leaf production eaten, plus a modest cut in leaf life where larvae are concentrated. The 17-day leaf life of the multivoltine Georgia river is an outbreak ceiling, not a norm. Clumping helps the beetles: sheltered coves get more beetles and more damage than wave-exposed shores (Poland). A sim rule could therefore make the damage rate fall with fetch or wave exposure.
- **Grazing raises leaf production** in Finland. The sim's lily leaf turnover could therefore respond to herbivory with faster leaf replacement, not net biomass loss, at low to moderate beetle loads.
- **As fish food** (inf), *Galerucella* is a minor, indirect item:
  - eggs, young larvae and pupae drown when a damaged leaf sinks;
  - larvae must move between leaves and fall into the water;
  - adults skate on the surface.
  - These are surface items for pumpkinseed, redbreast, fallfish and killifish at the pad edge. With 5–11% larval survival, about 90% of the larval cohort dies in place, most of it as drowned or dislodged larvae and some as predation by striders and ladybirds. That gives the sim a defensible source of small (≤ 8 mm) prey falling into FL cells in June–July.
- Striders and ladybirds in the sim could be represented implicitly, as a larval mortality term. A scripted strider population on the pad edge would be a cosmetic option.

### Gaps
- No egg, larva or adult density per leaf or per m² of pads from any northeastern North American lake. Kouki 1991 (Oikos) and Ścibior et al. 2022 likely have per-leaf numbers (not readable).
- The authors and full numbers of the Finnish Oecologia paper, and which paper the "×1.23/×1.61" and "−60%" figures come from, remain unverified.
- No development-time data at Maine summer temperatures; Tauber et al.'s degree-day thresholds are in the full text only.
- No fish-diet study that names *Galerucella*. Birds: only the European coot observation.
- No data on *Galerucella* on *Brasenia* in North America. Only the Asian *G. birmanica* on cultivated watershield is documented ([Insects 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4450/16/4/371), snippet).

## 2. *Donacia* (long-horned leaf beetles, reed beetles) on lilies, watershield, pickerelweed and bulrush: larvae on roots breathing plant air, adults on leaves, life-cycle length, densities, fish predation

### Takeaway
North America has a specific *Donacia* fauna for each of the sim's plants:
- nine *Donacia* species on *Nymphaea* and *Nuphar*;
- *D. caerulea*, *D. fulgens* and *D. rugosa* on pickerelweed;
- *Donacia* and most *Plateumaris* species on *Scirpus*, *Carex* and *Eleocharis*;
- *D. cincticornis* and others on *Potamogeton*;
- *Neohaemonia* on pondweeds.

The life history:
- Adults chew and lay eggs on floating leaves (on the underside, between overlapping leaves or inside the stem). The eggs hatch in about 10 days.
- Larvae drop to the roots and rhizomes and tap the aerenchyma with two spiracular hooks.
- They pupate in an air-filled silk cocoon fixed to the root. The new adult stays underwater in the cocoon over winter and floats up in late spring.
- The cycle takes 1 to 3 years by species and site; European accounts describe 2- and 3-year cycles with larvae overwintering twice.

No density per m² and no fish-predation record was found for any North American *Donacia*. Damage to leaves is small (≤ 2% of area in the Dutch study already in the plant note).

### Cited Findings
- **Host plants of North American species**:
  - *Nymphaea*/*Nuphar*: *Donacia cincticornis*, *D. liebecki*, *D. palmata*, *D. parvidens*, *D. piscatrix*, *D. proxima*, *D. megacornis*, *D. rufescens*, *D. texana*; *Donaciella pubicollis* also uses *Nymphaea*.
  - *Pontederia cordata*: *D. caerulea*, *D. fulgens*, *D. rugosa*.
  - *Sagittaria*: *D. caerulea*, *D. magnifica*.
  - *Sparganium*: *D. confluenta*, *D. fulgens*, *D. subtilis*, *D. tuberculifrons*.
  - *Potamogeton*: *D. hirticollis*, *D. magnifica*, *D. cincticornis*, plus *Neohaemonia* spp.
  - *Nymphoides*: *D. cincticornis*.
  - *Carex*, *Scirpus*, *Eleocharis*, *Juncus*: *D. biimpressa*, *D. distincta*, *D. porosicollis* and most *Plateumaris* (Askevold 1991).
  - The list is based on Downie & Arnett 1996 and Riley et al. 2003, and the page calls its citations "provisional". *Brasenia* is not mentioned.
  - — [aquaticinsects.org, Chrysomelidae](https://aquaticinsects.org/sp/Coleoptera/sp_com_chrysomelidae.html) (full text)
- *Donacia piscatrix* and *D. proxima* are listed on *Nuphar* "roots and/or leaves" — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar lutea* (HG2009)](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)
- **Larval respiration and pupation**:
  - Larvae "use a pair of specialized hooks at the end of the abdomen to tap oxygen" from plant air chambers (Jolivet & Hawkeswood 1995).
  - Mandibles macerate plant tissue, and the maxillae suck plant fluids.
  - "Larvae construct pupal chambers on their host plants" connected to the plant's air spaces.
  - — [aquaticinsects.org](https://aquaticinsects.org/sp/Coleoptera/sp_com_chrysomelidae.html) (full text)
  - "How donaciines obtain oxygen is not definitively demonstrated to be just one mechanism" (Askevold, in press) — [Korean *Donacia* paper, PMC12663724](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12663724/) (full text)
- **Oviposition types** (after Bieńkowski 1996):
  - (I) between overlapping floating leaves, under a protective membrane (*D. aquatica*);
  - (II) inside the stem near the water surface (*D. bicoloricornis*, *D. clavareaui*);
  - (III) along the feeding trace on the leaf underside (*D. provostii*, *D. lenzi*, which prefer nymphaeid habitats).
  - Adults feed on leaves and flowers above water.
  - — [PMC12663724](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12663724/) (full text)
- [VT] Eggs are glued to the leaves in rows and hatch after about 10 days, and the larvae drop to the roots. Larvae make a waterproof silk cocoon and fill it with air. **Adults stay underwater until the following spring, then float to the surface carrying air bubbles** — [VCE, "A Water Lily's World"](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) (popular)
- [WI; the page calls them "waterlily leaf beetles" but describes Donaciinae]:
  - Females "chew a hole in a lily leaf" to lay eggs, which hatch in about 10 days.
  - Larvae feed in submerged stems, roots and rhizomes and pupate after "a few weeks" of feeding.
  - The adult "aestivates inside its cocoon until it emerges at the beginning of the next summer".
  - Adults also feed on arrowhead, *Potamogeton*, milfoil, sedges and rushes.
  - The moth *Nymphula maculalis* lays its eggs around the beetle's egg holes.
  - — [UWM Field Station, "Waterlily Leaf Beetle"](https://uwm.edu/field-station/bug-of-the-week/waterlily-leaf-beetle/) (popular)
- [EU-UK] **Life-cycle length**:
  - Water-lily feeders (on *Nuphar* and *Nymphaea*) are described as having a **three-year** cycle, with adults overwintering in cocoons.
  - One species overwinters as 2nd-instar larvae in its first winter and 5th instars in its second.
  - An arrowhead species has a **two-year** cycle, with larvae overwintering as 1st and then 5th instars.
  - *D. cinerea* larvae feed on the rhizomes and roots of common club-rush (*Schoenoplectus lacustris*).
  - — [coleoptera.org.uk species pages, via search](https://coleoptera.org.uk/node?page=937) (snippet)
- [EU-NL, three Dutch waters; already in `plant_species_traits.md`] *Donacia crassipes* touched 54–73% of lily leaves but removed ≤ 2% of the area. Eggs are laid on the underside of *Nuphar* floating leaves — [PMC6590448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590448/) (full text, earlier note)
- Fish predation: searches found **no** record of *Donacia* larvae or adults in fish stomachs. Larvae spend their life inside root-zone sediment or attached to rhizomes. A lab study showed fish larvae lose their identifiable features quickly in bluegill and perch stomachs, so soft larvae may be under-recorded — [search summary](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1101-RR1200/RR1115.pdf) (snippet; general caveat only)

### Inferences
- **Sim mechanism** (inf). *Donacia* ties the FL and EM fields to the sediment:
  1. Adults live on the pads and stems from late May to July, where they are rarely eaten.
  2. Eggs sit on the pads in June–July.
  3. Larvae live in the rhizome zone of the FL/EM cell for 1–2 Maine winters, sealed from fish by the sediment.
  4. Pupae sit in air cocoons on the roots in late summer.
  5. Adults float up in late May–June after ice-out.
- The spring float-up of adults through the water column is the one moment *Donacia* is plainly exposed to fish. It is a plausible small surface or mid-water prey pulse in early June (inf, not documented).
- For a deterministic sim, a 2-year cohort (eggs → larvae through two winters → adults) is a reasonable default for Maine. The European 3-year cycle sets the upper bound (inf).
- Bulrush (*Schoenoplectus*) and pickerelweed beds each get their own *Donacia*/*Plateumaris* guild. The rhizome-feeding larvae drain only a small, unmeasured share of rhizome reserves. Treat them as a negligible plant loss and a negligible fish-food source except at adult emergence (inf).

### Gaps
- No North American *Donacia* larval density (per plant, per rhizome, per m²) and no adult density per leaf.
- No measured cycle length for any North American *Donacia*. Askevold's 1990/1991 monographs and Hoffman 1940 (Michigan Donaciini) are the likely sources, but none was readable.
- No fish- or bird-predation data on adults or larvae.
- *Brasenia*-specific *Donacia* hosts were not found in the readable lists.

## 3. Other pad and stem fauna: *Bellura* borers, aquatic caterpillars (*Elophila*/*Synclita*/*Parapoynx*), leaf-mining and petiole-boring midges, aphids and planthoppers, snails and eggs under the pads, fishing spiders, water striders and frogs; which are fish food

### Takeaway
The floating leaf carries a layered fauna:
- **On top**: *Galerucella*, *Donacia* adults, aphids (*Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae*), the pond-lily planthopper (*Megamelus davisi*), ladybirds, striders and fishing spiders on the leaf edge.
- **In the blade**: midges mining the upper surface (*Polypedilum braseniae* on *Brasenia* and *N. odorata*), and the young of the *Bellura* moth and *Hydromyza* fly.
- **In the petiole**: *Polypedilum fallax/tritum* and *Tribelos* filter-feeders boring in, older *Bellura* larvae, and the waterlily borer *Elophila gyralis*.
- **On the underside**: case-making caterpillars (*Nymphula/Parapoynx maculalis* very common on lilies and *Brasenia* in a central-Massachusetts lake), *Donacia* and caddis eggs, snails, sponges, bryozoans and rotifers.

The most fish-relevant parts:
- Bluegill in a Massachusetts lake have a distinct **"surface grazing" mode: searching beneath lily pads with explosive suction bouts**. That behaviour is the "popping" heard in lily beds at dawn and dusk.
- Small sunfish **eat fishing spiders** and wipe out tadpole and cricket prey in pools.
- No published density per m² of pad was found for any of the moth, midge or aphid fauna in the Northeast.

### Cited Findings
**Moths**
- [MA, Lake Quinsigamond, central Massachusetts] *Nymphula* (*Nymphaeella*) *maculalis* (now *Parapoynx maculalis*):
  - Its caterpillars live on *Castalia* (= *Nymphaea*), *Nuphar* and *Brasenia*, and were "very common wherever either of the waterlilies grew".
  - The larva cuts a piece from the leaf margin and silks it to the upper or under surface as a nest. The full-sized nest is a broad oval **1–1.5 in (25–38 mm)** long and makes a hump on the underside. Larvae can carry the cut piece as a water-filled portable case.
  - Young larvae eat one epidermis and the parenchyma under the nest. From about a quarter inch they feed on uninjured leaf pieces outside the nest and on young submerged leaves.
  - Pupation is in the last nest, lined with silk.
  - **First brood in July; young caterpillars from late August; egg stage about 10 days.**
  - — [Forbes 1910, Psyche 17:219](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/psyche/17/17-219.html) (full text)
- *Elophila gyralis* (waterlily borer moth):
  - Eggs are laid in masses of about **25–35** and hatch in about **12 days**.
  - Larvae skeletonize the lower leaf surface, then over **10–14 days** move to the petiole, bore **2–3 cm** into its top and rest head-up, feeding at night. The plant forms a gall-like swelling.
  - They pupate in the silk-lined cavity.
  - "**Populations in Michigan are univoltine**"; half-grown larvae overwinter.
  - Larvae breathe through the cuticle and have no gills.
  - — [UWM Field Station, "Waterlily Borer Moth" (2025), quoting Moths of North Carolina](https://uwm.edu/field-station/bug-of-the-week/waterlily-borer-moth/) (popular)
- [VT; species not named; my inference is the "white-tailed diver" *Bellura gortynoides*]:
  - Adults live about 6 days, and a female lays up to **900 eggs** on pad surfaces.
  - Tiny green caterpillars feed on the leaves and tunnel into the stalks. In about **3 weeks** they grow from a few mm to over an inch (> 25 mm) and turn deep red.
  - They then "swim to land using a porpoise motion with the rear third of their body" and pupate in a silk-lined chamber in soil.
  - — [VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) (popular)
- *Bellura gortynoides* (yellow pond-lily borer):
  - Adults fly **May–September**.
  - **One generation in the north** (two in the south), overwintering as mature larvae.
  - Eggs are laid in clusters on host leaves. Hatchlings **mine the blade for the first three instars**, then bore further in.
  - Hosts: *Nuphar*. Cattail and pickerelweed records are probably confusion with other *Bellura*.
  - — [BugGuide, via search](https://www.bugguide.net/node/view/83121) (snippet)
  - The primary study is Levine & Chandler 1976, Ann. Entomol. Soc. Am. 69:405–414 [IN] — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/647362b5e17b74d22253d12d) (record only).
  - The Illinois *Nymphaea* list calls a *Bellura* the "White-Tailed Diver" that bores leaves and stems — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nymphaea* spp.](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nymphaea_spp.html) (snippet)
- *Bellura densa* (pickerelweed borer):
  - Larvae feed within the stalks and leaf petioles of *Pontederia*; young larvae feed on the surface and later instars burrow into leaves or rhizomes.
  - At a site of 97% taro and 3% pickerelweed, larvae damaged **87% of the pickerelweed** [FL/HI-type warm site].
  - Natural populations are "so highly parasitized in the fourth and seventh instar that large populations… seldom occur" [FL; Army Corps report c. 1982, as *Arzama densa*].
  - — [search summary of Wikipedia/IFAS/DTIC](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA125007.pdf) (snippet)
- *Synclita obliteralis* (waterlily leafcutter):
  - Young larvae live in **water-filled** leaf cases and breathe through the cuticle. Older larvae make **air-filled** cases, detach them and float free. Pupae are in silk cocoons inside the cases.
  - Little life-cycle research exists, and no development times are published.
  - Congeners: *S. occidentalis* flies June–September in **2–3 generations**. *S. tinealis* in the lab laid 82 eggs per female, hatching in 5 d at 27 °C, with five instars of 3–4 d each, a ~3-d pupa and adults living 4–5 d.
  - — [UF/IFAS EDIS EENY424](https://edis-news.ifas.ufl.edu/2009/02/eeny424in803-waterlily-leafcutter-synclita-obliteralis-walker-insecta-lepidoptera-crambidae-acentropinae) and [Kinser 1981, J. Lepid. Soc. 35:137](https://images.peabody.yale.edu/lepsoc/jls/1980s/1981/1981-35(2)137-Kinser.pdf) (snippet)
- [FL] *Parapoynx seminealis* lays eggs on the **lower surface of floating leaves**. Neonates bore into the leaf or drop on a silk thread to feed on submersed leaves — [Buckingham 2001, J. Lepid. Soc. 55:111](https://images.peabody.yale.edu/lepsoc/jls/2000s/2001/2001-55(3)111-Buckingham.pdf) (snippet)
- *Parapoynx* larvae cut circular pieces from the edges of broad leaves to cover themselves while feeding — [macroinvertebrates.org](https://www.macroinvertebrates.org/taxa-info/lepidoptera-larva/crambidae/parapoynx) (snippet)
- [Upper Midwest, probably (inf); region not shown in the snippet; cafeteria trials of a semi-aquatic caterpillar] ***Brasenia schreberi* and *Nymphaea odorata* were preferred over 14 other vascular plants**. Preference was unrelated to protein, polyphenolics or toughness — [Dorn, Cronin & Lodge 2001, Oecologia, via search](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65dfbac84c5aef494fe4bed9) (snippet; the caterpillar species was not shown)
- *Nuphar* also carries *Ostrinia penitalis* (leaves and petioles), *Parapoynx maculalis* (submerged and floating leaves) and *P. obscuralis* (floating leaves) — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar*](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)

**Midges and flies in the leaf and petiole**
- *Polypedilum braseniae* (Leathers 1922) larvae "mine the upper surface of floating leaves of *Brasenia schreberi* and *Nymphaea odorata*" — [BugGuide](https://www.bugguide.net/node/view/228910); [Maryland Biodiversity Project](https://www.marylandbiodiversity.com/taxon/20480) (snippet)
- On *Nuphar*:
  - *Polypedilum fallax* and *P. tritum* larvae bore into petioles and are filter feeders.
  - *Tribelos quadripunctatus* bores petioles.
  - *Hydromyza confluens* (Scathophagidae) larvae live in floating leaves.
  - — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar* (HG2009)](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)
- [VT] Leaf-mining midge larvae leave serpentine trails that turn brown and rot; the adults do not bite — [VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) (popular)
- [EU-PL] Within *Nuphar* stands, *Polypedilum nubeculosum*, *Potamothrix bavaricus*, mayflies and Ceratopogonidae were denser than outside. *Chaoborus*, *Tanypus* and *P. hammoniensis* were commoner outside. The *Nuphar* effect was attributed to habitat heterogeneity, substrate stability and food — [Aquatic Ecology 2009, s10452-009-9309-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10452-009-9309-9) (snippet)
- *Cricotopus* and *Hydrellia* on pads: nothing specific found for *Nuphar*/*Nymphaea*/*Brasenia* in the Northeast (gap).

**Sap-suckers and their predators**
- On *Nuphar*: the waterlily aphid *Rhopalosiphum nymphaeae* (aerial and floating leaves), the pond-lily planthopper *Megamelus davisi* and the leafhopper *Draeculacephala inscripta* (emergent leaves) — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar*](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)
- [Asia, India, on water chestnut] Aphid infestation began in the last week of October at **1.35 aphids per leaf** and peaked at **21.95 per leaf** in the fourth week of January (a winter crop in a warm climate) — [Defence Life Sci. J. 5(1):49–53, 2020](https://publications.drdo.gov.in/ojs/index.php/dlsj/article/download/14403/7238) (snippet)
- *Harmonia axyridis* 4th instars and adults are efficient predators of *R. nymphaeae* in functional-response tests — [Insects 2024, 15:633](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4450/15/9/633/xml) (snippet)

**Under the pads**
- [VT] On pad undersides, mites, whirligig beetles and caddisflies lay eggs, snails graze algae, and freshwater sponges, rotifers and bryozoans colonize — [VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world) (popular; no numbers)
- [EU, mesocosms, *Nuphar lutea*] **Submerged leaves carried more invertebrates per unit area than submerged petioles or the undersides of floating leaves**. The authors attribute this to more periphyton on thinner-cuticled, less waxy surfaces — [Freshwater Biology, fwb.12674](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/fwb.12674) (snippet; 403)
- [EU-PL] At high cover (leaf cover set to 10/50/90%), *Nuphar* was a refuge for *Chironomus* larvae against perch and supplied fish with extra epiphytic prey — [Springer chapter 10.1007/978-94-011-5648-6_22](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-011-5648-6_22) (snippet)
- Caddis on *Nuphar*'s submerged leaves, petioles and stems: *Triaenodes* (three spp.), *Nectopsyche exquisita*, *Mystacides sepulchralis*, *Anabolia consocia* — [Illinois Wildflowers, *Nuphar*](https://m.illinoiswildflowers.info/plant_insects/plants/nuphar_lutea.html) (full text)

**Fishing spiders and water striders**
- [AB, three small ponds in central Alberta; 625 prey records]:
  - *Dolomedes triton* took mainly surface-active insects. **Truly aquatic species were about 14%** of the diet.
  - Aquatic and semi-aquatic **Heteroptera (striders, backswimmers etc.) were about 30%** through the season.
  - Diptera and adult Odonata were important, varying by season.
  - Cannibalism made up 5% of observations.
  - — [Zimmermann & Spence 1989, Oecologia 80:187–194, via search](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00240213) (abstract via search summary)
- [MS ponds, via TN enclosures]:
  - Natural density was **1.6 spiders per m²** (unpublished data cited).
  - Bluegill of **43 ± 2.4 mm TL**, at 0.8 fish per m², "can vigorously attack, injure, and consume D. triton".
  - Fish did not change spider survival (P = 0.41) but **reduced spider size** (4.85–4.93 mm vs 5.45–5.50 mm, P = 0.0023).
  - "No prey survived in pools containing sunfish" (tadpoles and crickets offered every 5 days).
  - — [Figiel & Miller 1994, J. Arachnol. 22:185–189, archive.org OCR](https://archive.org/download/biostor-214597/biostor-214597.pdf) (full text)
- [MD, Patuxent ponds with *Nuphar* and *N. odorata* margins]:
  - *D. triton* typically rests "on emergent vegetation with at least one leg contacting the water surface".
  - **47% of 32 nursery webs** were built solely in soft rush *Juncus effusus*, and another 19% in rush plus another plant.
  - Named prey: gerrids, notonectids and odonates; tadpoles and small fish "sometimes".
  - Fish predation on spiders was "documented several times as individuals moved across open water".
  - Females carrying egg sacs go **3–4 weeks without food**.
  - — [Kreiter & Wise 1996, J. Arachnol. 24:24–33, archive.org OCR](https://archive.org/download/biostor-214635/biostor-214635.pdf) (full text)
- [FL, Everglades] Overall adult density was **0.1 per m²**, higher in dense sawgrass with fewer bird and fish predators — [Wetlands, BF03160661, via search](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03160661) (abstract via search summary)
- Water striders:
  - *Gerris insperatus* eats *Galerucella* larvae and pupae (see §1).
  - Lab rearing used **145–155 (low) and 2,900–3,100 (high) first instars per m²** of *G. buenoi* and *G. pingreensis*; high density shortened nymphal development by 4–5 d — [Harada & Spence, Can. Entomol., via search](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-entomologist/article/nymphal-density-and-life-histories-of-two-water-striders-hemiptera-gerridae/02D689CF25E42C358446BAFBBF5FC678) (snippet; lab densities, not field)
  - [BC, Fraser Plateau] each gerrid species was tied to one vegetation-structure habitat type — [Spence & Scudder, Can. Entomol.](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-entomologist/article/habitats-life-cycles-and-guild-structure-among-water-striders-heteroptera-gerridae-on-the-fraser-plateau-of-british-columbia/ADA43CFDF6652F9B3A744E0A2394ED64) (snippet)

**Frogs** (cross-reference): green-frog and bullfrog densities and shore use are in `Maine lake shores and catchment/turtles_and_frogs.md`, about 1 green frog per 1–6 m of vegetated shore. The WXPR piece lists bullfrogs and leopard frogs among lily-bed wildlife — [WXPR 2021](https://www.wxpr.org/post/what-makes-snapping-sound-around-lily-pads-lakes) (popular).

**Fish feeding at and under pads**
- [MA, Lake Waban; underwater cameras] Bluegill used three grazing modes:
  - *active* (biting and pulling pondweed);
  - *passive* (hovering by milfoil, delicate suction);
  - ***surface grazing*: "searching beneath lily pads" with explosive bouts of suction feeding**.
  - Darker, deeper-bodied adults hunted; light fusiform fish fed pelagically.
  - No frequencies were given.
  - — [Postupaka et al. 2021, SICB abstract P22-5](https://sicb.org/?p=42635) (abstract)
- [WI] The "pop" heard among lily pads is most likely sunfish (bluegill, pumpkinseed, redear) sucking prey from under the pads hard enough to "tear a small circular hole in the leaf". This happens mostly at **dawn and dusk**, and the prey are worms, snails and insects — [WXPR, The Masked Biologist, 2021](https://www.wxpr.org/post/what-makes-snapping-sound-around-lily-pads-lakes) (popular; no data)
- [ON, Lake Opinicon, 2,435 pumpkinseed] Diet was chironomid larvae, molluscs and isopods, with fewer amphipods, caddis and mayflies — [QUBS project summary](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/feeding-interrelations-between-agegroups-of-pumpkinseed-lepomis-gibbosus-and-comparisons-with-bruegil-l-mauochirus) (snippet)
- Redbreast sunfish:
  - [AL streams] mainly insectivores, with aquatic and terrestrial insects the bulk of the diet — [SEAFWA 1982](https://seafwa.org/journal/1982/diet-overlap-redbreast-and-longear-sunfishes-small-streams-east-central-alabama) (snippet)
  - Adults take terrestrial insects and immature and adult aquatic insects, especially mayflies and dragonflies — [FishBase](https://fishbase.se/summary/SpeciesSummary.php?ID=3370) (snippet)
- No diet study naming *Galerucella*, *Donacia*, *Bellura*, *Synclita*, *Elophila* or *Parapoynx* in a sunfish, perch, bass, pickerel or minnow was found.

### Inferences
- **Which pad fauna reach fish** (inf), from most to least accessible:
  1. Underside fauna, picked off by sunfish "surface grazing": snails (few in soft water, see the invertebrates note), *Parapoynx/Synclita* cases, caddis, chironomids, *Donacia* egg rows.
  2. Insects falling or crawling off pads: drowned *Galerucella* stages, aphids washed off, *Bellura* larvae swimming ashore in midsummer, *Synclita* free-floating cases.
  3. Spiders and striders crossing open water.
  4. Petiole and blade miners, which fish cannot reach.
- In the sim, pumpkinseed and redbreast foraging at FL cells could use a distinct "under-pad pick" prey pool, active mostly at dawn and dusk. Fallfish and killifish take surface drop-ins at the pad edge.
- **Spiders** (inf): a *Dolomedes* density of **0.1–1.6 per m²** of vegetated margin is the published range (warm southern sites). For a Maine lake with pickerelweed and rush fringes, about 0.2–0.5 per m² of EM/FL edge in mid-summer is a placeholder. About 30% of their prey are striders and backswimmers, and young sunfish eat the spiders.
- ***Bellura*'s swim to shore** is a documented, visible behaviour: red caterpillars over 25 mm porpoising across the surface in midsummer. It is a strong candidate for a scripted surface-prey event in July (inf on timing: one generation in the north, larvae for about 3 weeks after egg-laying in early summer).
- **Leaf-case caterpillars** (*Parapoynx maculalis*) were "very common" on lilies and *Brasenia* in a central-Massachusetts lake. A first brood in July and overwintering young larvae from late August suggest two overlapping cohorts on Maine pads from July to September (inf).

### Gaps
- No density per m² of pad or per g of plant for *Bellura*, *Elophila*, *Synclita*, *Parapoynx*, *Polypedilum* miners, *Hydromyza*, aphids or planthoppers in any northern lake.
- No field density of water striders per m² of pad edge. No *Dolomedes* density from a northern lake.
- No quantitative diet share for any pad-dwelling insect in pumpkinseed, redbreast, perch, bass, pickerel, fallfish or killifish. The Lake Waban video study gives no frequencies.
- Nothing found on *Hydrellia* or *Cricotopus* on lily or watershield pads in the Northeast.

## 4. Emergent stems as emergence sites for dragonfly and damselfly nymphs and as substrate: numbers per stem or per m²

### Takeaway
No count of exuviae **per stem** of pickerelweed or bulrush was found. Published densities are per site or per area:
- about **3.7 exuviae per m²** over a season on a French canal;
- **0–41 exuviae per site** (median below 1) across US lakes;
- about **300 per 100 m** of bank on a Massachusetts river.

Exuviae are concentrated where emergent and shoreline vegetation is abundant, and are absent on bare sand, sea walls and lawns. Stem density shifts emergence timing and height: in sparse cattail, emergence began earlier and larvae climbed higher (18 vs 10 cm).

### Cited Findings
- [EU, southern France, irrigation canal] **2,410 exuviae** from a 325-m stretch sampled 10 times in one season, giving **3.7 exuviae per m²** — [Martinia 2208](https://martinia.insectes.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2208.pdf) (snippet)
- [US lakes]:
  - Mean exuviae per site ranged **0–41**; about **57% of lakes had < 1 per site** and about **10% had > 10**.
  - Mean shoreline tree cover, woody habitat and aquatic plant cover were higher where exuviae occurred.
  - No exuviae were found on bushes, smooth sea walls, sandy beaches or lawns.
  - — [Diversity 2011, 3(2):200](https://www2.mdpi.com/1424-2818/3/2/200) (snippet; authors and region not confirmed)
- [WI] Riparian wetland plant abundance best predicted odonate density, and gomphid exuviae density tracked late-instar larval density — [Remsburg 2007, PhD diss., UW–Madison](https://turnerlab.ibio.wisc.edu/remsburg-a-j-2007-aquatic-and-terrestrial-vegetation-influence-lacustrine-dragonfly-order-odonata-assemblages-at-multiple-life-stages-phd-dissertation-university-of-wisconsin-madison) (snippet)
- [MA, river] "Close to 300 exuviae were recovered from a 100 meter stretch of riverbank"; searching banks for skins was the best way to find rare odonates — [Mass.gov, Appendix E odonate survey](https://www.mass.gov/doc/appendix-e-odonate-survey-report/download) (snippet)
- [EU-ES, pond] Most exuviae were at **6–20 cm** height, all on emergent vegetation — [Notulae Odonatologicae 1995](https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/593562/NOIOS1995004006004.pdf) (snippet). The cattail-density result (*Pachydiplax*: 18/13/10 cm at low/medium/high density; earlier emergence in sparse stands) is in `plant_species_traits.md` §5.
- Abundant shoreline vegetation can mean more spiders, and teneral odonates are more easily damaged by plants moving in the wind — [a shoreline review in the search results; the source is either this page or Diversity 2011, not pinned down](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/449/44950834030.pdf) (snippet)
- [MN, Swan Lake] Comparing *Typha*, *Scirpus acutus*, *Potamogeton* and open water, open water gave the most organisms and *Typha* the largest invertebrate biomass. No per-stem values were seen — [Olson et al. 1993, MN DNR](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/eco/nongame/projects/consgrant_reports/1993/1993_olson_etal.pdf) (snippet)
- [MD] Fishing spiders build nursery webs in emergent rushes (47% in *Juncus effusus* alone) — [Kreiter & Wise 1996](https://archive.org/download/biostor-214635/biostor-214635.pdf) (full text). Emergent stems therefore also host the predator that takes emerging odonates and striders.

### Inferences
- **Sim rule** (inf): emergence sites are EM stems plus the edges of FL pads, which damselflies use. In Maine the emergence window runs late May to July, with a peak in June (Swedish 63°N lakes: May 31–July 19, in the invertebrates note).
- Exuvia density per m of vegetated shore, using the MA river figure: about 3 per m per season where the shore is good. A median lake shore has < 1 per site.
- The French canal's 3.7 per m² is a reasonable upper value for a well-vegetated EM fringe. Most emerging nymphs climb 5–50 cm, mostly 6–20 cm.
- During emergence, nymphs crawling to stems at dawn are prey for bass, perch and sunfish at the EM edge, and tenerals are prey for spiders, birds and frogs.
- Exuviae on a stem are a "sign" the renderer could show.

### Gaps
- No exuviae-per-stem count for pickerelweed or bulrush. No northeastern-lake per-m² emergence total.
- No macroinvertebrate density per bulrush or pickerelweed stem in a North American lake.

## 5. Biomass and production of the pad-dwelling invertebrate community vs submerged beds; invertebrate density on the undersides of lily pads

### Takeaway
Per gram of plant, floating-leaved plants carry the **fewest** invertebrates of the growth forms:
- the lowest of three architectures in a Virginia thesis;
- 5,931 per m² on *N. odorata* against 12,855 per m² on *Hydrilla* in Lake Seminole (both in the plant note).

On *Nuphar* itself, **submerged leaves carry more per unit area than petioles or floating-leaf undersides**. The one production figure for pad herbivores is the Georgia *Galerucella* larvae: 0.28 g AFDM per m² of leaf standing stock and 27 g m⁻² yr⁻¹, multivoltine. In Finland the beetles ate only 3–6% of annual floating-leaf production. **No per-cm² or per-leaf density of the underside fauna was found** for any northern lake.

### Cited Findings
- [VA] Floating-leaved plants had **the lowest invertebrate density per g DW** of three architecture types. Dissected milfoils had the highest; *Stylaria* and *Hyalella* dominated the multivariate patterns — [Rielly 2009, VCU MS thesis, abstract](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/etd/5604) (abstract; PDF 403)
- [FL/GA, Lake Seminole] Density was lowest on *Nymphaea odorata* (**5,931/m²**) and highest on *Hydrilla* (12,855/m²) — [ERDC record](https://erdc-library.erdc.dren.mil/jspui/handle/11681/6399) (snippet; the page is behind a browser-verification screen; already in `plant_species_traits.md`)
- [EU, *Nuphar* mesocosms] Submerged leaves > submerged petioles ≈ undersides of floating leaves, per unit area — [fwb.12674](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/fwb.12674) (snippet)
- [EU-PL] Kornijów & Ścibior 1999, "Seasonal changes in macrofaunal feeding groups associated with *Nuphar lutea* leaves in a small eutrophic lake", Pol. J. Ecol. 47:135–143. This is the one dedicated study of *Nuphar* leaf fauna by feeding group — [Lublin record](https://intranet.up.lublin.pl/Uczelnia/Publiczna/Wyszukiwarka/PublikacjaDetail/108576?profilId=0) (record only; the RCIN full text is behind browser verification)
- [GA] *Galerucella* larval standing stock **283.6 mg AFDM per m² of leaf**; production **27.14 g m⁻² yr⁻¹** — [Wallace & O'Hop 1985](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65dfd4047c7033e84bef196e) (abstract; earlier note)
- [EU-FI] *Galerucella* consumption **3.0–6.1%** of net annual floating-leaf production — [Oecologia BF00317528](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00317528) (snippet)
- [EU-PL] Most sediment macroinvertebrates were denser **within** *Nuphar* stands than outside — [Aquatic Ecology 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10452-009-9309-9) (snippet)
- [EU-PL] Dense *Nuphar* (90% cover) was a refuge for *Chironomus* from perch and added epiphytic prey — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-011-5648-6_22) (snippet)

### Inferences
- **Per m² of FL bed** (inf). Combine:
  - the plant note's per-gram factor (lily blades 0.3–0.4 of a broad-leaved pondweed);
  - the Lake Seminole ratio (FL about 0.45 of a dense submerged bed per m²);
  - the mesocosm result (undersides poorer than submerged leaves).

  A Maine FL bed then carries roughly **0.3–0.5× the epiphytic invertebrate biomass per m² of a submerged pondweed bed**. Most of it sits on the submerged petioles and submerged leaves (*Nuphar* has submerged "cabbage" leaves), not under the floating blades.
- **Herbivore production** (inf). If Maine *Galerucella* is univoltine with a small second brood, its production is perhaps 1/3–1/5 of the Georgia multivoltine value, of order **5–10 g AFDM m⁻² of leaf yr⁻¹** at heavy infestation and much less in a typical bed. This is an order-of-magnitude guess to tune against the Finnish 3–6% consumption figure, not a measurement.
- **Under-pad density** (inf). With no per-cm² data, keep the sim's per-gram approach (from the invertebrates note), applied to FL biomass with the reduced factor. Add the herbivore guild (*Galerucella* + *Donacia* adults + caterpillars + miners) as a separate small pool that eats 3–6% of FL leaf production a year.

### Gaps
- No per-cm² or per-leaf count of underside fauna on *Nuphar*, *Nymphaea* or *Brasenia* anywhere in North America. Kornijów & Ścibior 1999 (Poland) and the *Nuphar* mesocosm paper (fwb.12674) likely have per-area values but were unreadable.

## 6. Seasonal timing in a Maine-like climate (ice-out late April, 24 °C peak in late July, ice-on about Dec 1)

### Takeaway
The pad fauna is a **June–September** community tied to leaf appearance. Overwintering stages:
- *Galerucella* adults in shore litter;
- *Donacia* larvae, and new adults in cocoons, on the rhizomes;
- *Elophila*, *Parapoynx* and *Bellura* larvae, half- or full-grown, in petioles, cases or debris.

At northern latitudes most of these insects have **one generation a year**: *Galerucella* (partial second), *Elophila gyralis* (Michigan), *Bellura gortynoides* (north). Only small *Synclita* congeners reach 2–3 generations. Odonate emergence from emergent stems runs late May to July.

### Cited Findings
- *Galerucella*:
  - eggs in June [VT] ([VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world), popular);
  - an early fraction of new adults by mid-June breeds again, but most summer-generation adults diapause [NY] ([Tauber et al. 1996](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df8b847c7033e84bee10cf), abstract);
  - new adults from July and pupae July to late September [EU-UK] ([ukbeetles via search](https://www.ukbeetles.co.uk/galerucella-nymphaea-sagittariae), snippet);
  - diapause is induced by short days and low temperature (Tauber et al. 1996).
- *Donacia*: eggs hatch in about 10 days. The adult stays in the cocoon over winter and emerges "at the beginning of the next summer" [WI] ([UWM](https://uwm.edu/field-station/bug-of-the-week/waterlily-leaf-beetle/), popular). Adults float up in spring [VT] ([VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world)). Larvae overwinter in 1st/2nd and then 5th instars over 2–3 years [EU-UK] ([coleoptera.org.uk](https://coleoptera.org.uk/node?page=937), snippet).
- *Parapoynx* (*Nymphula*) *maculalis*: first brood July; young larvae late August; egg about 10 days [MA] ([Forbes 1910](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/psyche/17/17-219.html), full text).
- *Elophila gyralis*: univoltine in Michigan; half-grown larvae overwinter; eggs hatch in about 12 d; larvae spend 10–14 d on the leaf before boring the petiole ([UWM 2025](https://uwm.edu/field-station/bug-of-the-week/waterlily-borer-moth/), popular).
- *Bellura gortynoides*: adults May–September; one generation in the north; overwinters as mature larvae ([BugGuide via search](https://www.bugguide.net/node/view/83121), snippet). The VCE moth (probably *Bellura*) grows for about 3 weeks, then swims ashore to pupate ([VCE](https://vtecostudies.org/blog/a-water-lilys-world)).
- *Synclita*: *S. occidentalis* flies June–September in 2–3 generations ([search summary](https://worldspecies.org/ntaxa/1390972), snippet).
- *Dolomedes triton*: females carry egg sacs for 3–4 weeks without feeding, and nursery webs are in rushes [MD] ([Kreiter & Wise 1996](https://archive.org/download/biostor-214635/biostor-214635.pdf), full text).
- Lily leaves appear late April to early June, limited by temperature, and live 38–48 d on average [EU] (`plant_species_traits.md`, [PMC5410161](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5410161/)). Odonates emerge May 31 to July 19 at 63°N in Sweden (`littoral_invertebrates.md`).

### Inferences

| Period (Maine) | yf (approx.) | What is on the pads and stems |
|---|---|---|
| Ice-out to mid-May | 0.15–0.21 | No floating leaves yet. *Donacia* larvae and cocoons on rhizomes; *Elophila* and *Bellura* larvae resume feeding in petiole bases and submerged leaves; spiders on the shore margin |

| August | 0.42–0.50 | New *Galerucella* adults (mostly diapausing); partial second brood; *Parapoynx* young larvae; *Donacia* pupate on roots |

- Physiology hooks (inf): Tauber's degree-day models apply to eggs and pupae, so in the sim egg and pupal durations can be degree-day driven, larval durations food- and leaf-limited, and the date of diapause entry photoperiod-driven (short days).

### Gaps
- No Maine or New England calendar of first appearance, peak and departure for any of these insects. The dates above come from VT, MA, NY, MI, WI and the UK.
- No degree-day thresholds for *Galerucella* eggs and pupae (only in the Tauber full text). No development times for *Donacia* larvae or for the moths at northern temperatures.
- Unknown whether eastern-Maine *Galerucella* ever completes a second generation in a cool summer.
