## Q1 — Utricularia capture rate: prey per bladder/day, per g DW/day, per m²/day; bladder density; reset time; prey types and sizes

### Takeaway
No source gives a direct field capture rate per bladder per day for temperate aquatic *Utricularia*. Rates have to be assembled from four pieces:
- how many traps a plant carries per unit mass;
- how long a trap stays active (about 10–19 days, with most capture in the first 1–6 days);
- what fraction of traps hold animal prey in the field (about 1% in oligotrophic marshes, about 1 animal per trap in Austrian lakes and ponds, 19–55% in prey-rich cultures);
- the nutrient content per prey item (about 0.8–2.7 µg N and 0.11–0.25 µg P per copepod or ostracod).

Prey are mostly littoral cladocerans, ostracods and cyclopoid copepods of about 0.5–1.5 mm. Rotifers and algae are caught too, partly by accident.

### Cited Findings
**Trap turnover, trap age and reset**
- *U. vulgaris* at Wicken Fen, England, produced new leaf whorls, each bearing many traps, at about 1.4–2.8 per day from April to September. Leaves lived less than 50 days. Trapping efficiency peaked in bladders about 1–6 days old and then fell quickly. Very few traps older than 19 days could capture, and most were lost within 32 days. [AB/SN] — [Friday 1989, Oecologia 80:272](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00380163)
- Restated: "an individual utricle is able to trap for only 10–19 days of age … its trapping efficiency declines markedly after the fourth to sixth day". Most utricles complete their life cycle in 30 days or less. [FT, 2° for Friday] — [Sirová et al. 2009, Aquat. Bot. 90:129](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)
- After firing, the trap pumps out about 40% of its lumen water and is ready to fire again in about 30 minutes (Sydenham & Findlay 1975). Any mechanical irritation can fire a trap, so "prey" includes detritus, algae and bacteria. [FT, 2°] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)
- Traps are 1–4 mm long, hollow, mostly two cells thick, and water-filled. [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf); [Adamec 2006, Plant Biol. 8:765](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Utr_Resp.pdf)
- Apical shoot growth is 1–4.2 new leaf nodes per day, and senescent bases decay at the same rate (the "conveyer belt"). Biomass doubling time is 5–20 days. [FT] — [Adamec 2015, Phyton 55:131](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf)
- A model in [Adamec 2016, Fundam. Appl. Limnol. (FAL), DOI 10.1127/fal/2016/0780](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf) uses a doubling time of 15 days, i.e. 4.7% biomass increase per day, a DW:FW ratio of 8%, and shoot K of 3% DW. [FT]

**How much of the plant is trap**
- Bladders make up about 10–50% of total biomass in aquatic *Utricularia* (abstract). The text gives 10–55% of DW in species with homogeneous shoots, citing Friday 1992, Knight 1992, Richards 2001 and Englund & Harms 2003. [FT] — [Adamec 2006](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Utr_Resp.pdf)
- *U. vulgaris* in the field (Friday 1992) had a mean investment in carnivory of 49.7 ± 1.1% of DW, constant through the season. Four plants with the same investment had 3.3–14.4 traps per mg of leaf-node DW and a mean trap DW of 93–365 µg. The µ symbol is lost in the text extraction; µg is the only plausible unit. [FT, 2°] — [Adamec 2015](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf)
- *U. australis* had a mean of 40–70 traps per mg of leaf-node DW in a greenhouse experiment. In a field survey of 17 sites (Adamec 2009) the range was 9–67, with a mean of 26.4 ± 3.8. [FT] — [Adamec 2015](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf)
- *U. australis* in an 11-day greenhouse experiment (Adamec, Sirová & Vrba 2010) [FT] — [FAL 176:153](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf):
  - investment in carnivory was 52–63% of DW;
  - mean trap DW fell from 60.7 to about 27–37 µg;
  - maximum trap length was 3.1–3.7 mm.
- *U. purpurea* in the Everglades put an average of 26% of its biomass into bladders. [AB/SN] — [Richards 2001, Am. J. Bot. 88:170](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub80237)
- A typical 2-m *U. foliosa* plant carries about 80,000 traps. [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)

**Share of traps holding prey (field vs culture)**
- In the field-grown *U. foliosa* and *U. purpurea* (oligotrophic carbonate marsh, Belize), only 0.9% and 1.4% of traps held larger zooplankton prey. [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)
- Eight Austrian populations of *U. australis*, *U. vulgaris* and *U. minor* (peat bogs, lakes, ponds; May–June) [FT] — [Koller-Peroutka et al. 2015, Ann. Bot. 115:227](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551088):
  - 1,844 traps held 20,856 objects, i.e. 11.3 per trap;
  - the objects were 50.3% algae, 33.6% pollen and 9.2% animals;
  - animals came to about 1.0 per trap;
  - 33.4% of traps held algae but no animals;
  - population means ran from 1.7 objects per trap (*U. australis*, Postteich) to 31.1 per trap (*U. vulgaris*, Seetaler Lake, about 80% pollen).
- In the outdoor culture before the Adamec et al. 2010 experiment, only about 0.5% of *U. australis* traps held prey, against 18.5% of *Aldrovanda* traps. "Natural prey capture" is described as much lower than the 19% of mature traps with prey reached in fed cultures. [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- In fed cultures, about 33–35% of traps on leaf nodes 6–12 held prey after 10–11 days. More than 50% held prey on nodes 19–20. [FT] — [Adamec 2016](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf)

**Absolute capture numbers**
- In a prey-rich container, one *U. australis* shoot of about 15–20 cm captured an estimated 314 diaptomids (*Eudiaptomus* sp., 1.4 mm) in 8 days, about 39 per shoot per day. One *Aldrovanda* shoot captured 66.6 ostracods of about 1.2 mm. [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- A Society for Integrative and Comparative Biology (SICB) conference abstract on *U. vulgaris* fed ostracods [SN, not fetched; conference abstract] — [sicb.org/?p=11364](https://sicb.org/?p=11364), [sicb.org/?p=11442](https://sicb.org/?p=11442):
  - capture rates were "up to 5 per day" (unit per trap or per plant unclear), against more than 5 per hour for first-feeding fish;
  - mature traps succeeded on 30–40% of suction events;
  - traps matured in about 10 days;
  - in the laboratory, prey density declined as a first-order (exponential) process.
- Hall, Berg & Muller (SICB, about 2016) frame capture efficiency as captures per strike, measured from acoustic recordings, but give no numbers. [AB] — [sicb.org/?p=15248](https://sicb.org/?p=15248)
- *U. australis* caught *Ceriodaphnia dubia* within about 9 ms, at suction speeds up to 4 m/s and accelerations up to 2,800 g. [SN] — search summary of [SICB/biomechanics literature](https://sicb.org/?p=6435)

**Nutrient content per prey item (useful for converting zooplankton biomass to plant N and P)**
- Ostracod (about 1.2 mm): 2.74 µg N, 0.245 µg P, 3.08 µg K and 1.14 µg Mg per individual. *Eudiaptomus* sp. (1.4 mm): 0.785 µg N, 0.111 µg P, 1.19 µg K and 0.208 µg Mg per individual. [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- Ostracod respiration is 9.3 nmol O₂ per individual per hour; *Diaptomus* is 8.5. [FT] — [Adamec 2006](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Utr_Resp.pdf)

**Prey types and selectivity**
- In two northern Michigan lakes (2020, stratified season), bladderwort prey were mostly littoral cladocerans and ostracods [SN, abstract via search; direct fetch 403] — [Diversity 2023, 15(2):171, "Bladderwort Relationship to Zooplankton in Two Northern Michigan Lakes"](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-2818/15/2/171):
  - ostracods dominated early in the season and cladocerans later;
  - ostracods were over-represented in traps relative to the water column, and cladocerans under-represented;
  - larger traps caught larger prey, but trap size did not change the number of prey or of taxa.
- *U. vulgaris* selects cyclopoid copepods over the cladoceran *Polyphemus* (Harms 1999; Harms & Johansson 2000). [FT, 2°] — [Solís-Parra & Críales-Hernández 2016, Rev. Biol. Trop. 64:1297](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/449/44946472030.pdf)
- *U. foliosa* in a Colombian swamp showed significant positive selection (Savage's index, p < 0.05) for *Lecane* (a rotifer), *Alona*, *Ceriodaphnia* and *Bosmina*, although *Brachionus*, *Keratella* and *Polyarthra* dominated the water (*Brachionus* at 70–460 ind/L). [FT] — [Solís-Parra & Críales-Hernández 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/449/44946472030.pdf)
- In a French Guiana pond, *U. gibba* and a second species appeared to prey strongly on cladocerans and copepods, especially large individuals, although rotifers and gastrotrichs were far more abundant. The two species partitioned prey by trap size and habitat. [SN; PDF 403] — [Guiral & Rougier 2007, Ann. Limnol. 43:147](https://www.limnology-journal.org/10.1051/limn:2007009)
- A preprint found that daphniids keep further from predatory *U. vulgaris* than from a non-predatory plant, so the plant also has a behavioural effect on its prey. [SN] — [EcoEvoRxiv 14278](https://ecoevorxiv.org/repository/view/14278/)
- The general prey list covers cladocerans, copepods, ostracods, mites, nematodes, rotifers and protozoa. [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)

**Trap costs (for the plant's carbon budget)**
- Measured in six aquatic species at 20 °C [FT] — [Adamec 2006](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Utr_Resp.pdf):
  - bladder dark respiration was 5.1–8.6 mmol O₂ kg⁻¹ FW h⁻¹, 75–200% higher than leaves (1.7–6.1);
  - net photosynthesis in leaves (40–117 mmol kg⁻¹ FW h⁻¹) was 7–10 times that of bladders;
  - empty and fed bladders respired at the same rate.
- Knight (1992; *U. macrorhiza*, Wisconsin) found bladder net photosynthesis at 41–67% of leaf rates and bladder respiration about 10% above leaves. [FT, 2°] — [Adamec 2006](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Utr_Resp.pdf)

### Inferences
These are mine, not from the sources.

**Traps per unit biomass**
- If traps are about 50% of DW and weigh about 30–365 µg DW each, a stand has roughly 1,400–17,000 traps per g plant DW. That fits the 3.3–70 traps per mg leaf-node DW range.
- A Hot Brook stand of 30–80 g DW/m² would then carry about 4×10⁴ to 1.3×10⁶ traps/m².

**Field capture per trap per day**
- In Austria, about 1 animal per trap accumulated over a trap's roughly 15-day active life, i.e. about 0.07 animals per trap per day.
- In Belize, about 1% of traps held prey, i.e. about 0.001 per trap per day.

**Capture per stand**
- Those per-trap rates give about 1.4–1,200 animals per g DW per day, and about 40 to about 90,000 animals per m² per day for a 30–80 g/m² stand.
- That is a two-to-three-order-of-magnitude uncertainty. It is driven mainly by prey density and by the fact that *U. purpurea* hardly hunts.

**Suggested model form**
- Make capture first-order in local zooplankton concentration, as the SICB laboratory result suggests:
  - capture (µg prey per m² per day) = k × B_utric (g DW/m²) × Z (µg/L);
  - weight k by selectivity, high for ostracods, chydorid and bosminid cladocerans and cyclopoids, low for rotifers;
  - give daphniids a reduced encounter rate because they avoid the plant.
- Calibrate k so that in summer littoral water it reproduces something between the Austrian and Michigan-style field numbers.
- Scale k down for the *U. purpurea* share of the combined species.
- *U. intermedia*'s colourless carnivorous shoots sit in the bed or litter, so its prey should be weighted toward benthic ostracods and chydorids. This is from its shoot morphology (see Q2 notes), not from a measured diet.

**Converting captured prey to plant N and P**
- Use about 0.8–2.7 µg N and 0.11–0.25 µg P per crustacean item. The mass N:P is about 7–11.

### Gaps
- I found no published field estimate of prey per bladder per day for *U. vulgaris*/*macrorhiza*, *U. intermedia* or *U. purpurea*.
  - Englund & Harms 2001 (Oikos 94:175, the functional response to swarming zooplankton) and Harms 2002 (Freshw. Biol. 47:1608, the effect of predation on microcrustaceans) almost certainly hold the best rate data, but I could not reach their abstracts or texts.
  - Friday 1989 may contain counts of prey per trap by age; only the abstract-level summary was accessible.
- No number for bladders per m² in a natural stand. Friday 1992 and Knight & Frost 1991 measured bladders per leaf, not per area.
- No prey dry mass per item. Only N, P, K and Mg per item (above) were found; the C content of prey is unsourced here.
- The "up to 5 per day" SICB figure is a snippet from a conference abstract with an unclear unit.
- *U. intermedia*-specific prey data were not found.

## Q2 — Share of plant N and P from prey; dependence on water nutrient status; growth with vs without prey

### Takeaway
For N, isotope and budget studies agree that prey can supply a large share in nutrient-poor water. Estimates:
- about 40–100% (Australian natural-abundance δ¹⁵N mixing models);
- up to 75% of the seasonal N gain (Knight's Wisconsin *U. macrorhiza*);
- about 68% of growth N in fed cultures;
- an uptake efficiency of about 76–83% of prey N (Friday & Quarmby's ¹⁵N work).

For P, from-prey shares for aquatic *Utricularia* are essentially unmeasured. Trap-fluid stoichiometry (molar N:P about 100) points to P, not N, as the limiting element that traps and their microbes help supply.

Plant investment in traps falls as water P rises (Kibriya & Jones) and as NH₄⁺/DOC rise (Kurosawa et al.). It rises with free CO₂, which tea-coloured lakes tend to have. Growth responses to prey in *Utricularia* are modest and condition-dependent: +9% to +73% in apical growth rate, and often no significant change in biomass.

### Cited Findings
**N from prey and its efficiency**
- Friday & Quarmby (1994, New Phytol. 126:273, prey-derived ¹⁵N and ³²P in *U. vulgaris*) found 83% efficiency of N uptake from young mosquito larvae. The review calls the nutrient uptake efficiency from prey "almost unknown" otherwise; this is the one rare example. [FT, 2°] — [Adamec 2016](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf)
- A model budget assumed 76% N uptake efficiency from prey (Dixon et al. 1980; Friday & Quarmby 1994) and 90% for P, K and Mg (Adamec 2002). [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- The same study found that fed *U. australis* could cover about 68% of its growth N from prey; Knight (1988, PhD, U. Wisconsin) estimated that field-grown *U. macrorhiza* could compensate up to 75% of its seasonal N gain from prey. Fed *Aldrovanda* covered 52% of N, 42% of P, 78% of K and 100% of Mg from prey. [FT, 2° for Knight] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- In fed plants, shoot N rose by 42–47%. Shoot P was unchanged in *U. australis*, while it rose 74% in *Aldrovanda*. Shoot N ranged about 1.7–2.4% of DW and P about 0.18–0.32% of DW. Which column belongs to which species is uncertain in the PDF text extraction. [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- Kurosawa, Wells, Gibson, Lyons, Kesseli & Oakes (2024, Ann. Bot.) studied *U. australis*, *U. gibba* and *U. uliginosa* in 11 eastern Australian wetlands along a 794-km gradient, using natural-abundance δ¹⁵N mixing models. [SN] — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10554989-eat-eat-novel-stable-isotope-models-reveal-shift-carnivory-nutrient-availability-aquatic-utricularia-spp); [SCU research portal](https://researchportal.scu.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/To-eat-or-not-to-eat/991013214013702368)
  - Carnivory supplied 40–100% of plant N.
  - Carnivory was lowest where water NH₄⁺ and DOC were highest.
- In Austrian *Utricularia* (Koller-Peroutka et al. 2015) [FT] — [PMC4551088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551088):
  - the standard δ¹⁵N formula gave mean prey-derived N of 71 ± 18% (*U. vulgaris*), 68 ± 26% (*U. minor*) and 120 ± 86% (*U. australis*, up to 383%);
  - the authors state the formula "was no longer applicable" where algae dominated the catch;
  - algae explained 37% of the δ¹⁵N variance (R² 0.37) in *U. australis*.

**P and trap microbes**
- The trap fluid of traps without prey accumulates about 100 mg/L of C and N and 0.2–0.6 mg/L of P over a trap's life. [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)
  - The trap-fluid molar N:P is about 100, and the microbial cells' N:P is 25–61. Both indicate P, rather than N, limits *Utricularia*.
  - Bacteria are more than 58% of viable microbial biomass and outnumber those in the ambient water by 1–3 orders of magnitude.
- The P uptake efficiency from prey is unknown for aquatic carnivorous plants. [FT] — [Adamec 2016](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf)

**Uptake from the water and re-use of old tissue**
- Shoots take up nutrients from water as well [FT] — [Adamec 2016](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf):
  - NH₄⁺ at about 1 mmol kg⁻¹ FW h⁻¹, with nearly zero NO₃⁻ uptake (NH₄⁺ is strongly preferred);
  - phosphate at 26–91 µmol kg⁻¹ FW h⁻¹;
  - uptake affinity reaches at least 0.4 µM for NH₄⁺ and 0.1 µM for phosphate.
- N and P are efficiently re-used from senescent shoots (re-utilisation about 28–62% in that study's Table 4). K is not re-used.

**Water nutrient status and investment in traps**
- *U. macrorhiza* in Wisconsin lakes [AB] — [Knight & Frost 1991, Ecology 72:728](https://lternet.edu/biblio/bladder-control-in-utricularia-macrorhiza-lake-specific-variation-in-plant-investment-in-carnivory):
  - bladders per leaf varied significantly among lakes but were consistent within a lake;
  - bladder number correlated positively, not negatively as predicted, with specific conductance;
  - reciprocal transplants changed bladder number on new leaves within weeks;
  - prey manipulation suggested water chemistry, not prey supply, drives bladder number.
- Kibriya & Jones 2007 (Freshw. Biol. 52:500), N × P additions to *U. vulgaris* [SN/AB] — [NORA record](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/5436):
  - investment in traps fell with P loading;
  - N had no effect;
  - growth and periphyton were unchanged;
  - a reanalysis of published data also showed investment falling as P availability rose.
- Adamec 2015 (*U. vulgaris*, *U. australis*, *U. reflexa*) [FT] — [Adamec 2015](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf):
  - CO₂ addition (0.30–0.58 vs 0.024–0.062 mM) raised mean trap DW 2.7–249-fold and increased investment in carnivory in all species, much more than prey addition did;
  - investment correlated negatively with shoot N and P;
  - at very low CO₂ (below about 0.02–0.03 mM) or in deep shade, traps are not produced;
  - shaded *U. vulgaris* fed prey made no traps (Englund & Harms 2003).
- Aquatic *Utricularia* typically grow in shallow, humic, slightly acidic, oligo- to mesotrophic waters. These are high in free CO₂, with NH₄⁺ well above NO₃⁻, and K can be as low as 0.01 mg/L. [FT] — [Adamec 2016](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2016.pdf); [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)
- In the Austrian populations, trap density was higher in more nutrient-rich water, and the authors conclude the benefit of prey is greater in mesotrophic than oligotrophic waters. In nutrient-poor water algae made up more of the catch. [FT] — [Koller-Peroutka et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551088)
- The importance of carnivory has been disputed for species in very nutrient-poor water, where zooplankton is scarce (Bern 1997; Richards 2001). [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf)
  - *U. purpurea* bladders "capture few aquatic microinvertebrates". Almost all mature bladders hold living algae, zooplankton and debris; this is interpreted as mutualism rather than predation. [AB/SN] — [Richards 2001](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub80237)

**Growth with vs without prey**
- *U. australis*, 11 days at about 26 °C in oligotrophic water (NH₄-N 0–21 µg/L, PO₄-P 9–12 µg/L) [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf):
  - no significant biomass effect from prey (doubling time 5.2 days fed vs 4.7 days unfed);
  - main shoots were longer, with more nodes;
  - apical growth was about 9% faster when fed (3.99 nodes/day);
  - *Aldrovanda* biomass rose 60% (doubling time 6.4 vs 10.6 days).
- Prey raised apical growth by 11–73% across three species; CO₂ raised it by 22–94%. The two effects were additive and independent. [FT] — [Adamec 2015](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf)
- Growth responses to prey in aquatic *Utricularia* are "rather ambiguous". They depend on CO₂, mineral nutrients, light and temperature (Sorenson & Jackson 1968; Knight & Frost 1991; Kosiba 1992; Jobson et al. 2000; Englund & Harms 2003; Adamec 2008b). [FT] — [Adamec et al. 2010](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/FAL2010A.pdf)

### Inferences
These are mine.

**Prey N share for the model**
- For Hot Brook's soft, tea-coloured (humic) water, a defensible default is that prey supply about 50% of new N, within a range of 30–75%.
- Taper it toward 30% when littoral zooplankton is scarce (spring, after ice-out) and toward 75% at the summer peak.
- Keep the sim's closed mass budget: N and P in captured zooplankton move from the zooplankton fields into the plant, at about 80% N efficiency.
- The 20% that is not absorbed should go to the bed's organic/detritus pool, not vanish.

**P from prey**
- No measurement exists. I would assume a P efficiency similar to N's (about 80–90%). Treat trap microbes and periphyton as an additional P source; Sirová and Richards suggest this, but it is not quantified.

**Investment in traps**
- Make it respond to water P (higher dissolved P → fewer traps) and to light/CO₂ (shade → no traps).
- Humic lakes have high free CO₂, which per Adamec 2015 favours investment in traps.

**Prey needed per m²**
- At a field-realistic net growth of about 1–3% per day, 55 g DW/m² and about 2% N in DW, the plant takes up about 11–33 mg N/m²/day.
- If half of that comes from prey, the plant needs about 7,000–21,000 copepod-sized items per m² per day (at 0.785 µg N each), or about 2,000–6,000 ostracods.
- That falls inside the capture range inferred in Q1, so the two independent routes agree on order of magnitude.

### Gaps
- I could not reach the Friday & Quarmby 1994 original. Its exact share of prey-derived N or P in field plants, and its ³²P results, are unknown.
- The often-quoted "52% of N from prey" for *U. vulgaris* could not be found in any source and should not be used.
- Knight 1992 results: only the study set-up was accessible.
- No P-from-prey percentage exists for any aquatic *Utricularia*.
- No North American soft-water or humic-lake isotope study of *U. vulgaris*/*macrorhiza*, *U. intermedia* or *U. purpurea* was found.

## Q3 — Could a dense Utricularia stand measurably deplete littoral zooplankton?

### Takeaway
No whole-stand field estimate was found. The direct studies (Harms 2002; Englund & Harms 2001; Kurbatova & Yershov 2009) could not be accessed.

The indirect evidence is mixed:
- In culture, prey density declines exponentially under bladderwort predation (SICB laboratory result). Prey are size- and taxon-selective, and the Michigan prey spectra differ from the water column.
- Field trap contents are often dominated by algae and pollen, and in oligotrophic marshes only about 1% of traps hold animals.

Order-of-magnitude arithmetic (Q1/Q2) says a dense, actively hunting *U. vulgaris*-type stand could remove a meaningful daily fraction of littoral crustaceans. A *U. purpurea*-dominated stand probably would not.

### Cited Findings
- Bladderworts are described as zooplankton predators in littoral zones and shallow lakes from tropical to arctic, oligotrophic to eutrophic. Littoral zooplankton face different predators than pelagic zooplankton, and more data on predator effects are needed. [SN; 403] — [Diversity 2023, 15:171 (two northern Michigan lakes)](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-2818/15/2/171)
- Harms 2002, "The effect of bladderwort (*Utricularia*) predation on microcrustacean prey" (Freshw. Biol. 47(9):1608–1617), and Englund & Harms 2001 (Oikos 94:175–181) are the experimental studies on the topic. Most work is experimental, and the plant is a sit-and-wait predator (Harms 1999, 2002). [FT, citations only] — [Solís-Parra & Críales-Hernández 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/449/44946472030.pdf)
- In the laboratory, *U. vulgaris* capture behaved as a first-order reaction, with prey density declining exponentially over time. [SN, conference abstract] — [sicb.org/?p=11364](https://sicb.org/?p=11364)
- Selective predation by two coexisting bladderworts on large cladocerans and copepods is discussed as affecting the pond's trophic organisation. [SN] — [Guiral & Rougier 2007](https://www.limnology-journal.org/10.1051/limn:2007009)
- Daphniids avoid predatory *U. vulgaris*, a non-consumptive effect. [SN] — [EcoEvoRxiv 14278](https://ecoevorxiv.org/repository/view/14278/)
- *Utricularia* is described as sitting in a closed feedback loop with its prey and the epiphytic algae that colonise it (Englund & Harms 2003). [SN, secondary] — search summary citing [Adamec 2015](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Phyton2015.pdf)
- Counter-evidence for weak impact: in oligotrophic Belize marshes only 0.9–1.4% of traps held animal prey [FT] — [Sirová et al. 2009](https://www.butbn.cas.cz/adamec/Sirova09.pdf). Only about 9% of Austrian trap objects were animals [FT] — [Koller-Peroutka et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4551088).

### Inferences
These are mine.

**Size of the effect**
- With about 4×10⁴ to 1.3×10⁶ traps/m² and a field capture of 0.001–0.07 per trap per day, removal is about 10^1.6 to 10^5 animals per m² per day.
- A littoral column 1 m deep holding 10–100 crustaceans per L contains 10⁴–10⁵ per m².
- So daily removal could range from well under 1% to tens of percent of the standing stock. The high end needs a dense *U. vulgaris*/*macrorhiza* stand with high prey density.
- The first-order form self-limits: as Z falls, capture falls. That makes it safe in the sim. It cannot drive the fields negative if it is implemented as an exponential decay, Z × (1 − e^(−k·B·dt)).

**Spatial structure**
- Depletion should be local, within the stand's cells and their immediate water.
- The sim's field resolution (0.3-m cells) is coarse, so apply the removal to the zooplankton biomass of the cell the stand occupies, weighted toward the bottom-most wet layer for *U. intermedia*.

**What would show it**
- A detectable signature would be the cladoceran:copepod ratio, or ostracod depletion, inside dense stands. It is a plausible "emergent" diagnostic.
- Fish would also see lower littoral crustacean densities inside bladderwort beds, a small competitive effect on young-of-year fish.

### Gaps
- No field measurement of zooplankton density inside vs outside *Utricularia* stands was accessible, and no clearance rate (L per g DW per day) was found.
- Harms 2002, Englund & Harms 2001, and Kurbatova & Yershov 2009 (Inland Water Biol., crustaceans and rotifers in *Utricularia* feeding) were unreachable.
- Littoral zooplankton densities for Hot Brook itself are not covered here; see `docs/ECOLOGY_RESEARCH.md`.

## Q4 — Isoetid radial O₂ loss (ROL) to sediment: rate per m², oxidised layer depth, diel pattern

### Takeaway
*Lobelia dortmanna* sends almost all of its photosynthetic O₂ (>97%) out through its roots. *Littorella* sends less, because its leaf cuticle is more permeable.

A dense Swedish *Lobelia* lawn has about 90 g DW/m², 28,000 roots/m² and 3.6 m² of root surface per m². It photosynthesises 1–2 mg O₂ per g DW per hour at light saturation. That implies roughly 90–180 mg O₂ m⁻² h⁻¹ delivered to the rhizosphere at midday, or about 0.7–2 g O₂ m⁻² d⁻¹ (my inference, gross). This is consistent with a lawn's net carbon uptake of about 0.7 g C m⁻² d⁻¹, whose O₂ does not show up in the water.

Oxic sediment reaches about 4.3 cm under *Lobelia* and 3.0 cm under *Littorella* in low-organic sand. Under *Potamogeton* it reaches only 1.6–2.2 cm. Porewater O₂ swings strongly between day and night.

### Cited Findings
**Lobelia dortmanna (Sand-Jensen, Pedersen, Binzer & Borum 2005, Ann. Bot. 96:613; Lake Skånes Värsjö, Sweden)** [FT] — [PMC4247029](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4247029)
- Release via roots is over 97% of total photosynthetic O₂ release in split-chamber experiments in the light; the abstract says "almost 100%".
- Root O₂ respiration is about 14 times smaller than root release, leaving a substantial flux to the sediment.
- Photosynthesis at saturating light is 1–2 mg O₂ g⁻¹ plant DW h⁻¹.
- The lawn:
  - maximum summer biomass about 90 g DW/m²;
  - about 28,000 roots/m², about 5 mm apart;
  - leaf area 2.2 m²/m², root area 3.6 m²/m²;
  - 10–17 leaves and 46–77 roots per plant;
  - leaf:root DW ratio 1.4.
- Root O₂ permeability per unit area is 2–3 times that of leaves.
- Gas in the lawn:
  - lacunal gas volume is 945 cm³/m², an O₂ pool of 265 mg O₂/m² at air saturation;
  - the lacunal O₂ store can sustain dark respiration for 5–10 hours.
- In the field, sediment O₂ at 40 mm depth ranged from 21 to 3 kPa near a root (about 100–10% of air saturation), and 10 to 3 kPa at a second position.
  - Porewater held O₂ through day and night.
  - Maxima came in late afternoon and minima late at night.
- Sediment O₂ consumption was "among the lowest measured for freshwater sediments" (no number given). Sand porosity was 40–45%.
- In the laboratory in darkness, sediment O₂ fell from about 8 kPa to zero within 5 hours.

**Pedersen, Colmer, Garcia-Robledo & Revsbech 2018 (Ann. Bot. 122:605)** [FT] — [PMC6153474](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153474)
- For isoetids, "a large proportion of O₂ enters the sediment by root ROL".
- *Littorella* has a more permeable cuticle, so more of its O₂ goes to the water and less to the roots (Sand-Jensen et al. 1982; Møller & Sand-Jensen 2012).
- Porewater pO₂ at about 3 cm depth rose from about 0–10 kPa to 8–18 kPa within 6–8 hours of lights-on. In darkness it fell, in some cores below 0.007 kPa and in others holding near 10 kPa.
- Porewater pCO₂ was about 5 kPa at dawn and about 2 kPa in the light.
- The sediment held 0.74 ± 0.03% organic matter (w/w, top 3 cm) and consumed O₂ at 19.4 ± 0.83 nmol per L of sediment per second (about 1.7 mmol L⁻¹ d⁻¹).

**Littorella uniflora (Christensen, Revsbech & Sand-Jensen 1994, Plant Physiol. 105:847)** [AB] — [PMC160731](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC160731)
- O₂ at the root surface was 20–450 µM from darkness to saturating light (air saturation is 280 µM).
- The oxic shell around roots was about 0.5–5 mm thick.
- O₂ flux from the roots was a saturating function of light on the leaves.
- The authors predict large diurnal swings in dense stands on nutrient-poor sediment.
- The experiment used agar with FeS as the O₂ sink, not field sediment.

**Oxic depth: Raun, Borum & Sand-Jensen 2010 (Freshw. Biol. 55:1891)** [SN] — [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/influence-of-sediment-organic-enrichment-and-water-alkalinity-on-); journal and citation confirmed in the [reference list of He et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2021.766413/pdf) [FT]
- In low-organic sediment the oxic depth was 4.3 cm under *Lobelia*, 3.0 cm under *Littorella*, and 1.6–2.2 cm under *Potamogeton* species.
- Modest organic enrichment cut it to 0.4–1.0 cm for all four species.

**Whole-lawn carbon budget: Ribaudo, Bertrin, Jan, Anschutz & Abril 2016 (Hydrobiologia 784:21; soft-water sandy lakes, SW France)** [SN; HAL record bot-blocked] — [HAL record](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02603665); metadata confirmed at [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s10750-016-2848-x)
- *Lobelia* lawns were a net carbon sink of −0.7 ± 0.4 g C m⁻² d⁻¹ (DIC + CH₄). Bare sediment was a net source of +0.6 ± 0.5 g C m⁻² d⁻¹.
- Photosynthetic quotients were below 1, meaning photosynthetic O₂ "was not released into the water column" but stayed in the lacunae or went to the rhizosphere.

**Diel pattern, other sources**
- Pedersen, Sand-Jensen & Revsbech 1995 (Ecology 76:1536) is the classic "Diel pulses of O₂ and CO₂ in sandy lake sediments inhabited by *Lobelia dortmanna*". [citation only] — [Wikipedia reference list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobelia_dortmanna)
- A planar-optode study (Lenzewski et al. 2018, New Phytol. 218:131; Ihlsee, Germany) imaged O₂ and CO₂ around root tips over 12 h dark, 12 h light and 12 h dark. Both varied strongly between day and night. [SN, dataset description] — [PANGAEA 911073](https://doi.pangaea.de/10.1594/PANGAEA.911073)
- Iron plaques on *Lobelia* roots reduce ROL along the root (loss declines with thicker plaque) and keep more O₂ inside for the root meristems. [SN] — [Møller & Sand-Jensen 2008, New Phytol. 179:848 (KU record)](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/iron-plaques-improve-the-oxygen-supply-to-root-meristems-of-the-f)

### Inferences
These are mine.

**Areal ROL**
- Peak ROL is about (1–2 mg O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹) × 90 g/m² × 0.97, i.e. about 90–175 mg O₂ m⁻² h⁻¹ (2.7–5.5 mmol m⁻² h⁻¹), at light saturation.
- Over a summer day with about 8–12 light-saturated-equivalent hours, gross ROL is about 0.7–2 g O₂ m⁻² d⁻¹ (22–65 mmol m⁻² d⁻¹). Subtract plant and root respiration at night for the net figure.
- Cross-check: −0.7 g C m⁻² d⁻¹ net uptake is about 58 mmol C, about 1.9 g O₂ at a quotient of 1. The two routes agree.
- For eastern-Maine pipewort/lobelia lawns of about 80–90 g DW/m² at 0.1–1.5 m depth in water with Secchi 3–4 m, the lawn sits well inside the euphotic zone. Summer midday photosynthesis is probably near saturation; scale it by the sim's light at the bed.

**Model form**
- ROL(t) = f_root × P_gross(I_bed, T) × B_isoetid.
- Use f_root ≈ 0.95 for *Lobelia*. For *Littorella*-like or *Eriocaulon* plants use a lower, uncertain value, perhaps 0.5–0.8; no *Eriocaulon* measurement was found.
- Treat the flux as an O₂ source spread over the top 3–4 cm of the bed cell, competing with the bed organic layer's O₂ demand (sedOM).
- At night ROL goes to zero and the lacunal store (about 265 mg O₂/m²) carries the plant 5–10 hours, so late-night sediment anoxia is normal in summer.

**Oxidised-layer depth**
- A useful state variable: about 3–4.3 cm under healthy lawns on low-organic sand, collapsing to about 0.4–1 cm as organic matter rises.

### Gaps
- No direct published ROL in mmol O₂ m⁻² d⁻¹ was found. Sand-Jensen, Prahl & Stokholm 1982 (Oikos 38:349) and Sand-Jensen & Prahl 1982 (New Phytol. 91:103) likely hold per-root or per-plant rates but were not accessible.
- No ROL data for *Eriocaulon aquaticum* or North American *Isoetes* (e.g. *I. echinospora*, *I. lacustris*) were found.
- The effect of humic water (lower light) on isoetid ROL is unquantified.
- Conflict to note: leaf-lacunal O₂ late at night was about 10 kPa in the Swedish field study (Sand-Jensen et al. 2005) but below 0.2 kPa in the dark in the 2018 laboratory study at 20 °C (Pedersen et al.). Night-time anoxia depends on temperature, night length and sediment O₂ demand.

## Q5 — Effect on porewater and sediment P (Fe-bound P, P release), Fe²⁺, and N/CH₄ cycling

### Takeaway
Isoetid lawns raise sediment redox and trap P as iron-oxide-bound P while taking up porewater P quickly:
- In Lake Kvie (Denmark), SRP release was below 40 µmol m⁻² d⁻¹ everywhere. Release from vegetated sediment was far below the calculated diffusive flux and supplied only about 5% of the plants' annual P need, with the porewater SRP pool turning over 500–900 times a year.
- In column experiments, *Littorella* sediment retained P, Fe and Mn 2–5 times better than sediment with benthic microalgae (34% vs 11% of added P as metal-oxide-bound P with Fe supply).

For N, coupled nitrification–denitrification is more than 6 times higher in *Lobelia* rhizospheres than in bare sediment. Denitrification is 24–30 µmol N m⁻² h⁻¹ under *Lobelia*/*Littorella* versus 1.5–5 under elodeids or seagrass.

For carbon and methane, vegetated lawns were net C sinks where bare sand was a source.

When organic loading wins, NH₄⁺, Fe²⁺ and CO₂ accumulate.

### Cited Findings
**P: Lake Kvie (Andersen & Olsen 1994, Hydrobiologia 275/276:267; shallow oligotrophic Danish lake, isoetid lawns cover 55% of its area)** [SN, abstract via search; bibliographic record confirmed at [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/BF00026717)] — [DOI 10.1007/BF00026717](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00026717)
- Porewater SRP was generally low. In vegetated littoral sediment it peaked just below the surface and decreased with depth.
- SRP release was below 40 µmol m⁻² d⁻¹ at all stations. At the vegetated station it was much lower than the calculated diffusive flux, attributed to P assimilation by the isoetids and SRP binding to oxidised Fe.
- Annual P release from vegetated sediment was only 5% of the macrophytes' P requirement. Fast plant uptake gave the SRP pool a turnover of 500–900 times per year.
- The high redox in the rhizosphere is attributed to O₂ excreted by the roots. Isoetids are "important for keeping lakes in an oligotrophic state".

**P: experiments**
- *Littorella* in sediment percolated with artificial porewater (Christensen & Andersen 1996, Aquat. Bot. 55:183) [SN] — [record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/2083595):
  - redox potential was markedly higher than in bare sediment, owing to root O₂ release;
  - steady-state P retention was about 43% in light and 47% in darkness, largely by plant uptake;
  - in planted sediment, 63% of recovered P was in the sediment, mostly adsorbed and Fe-bound, below 5 cm;
  - in bare sediment, retained P sat near the surface as organic P and Fe-bound P.
- Andersen, Jensen & Christensen 1997 (Biogeochemistry 38:149), two 46-day column runs with oligotrophic-lake sediment [SN] — [catalogue record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edb,8410184):
  - cumulative retention of P, Fe and Mn was 2–5 times higher with *Littorella* than with benthic microalgae, through higher P uptake and more efficient Fe and Mn oxidation;
  - with Fe supplied, 34% of added P was retained as metal-oxide-bound P under *Littorella* vs 11% under microalgae;
  - with Mn supplied, 21% vs 2%.

**Reduced compounds under organic loading**
- Small additions of organic matter caused sediment O₂ depletion and accumulation of NH₄⁺, Fe²⁺ and CO₂. [SN/AB] — [Møller & Sand-Jensen 2011, New Phytol. 190:320 (KU record)](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/high-sensitivity-of-lobelia-dortmanna-to-sediment-oxygen-depletio)
- In enriched sediment, *Lobelia* leaf N and P fell below the minimum needed for cell function, with very low chlorophyll and photosynthesis. The proposed cause is that anoxia limits ATP formation and nutrient transfer to the leaves.

**N cycling**
- *Lobelia* sediment, studied with ¹⁵N isotope pairing and O₂/NO₃⁻/NH₄⁺ microsensors (Risgaard-Petersen & Jensen 1997, L&O 42:529) [SN; abstract text reproduced on a hobbyist forum, so verify against the journal] — [forum copy of abstract](https://www.monsterfishkeepers.com/forums/goto/post?id=8469246):
  - coupled nitrification–denitrification was more than 6 times higher than in bare sediment over the light–dark cycle;
  - light cut denitrification in *Lobelia* sediment by about 30%;
  - root O₂ expanded the oxic zone, and NO₃⁻ reached about 30 µM in the rhizosphere.
- Rhizosphere denitrification was 24 µmol N m⁻² h⁻¹ under *Lobelia* and 30 under *Littorella*, against 1.5–5 under *Zostera marina* and *Potamogeton pectinatus*. [SN; site returned 429] — [Ottosen, Risgaard-Petersen & Nielsen 1999, Aquat. Microb. Ecol. 19:81](https://int-res.com/abstracts/ame/v19/n1/p81-91)

**Carbon and CH₄**
- Vegetated lawns were a net sink of −0.7 ± 0.4 g C m⁻² d⁻¹ (DIC + CH₄); bare sediment was a source of +0.6 ± 0.5 g C m⁻² d⁻¹. The paper's title stresses methane oxidation in *Lobelia* lawns. [SN] — [Ribaudo et al. 2016, Hydrobiologia 784:21](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02603665)
- Isoetid porewater CO₂ is often 10–250 times atmospheric equilibrium, because of organic-matter mineralisation. [SN, secondary summary] — search summary citing [Pedersen et al. 1995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobelia_dortmanna)
- Measured porewater pCO₂ swung between about 5 kPa at dawn and 2 kPa in the light. [FT] — [Pedersen et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153474)

**Review**
- Smolders, Lucassen & Roelofs 2002, "The isoetid environment: biogeochemistry and threats" (Aquat. Bot. 73:325–350), covers radial oxygen loss and the threats of acidification and eutrophication. [record only] — [marineinfo record](https://marineinfo.org/id/publication/26113)

### Inferences
These are mine, for the sim's sediment P store and bed organic layer.

**Under-lawn P release multiplier**
- Multiply the bed cell's diffusive SRP release by about 0.1–0.3 while the isoetid lawn is healthy, i.e. while its oxidised layer is deeper than about 1 cm.
- Kvie's "much lower than diffusive" and the 2–5-fold retention in the columns bracket this.
- Route the difference into an Fe-bound P pool that stays in the sediment P store. Do not delete it: the closed budget must hold.

**Plant P uptake**
- Let isoetids draw P from porewater, i.e. the sediment P store, rather than from the water column. That reproduces the 500–900 per year turnover qualitatively.

**Losing the oxidised cap**
- When the oxidised layer collapses (organic loading, Q6), switch off the retention multiplier.
- Release the Fe-bound P gradually, e.g. with a time constant of days to weeks. This is the internal-loading feedback that isoetid loss triggers.

**N**
- Add a rhizosphere denitrification term of about 0.6–0.7 mmol N m⁻² d⁻¹ (about 8–10 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹) under lawns, compared with roughly 1/6 of that, or the elodeid value, on bare sand.
- Debit sediment/porewater NO₃⁻ or NH₄⁺ and send it to N₂ leaving the system. That is a legitimate exit in a closed budget if the ledger tracks it.

**CH₄**
- Qualitatively, an isoetid lawn should oxidise CH₄ from below, so its net CH₄ flux is about 0. Quantification is a gap.

### Gaps
- No porewater Fe²⁺ or PO₄³⁻ concentrations (µM) for vegetated vs bare sand were obtained. Neither were Fe:P ratios or redox values in mV.
- The Kvie and Christensen full texts were not accessible, and neither was the Smolders et al. 2002 review.
- No CH₄ flux numbers separated from the combined DIC + CH₄.
- No North American (e.g. Adirondack, Maine or Nova Scotia soft-water) isoetid sediment P study was found.

## Q6 — Organic loading threshold that kills isoetids (is ~2.4% labile OM right?)

### Takeaway
The 2.4% figure is real but needs care. Lobelia roots shorten from about 10 cm to 2 cm, and the plant dies or loses anchorage, at "even modest contents (2.4%) of degradable organic matter" in the sediment. This comes from a 2008 addendum by Møller & Sand-Jensen, seen only as a search snippet. It refers to degradable (labile) organic matter, not total loss-on-ignition.

The supporting evidence is consistent:
- Healthy *Lobelia* sand has about 0.74% organic matter (Pedersen et al. 2018).
- Modest enrichment shrinks the oxic layer from 3–4.3 cm to 0.4–1.0 cm (Raun et al. 2010).
- Enrichment prolongs nightly anoxia and pushes leaf N and P below the minimum (Møller & Sand-Jensen 2011).
- *Littorella* tolerates anoxia better, because its leaves exchange O₂ with the water much faster.

### Cited Findings
- *Lobelia* "dies or loses anchorage by shortening the roots from 10 to 2 cm at even modest contents (2.4%) of degradable organic matter". Iron plaques on roots in enriched sediment reduce ROL along the root and push O₂ to the root tips. They may also act as a redox buffer against sulphide and other reduced toxins at night. [SN; publisher page returned 403] — [Møller & Sand-Jensen 2008, Plant Signal. Behav. 3(10), "Outstanding *Lobelia dortmanna* in iron armor"](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.4161/psb.3.10.6500)
- Brief anoxia in the sediment and leaf lacunae late at night is a recurring summer event in *Lobelia* populations. Extra organic input lengthens it and lowers survival. Small organic additions raise NH₄⁺, Fe²⁺ and CO₂. [SN/AB] — [Møller & Sand-Jensen 2011, New Phytol. 190:320](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/high-sensitivity-of-lobelia-dortmanna-to-sediment-oxygen-depletio)
- Organic enrichment reduces *Lobelia* growth through O₂ stress (Sand-Jensen, Borum & Binzer 2005, Freshw. Biol. 50:1034–1048, "Oxygen stress and reduced growth of *Lobelia dortmanna* in sandy lake sediments subject to organic enrichment"). [record only; title and keywords] — [KU research profile](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/oxygen-stress-and-reduced-growth-of-emlobelia-dortmannaem-in-sand/)
- With modest enrichment the oxic depth fell from 4.3 cm (*Lobelia*) and 3.0 cm (*Littorella*) to 0.4–1.0 cm. The study also raised alkalinity from 0.17 to 3.20 meq/L to compare isoetids and elodeids. [SN] — [Raun, Borum & Sand-Jensen 2010, Freshw. Biol. 55:1891](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/influence-of-sediment-organic-enrichment-and-water-alkalinity-on-)
- The natural *Lobelia*/*Littorella* sediment in Pedersen et al. 2018 had 0.74 ± 0.03% organic matter (w/w, top 3 cm). [FT] — [PMC6153474](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153474)
- *Littorella* (a CAM plant) kept leaf pO₂ at 10–15 kPa in darkness, while *Lobelia* (C3) fell below 0.2 kPa. [FT] — [Pedersen et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153474)
- Gas exchange across the leaves is much higher in *Littorella* than in *Lobelia*. The snippet reads "1316 times higher", which is probably a garbled "13–16 times". That gives *Littorella* tolerance to sediment anoxia. [SN] — [Møller & Sand-Jensen 2012, Freshw. Biol. (KU record)](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/rapid-oxygen-exchange-across-the-leaves-of-emlittorella-uniflorae)
- In Pulido, Lucassen, Pedersen & Roelofs 2011 (Freshw. Biol. 56:939), both the quantity and the lability of sediment organic matter affected the biomass of *Littorella uniflora* and *Echinodorus repens*. [citation confirmed in a reference list; findings not accessed] — [He et al. 2021 reference list](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2021.766413/pdf)

### Inferences
These are mine, for the sim.

**Lethal threshold**
- Express isoetid stress as a function of the bed organic layer (sedOM) converted to degradable OM as % of sediment DW.
- No stress below about 1%. Rising stress from 1% to 2.4%. Mortality and uprooting above about 2.4% labile OM for *Lobelia*-type rosettes.
- Give pipewort (*Eriocaulon*) an intermediate tolerance, with *Littorella* the most tolerant. That ordering is my assumption; there are no *Eriocaulon* data.
- If the sim's sedOM is total organic matter rather than labile, the threshold in total-OM terms is higher. How much higher depends on how labile the material is.
  - Pulido et al. 2011 suggest lability matters as much as quantity.
  - A conservative mapping treats about 25–50% of fresh deposits as labile. That fraction is an assumption.

**Positive feedback**
- Healthy lawn → deep oxic layer, low P release and fast OM mineralisation. More organic deposition (leaf litter, dead *Utricularia*/elodeid detritus, settled phyto) → a shallower oxic layer → lawn stress → less ROL → more reduced sediment and P release.
- That is a realistic switch. Keep it slow, over weeks, using the night-anoxia duration as the driver.
- In the sim's shallow, non-stratifying Hot Brook lakes, the night anoxia that matters is in the sediment, not the water column.

**Season**
- The ROL and anoxia stress should be strongest in July–August: warm nights raise sediment respiration and nights are short, though ROL is high by day.
- Under ice, ROL is near zero but sediment respiration is low too, so isoetids are probably dormant and safe. This is a design assumption to walk through all four seasons; no winter data were found.

### Gaps
- The 2.4% figure is seen only as a snippet of a short addendum. The original experiment's method (how "degradable OM" was measured) is not confirmed.
- No threshold in loss-on-ignition terms was found for *Eriocaulon aquaticum* or for North American *Lobelia* populations.
- The Pulido et al. 2011 numbers are unknown.
- An unrelated snippet claimed *Littorella* biomass peaked at "75%" sediment organic matter. That is implausible (perhaps 7.5%), was unverified, and I excluded it.
- Lucassen et al. (about 2016) reported a roughly 60% decline in isoetid cover in limed Norwegian lakes between 1995 and 2010, linked to sediment anoxia and uprooting. This was seen only in a search summary without a resolvable source, so it is not used.
