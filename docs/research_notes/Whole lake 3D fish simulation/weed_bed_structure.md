# Weed-bed structure in small, soft, coloured northeastern lakes: how much of the bed is vegetated, where beds sit, how dense they are inside, and how much they hide small fish

Compiled 2026-10-18 for the 3-D Hot Brook lakes (Upper ~370 ha of bed, max 5.5 m, mean ~3 m; Lower ~275 ha, a 2.5–3.9 m saucer; Secchi ~3–4 m, colour ~30–50 Pt-Co, low alkalinity). The question is spatial structure, in-stand density and refuge architecture. Growth physiology, light and depth limits, lake-averaged biomass and season are NOT repeated here.

## 0. Takeaways

1. **Vegetated share is low and patchy.** Surface-mapped beds cover 3–39% of lake area (median ~9–16%) in eight Adirondack soft-water lakes, and rakes hit plants at 4–56% of littoral points [primary]. Inferred for Hot Brook by depth band: 0–0.5 m 20–40%; 0.5–1 m 40–70%; 1–2 m 40–70%; 2–3 m 15–40%; 3–4 m 0–15%; >4 m ~0. That is **~20% of Upper's bed and ~24% of Lower's** [inf]. Lake-wide PVI is ~2–5%, but 15–40% inside the coves' beds [inf].
2. **Patches.** Bed sizes are heavy-tailed: the median bed is ~0.5–0.9 acre (2,000–3,600 m²), and the 1–2 largest beds hold 42–74% of bed area [primary/inf]. Maine's community types are "small patch, linear" along shore. Within a bed, each species covers mostly 6–25% [primary].
3. **Placement (Maine MNAP) [primary].** Pipewort–lobelia lawns grow at 0.2–1.1 m on mineral soil. Water-lily beds grow at 0.5–2.5 m on silty organic muck in quiet water. Pickerelweed marsh grows at <0.7 m on muck, with total cover 30–100%. Bulrush stands in water >15 cm deep [snippet]. Bare ground: wave-washed cobble and boulder (the user's dock shore is bare to 0.6 m) and bedrock or steep drop-offs. The outer edge thins gradually with light; the inner edge is set abruptly by exposure and substrate. Slope explained 72% of maximum biomass (88% with sediment organic matter) in Lake Memphremagog (Duarte & Kalff) [snippet, in `macrophytes.md`].
4. **Refuge thresholds by architecture.**
   - A bass's capture rate falls between 50 and 250 stems/m², which is stem spacing of ~140 → 63 mm.
   - Bluegill choose a patch as refuge at ~516 stems/m² [secondary].
   - Pike (the pickerel surrogate) enter structure below ~600 stems/m² and still catch minnows at 1,000.
   - Fine-leaved pondweed has 2.5–7× the interstitial complexity of milfoil. Bass diets there held 14% fish against 75% [primary, per the delegated reading].
   - Lily and watershield stands have only 19 and 209 stems/m², so their stems are no barrier [primary, per the delegated reading]. Their value is shade: a sunlit predator's detection distance into shade is half the reverse (Helfman) [secondary].
   - Structure protects only fish that use it. Prey hold ~1 m inside or outside the edge, and esocids hunt the edge.
5. **Hot Brook.** The only records are *Nitella flexilis* and *N. tenuissima* (2015). Surface-reaching beds are only 0.3–0.6% of lake area, a shore-hugging fringe at 0.3–1.0 m. The submerged beds are unmeasured.

## 1. Areal extent and patchiness

### Takeaway
No Maine soft-water lake has a published, readable depth-band survey of vegetated area. The nearest evidence points one way: in small, soft, coloured northeastern lakes, vegetation is **patchy and shore-hugging, not a continuous carpet**. Surface-visible beds cover **3–39% of lake area** (median ~9–16%) in nine Adirondack lakes. Rake tosses across the whole littoral hit plants at only **4–56%** of points (median ~13%). Beds come in a heavy-tailed size distribution: a median bed is **~0.5–0.9 acre (2,000–3,600 m²)**, and the one or two largest beds hold **42–74%** of the bed area. Within a bed, any one species typically covers only **6–25%** of the bottom. Point-intercept surveys of comparable lakes put **~40–70% of points shallower than the plants' depth limit** in vegetation, falling off steeply as the light limit nears. At Hot Brook itself the surface-reaching forms (lilies, emergents) are only **0.3–0.6% of lake area** (aerial photos; `plant_species_hot_brook.md`).

**Point-intercept frequencies (surrogates: Wisconsin, Minnesota)**
- **Rock Lake**, Vilas Co., WI (northern lakes ecoregion), 2025 [primary]: 306 sites, 107 with vegetation. Plants grew to 13 ft (4.0 m); the most frequent depth of colonisation was 3 ft (0.9 m). **42.3%** of the sites shallower than the maximum depth of plants were vegetated. Muck was the dominant substrate at the vegetated sites. Average species per vegetated site 2.66. — [Rock Lake PI report 2025](https://winchesterwi.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Rock_PI_Report_2025.pdf)
- **Lake Vermilion**, MN (a Canadian Shield lake), 2019 [primary]: mean Secchi ~8.5 ft (2.6 m), close to Hot Brook. 526 points sampled to 14 ft; **no plants beyond 11 ft (3.4 m)**; 150 points (29%) had no vegetation. Presence and density fell with depth. Some shallow points were bare "due to the rocky substrate". Robbins' pondweed was at 12–13% of points and *Fontinalis* at 1%. — [2019 RMB report, Lake Vermilion](https://www.vermilionlakeassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/2019-RMB-report-vermilion.pdf)
- **Minnesota DNR lake surveys** (mostly hard water: SURROGATE, denser than Hot Brook should be) [snippet]: shore–15 ft (0–4.6 m) zones were vegetated at 49% (Farm Island), 78% (Grand, Stearns), 82% (Round, Crow Wing) and 99% (Big Swan) of sites; Farm Island 6% beyond 15 ft. Quamba Lake: 77% of sites at 0–5 ft but only 15% deeper than 5 ft. — [MN DNR vegetation reports](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/natural_resources/water/lakes/vegetation_reports/01015900.pdf)
- Typical point-intercept definitions [primary, Rock Lake]: "littoral frequency" = vegetated points ÷ points shallower than the maximum depth of plants; the maximum depth is often taken at the 95th percentile of vegetated points (MN) [snippet].

**Colour: clear vs stained lakes in the same park** (Kejimkujik, Nova Scotia) [snippet of a Hydrobiologia abstract, link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00192868; Springer redirected to a login]:
- Clear Beaverskin Lake had macrophyte beds across most of its bottom to 6.5 m, the commonest community at 2.0–6.5 m being *Sphagnum macrophyllum* + *Utricularia vulgaris*.
- Humic-stained Pebbleloggitch Lake had plants only in a narrow fringe shallower than ~1.8 m, dominated by *Nuphar variegata* rooted to 1.8 m.

**Isoetids as a share of the littoral** [snippet; SURROGATE NW Poland, 25 soft-water "lobelia lakes"]: water lobelia covered on average **4.8%** of the littoral zone, with maximum colonisation depth 0.1–2.2 m (median 0.8 m). — [Polish lobelia-lake study](https://www.mdpi.com/2300-7575/17/4/171). Lake Kalgaard (Denmark): isoetids on 40% of the bed (in `macrophytes.md`).

**Maine community types: depth, substrate, cover and landscape pattern** (Maine Natural Areas Program fact sheets):
- **Pipewort – Water Lobelia Aquatic Bed** ("Sandy lake-bottom", S5) [primary, PDF text]: depths usually **0.2–1.1 m**; "predominant mineral soil component, rather than muck"; cover "sparse to extensive"; watershield "may be abundant in patches"; lilies and pickerelweed at low cover. **Landscape pattern: small patch, linear.** Associated rare plants include Acadian, prototype and shore quillworts. — [MNAP sandy lake bottom](https://www1.maine.gov/DACF/mnap/features/communities/sandy_lake_bottom.pdf)
- **Water-lily – Macrophyte Aquatic Bed** ("Open-water marsh", S5) [primary, PDF text]: quiet water at **0.5–2.5 m**, "silty organic muck"; white or yellow water-lily or watershield dominant, or in variants floating-leaved pondweeds such as bigleaf pondweed; bladderworts and pipewort submerged associates. **Landscape pattern: small patch, often linear along lakeshores.** — [MNAP open-water marsh](https://www1.maine.gov/DACF/mnap/features/communities/open_water_marsh.pdf)
  - The same sheet names **Robbins' pondweed** with water-marigold, common waterweed and straight-leaved pondweed as *alkaline*-water indicators of a separate "Circumneutral–Alkaline Water Macrophyte Suite" [primary]. In Maine, a big Robbins' carpet is thus less typical of a soft, coloured lake than of a circumneutral one (inf). It does grow in soft Adirondack lakes (Lake George: below).
- **Pickerelweed Marsh** (Pickerelweed – Macrophyte Aquatic Bed, S5) [primary, HTML]: depth generally **<0.7 m**, usually mucky substrate, acidic to circumneutral; **total plant cover 30–100%, typically >50%**; landscape pattern small patch. — [MNAP pickerelweed marsh](https://www11.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/features/communities/pickerelweedmarsh.htm)
- **Bulrush Bed** (S4) [snippet]: standing water typically deeper than 15 cm year-round; hardstem, softstem bulrush, chair-maker's rush and bayonet rush dominant. (The maine.gov page returned 403 and was not retried.)

**Bare ground in the shallows**
- Lake George, NY (soft, oligotrophic) [snippet, LGPC/APA survey reports]: the wave-break zone at 1–4 ft (0.3–1.2 m) was mostly sand with little organic matter; areas by bedrock or steep drop-offs lacked macrophytes. *Nitella* there grows at 25–35 ft (7.6–10.7 m) and *Isoetes lacustris* at 20 ft and deeper (clear water, Secchi ~8–10 m).
- Lake George deep fringe [snippet, an RPI study]: at ≥10 m, densities were only 0.01–10 plants/m² (*Elodea*) and 0.02–2 plants/m² (*P. robbinsii*); *P. robbinsii* and *Isoetes macrospora* were most abundant below 6 m. The edge toward the light limit is a thinning scatter, not a sharp wall.
- Belgrade Lakes, Maine (Bevier et al. 2024 preprint) [primary]: at 0.5 m in front of undeveloped shores, sand was ~20% of the substrate (17% at buffered developed sites, 52% at unbuffered ones); East Pond had significantly more boulder at both 0.5 and 1.0 m. Macrophyte abundance at 0.5 and 1.0 m did not differ by shore treatment. — [bioRxiv 10.1101/2024.08.06.606825](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.06.606825.full.pdf)
- USGS 43-lake Maine study, as summarised in `shore_geometry_and_vegetation.md` [secondary]: clear Maine lakes with rocky, gravelly or sandy littoral zones usually have only sparse rooted plants, dominated by pipewort; highly coloured lakes have mainly bulrush and floating-leaved communities.

**Lake-wide cover for fish** (in `macrophytes.md`): MN DNR, game fish do best at 18–40% basin cover.

- **Lake-wide (inf).** With the 3-D bed's hypsography (Upper: 0–1 m 5%, 1–2 m 19%, 2–3 m 20%, 3–4 m 34%, >4 m 22% of area; Lower: 6%, 16%, 35%, 43%, 0%; `habitat3d.md`), the mid values give **~20% of Upper's bed and ~24% of Lower's** vegetated (range ~12–35%). That sits inside the Adirondack range (3–39%) and the MN game-fish optimum (18–40%).
- **PVI (inf).** Taking in-bed cover ~0.6 and canopy height ÷ depth ~0.6 at 0–1 m, ~0.4 at 1–2 m, ~0.1 at 2–3 m and ~0.05 deeper, the lake-wide PVI is only **~2–5%**. Locally, in the coves' lily–pondweed beds, it is **15–40%**. So the zooplankton refuge (½ at ~20% PVI) is a cove-scale effect, not a lake-scale one.
- **Patch structure to draw (inf).** Use a heavy-tailed patch-size distribution: most beds 0.01–1 ha, a median ~0.2–0.4 ha, and one or two large beds per lake (in the sheltered coves) holding about half the vegetated area. Beds are linear along the shore (MNAP "small patch, linear"). Inside a bed, cover per species is mostly 5–25%, total 30–100%.
- **Sharpness.** The inner edge is set by exposure and substrate (abrupt: rock vs muck). The outer edge is set by light (gradual: frequency and height fall over ~0.5–1 m of depth).

## 2. What controls where beds are

**Status:** the research agent on this question had not reported when this note was written. What follows is only what the other passes and the earlier notes hold. Still to add: Keddy's exposure index (Axe Lake), Duarte & Kalff's slope equation, the ice-scour depths and the substrate optima.

- **Substrate and quiet water decide the inner edge (Maine)** [primary, MNAP; §1]:
  - rosettes on mineral sand or gravel at 0.2–1.1 m;
  - lilies on silty organic muck in quiet water at 0.5–2.5 m;
  - pickerelweed on muck shallower than 0.7 m.
- **Exposure.** "Clear Maine lakes with rocky, gravelly or sandy littoral zones usually have only sparse rooted plants, dominated by pipewort" (USGS WRI 80-69) [secondary]. Rocky points and the wave-break zone are bare: Lake George at 0.3–1.2 m is sand with little organic matter, and bedrock and steep drop-offs lack macrophytes [snippet]. Lake Vermilion's shallow rocky points were bare [primary].
- **Slope.** Slope explained 72% of maximum submerged biomass in Lake Memphremagog, and 88% with sediment organic matter (Duarte & Kalff 1986, 1990) [snippet, in `macrophytes.md` §3.1]. Within a lake, biomass varies with littoral slope; between lakes it varies with slope and chemistry (Duarte's McGill thesis abstract) [snippet].
- **Ice and waves.** The sim already uses a wave half-saturation depth of 0.15 m and ice scour shallower than 0.5 m (`plants3.js`). Ice push builds ramparts 0.5–1.5 m high on gently sloping till shores of lakes 0.8–4 km across (`shore_geometry_and_vegetation.md`) [secondary].
- **Colour and light set the outer edge.** In Kejimkujik, the clear lake had beds to 6.5 m and the stained lake only to <1.8 m [snippet]. Lake Vermilion (Secchi 2.6 m) had no plants beyond 3.4 m [primary]. The light-limit numbers are in `macrophytes.md` §2.
- **Hot Brook's slopes (inf, from `habitat3d.md`).** Mean depth reaches ~1 m within 30 m of shore, 1.9 m by 30–60 m and 2.7 m by 60–100 m. That is about 2–6%: gentle, the high-biomass end of the slope rule. The user's camp shore has a shelf to ~1.3 m, then a drop.

## 4. Fish refuge value by plant architecture

Already in `macrophytes.md` §5.2 (not repeated): Savino & Stein 1982 (bass capture of bluegill similar at 0 and 50 stems/m², near zero at 250 and 1,000); Gotceitas & Colgan 1987; Crowder & Cooper 1982; Werner et al. 1983; Diehl 1988; Eklöv & Diehl 1994; Killgore et al. 1989; Weaver et al. 1997. The sim already uses capture × 1/(1 + (S/S50)³) with S50 ≈ 150 stems/m² (`plants3.js`).

### Takeaway
Structure protects small fish through **gaps narrower than the predator's body**, in **the depth layer the prey occupies**, and **only for prey that use it**. The protective step for a 25–35 cm bass lies between **50 and 250 stems/m²** (stem spacing ~140 → ~63 mm). Bluegill pick a patch as refuge only at **~500 stems/m²**. A pike-like ambusher (the pickerel's surrogate) keeps hunting in and beside structure up to **~600 stems/m²** and still catches minnows at 1,000. Fine-leaved pondweeds give 2.5–7× the interstitial complexity of a milfoil-type canopy, and bass in them ate **14% fish against 75%**. Lily and watershield stands are far too sparse to impede predators (**19 and 209 stems/m²**); what they offer is **shade**, which halves a sunlit predator's detection distance. Edges: no measured decay curve exists; prey hold within ~1 m of a bed edge, and esocids hunt the edge.

### Cited findings

**Stem-density thresholds** (all SURROGATE: largemouth bass, bluegill, pike, Eurasian perch)
- **Gotceitas & Colgan 1989**, *Oecologia* 80:158: largemouth bass 25–35 cm TL on bluegill 30–60 mm (Lake Opinicon stock), artificial stems, 20 °C. Logistic fits gave a capture-success threshold and a higher refuge-choice threshold [abstract] ([QUBS](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/predator-foraging-success-and-habitat-complexity-quantitative-test-of-the-threshold-hypothesis)). The refuge-choice value is **516 stems/m²** as quoted by Shoup & Broderius 2018 [secondary]; the capture-success value was not found.
- **Shoup & Broderius 2018**, *NAJFM* 38:630 [primary, per the delegated reading] ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/najfm/article/38/3/630/7817463)): 1-m² enclosures at 0, 50, 250, 500 stems/m²; juvenile bass 40 mm, 30 bluegill/m² (25–35% of bass length). Bass ate fewest and grew least at **250**; 500 did not differ from 0 — **non-monotonic for small predators**. Their review: 0 and 50 stems/m² "ineffective", 250–500 effective; adult bass forage poorly at 250–1,000 and switch to ambush above ~250. Broderius 2015: juvenile bass ate most fathead minnows at **125 stems/m²** [secondary].
- **Gotceitas 1990**, *Oecologia* 83:346: bluegill 3.5–5.0 cm SL choosing among 100, 250, 500 stems/m² chose by energy gain without a predator and by minimum mortality ÷ foraging rate with a bass present [abstract]. Gotceitas & Colgan 1990 (*Copeia* 1990:409): bluegill shifted time into 500-stem plots and fed faster while in the sparse ones [abstract]. 500 stems/m² lowered bluegill foraging success and lengthened capture time [snippet].
- **Savino & Stein 1989**, *Anim. Behav.* 37:311 (0, 50, 250, 1,000 stems/m²) [snippet]: bass switched from searching to ambushing as density rose; pike always ambushed. At high density **both predators still caught fathead minnows but not bluegill**: structure protects only prey that hide in it.
- **Bean & Winfield 1995**, *Ecol. Freshw. Fish* 4:37 (200, 400, 600 stems/m²) [abstract] ([Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/187862)): **pike entered structure only below 600 stems/m²**. With pike present roach and rudd spent more time in structure, but Eurasian perch spent *less* at every density. Capture success rudd > roach > perch.
- **Snickars et al. 2004**, *J. Fish Biol.* 65:1604 [abstract]: age-0 Eurasian perch facing pike preferred vegetation to open water; density affected use only in clear water, where use fell as density rose.
- **Chang & Todd 2023**, *npj Biodiversity* 2:1, meta-analysis of 95 papers (572 effect sizes, 89% lab) [primary, per the delegated reading]: complexity reduces predation, Hedges' g = −0.85 (95% CI −1.14 to −0.55); weaker with a top predator present (−0.41 vs −0.88) or several predator species (−0.52 vs −0.90); hunting mode did not change the overall effect.
- **Stem spacing** for a regular array, s ≈ 1000/√D mm (inf): 50 stems/m² → 141 mm; 250 → 63 mm; 500 → 45 mm; 1,000 → 32 mm. So the bass refuge step sits at gaps of ~60–140 mm for a ~300 mm bass (body depth ~70–80 mm, inf).

**Architecture and interstices**
- **Dibble & Harrel 1997**, *J. Aquat. Plant Manage.* 35:74 [primary, per the delegated reading]: 10-m enclosures, 1.5–1.6 m deep, ~80% surface cover; *Potamogeton nodosus* vs *Myriophyllum spicatum*; largemouth bass, Texas (SURROGATE). From 1-m diver transects:

  | Plant | Horizontal interstices per m | Horizontal interstice length (mm) | Vertical per m | Vertical length (mm) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Pondweed | 32.7–44.7 | 15.0–27.9 | 12.9–41.9 | 22.7–35.3 |
  | Milfoil | 6.1–22.0 | 33.6–76.9 | 8.9–25.0 | 33.2–88.4 |

  Adult bass (mean 264 mm) diet was **14% fish in pondweed vs 75% in milfoil**; juveniles (102 mm) 29% vs 67%. Prey: bluegill (32 mm), bass (57 mm), fathead minnow. Invertebrate densities did not differ. With the index I = f_h/l_h + f_v/l_v, pondweed ≈ 2.6–3.2 and milfoil ≈ 0.44–1.06, so pondweed was **2.5–7× as complex** (inf).
- **Theel, Nelson & Mudge 2012**, *JAPM* 50:129 [primary, per the delegated reading]: defines the same index; untreated hydrilla scored 104, growth-regulated hydrilla 4.5–12. Citing Theel & Dibble 2008: monotypic hydrilla was 7× more complex than a native bed and bluegill fed less efficiently in it [secondary]. Citing Valley & Bremigan 2002 (*TAFS* 131:234): **evenly partitioned complexity through the water column favours bass foraging**; dense canopies are barriers [secondary].
- **Valley & Bremigan 2002**, *JAPM* 40:79, southern Michigan lakes [primary, per the delegated reading]: lake-wide milfoil cover of 8–91% did not explain age-0 bass growth (p = 0.37); age-0 bluegill availability did (r² = 0.84). Age-0 bass turned piscivorous at **60–80 mm TL** and ate fish averaging **31% of their length**; bluegill counted as vulnerable at ≤ 40% of bass TL.
- **Warfe & Barmuta 2004**, *Oecologia* 141:171, artificial plants, Tasmania [snippet]: over the tested range, density did not affect capture by pygmy perch or damselfly larvae; both did worse in the complex *Myriophyllum* analogue than in the simpler *Triglochin* and *Eleocharis* analogues. (Repository 403; no numbers.)
- **Keast 1984**, *Can. J. Zool.* 62:1289, Lake Opinicon, Ontario [snippet]: after milfoil invaded the 2.0–3.5 m zone, bluegill declined; daytime fish counts were several times higher over native *Potamogeton*/*Vallisneria*; prey taxa 3–7× more abundant under native beds.
- **Carden 2002**, UW–Stevens Point MS (18 sites of 5 × 15 m in 12 north-temperate lakes) [abstract]: bluegill, pumpkinseed, yellow perch and crappie rose with complexity and fell with bed porosity (r² 0.24–0.62), inconsistently across lakes.
- **Brown & Colgan 1982**, Lake Opinicon [abstract]: milfoil 1.5–2.5 m tall formed an inshore "curtain" at 2–3 m depth, 25–30 m offshore.

**Gap size against predator size**
- **Lynch & Johnson 1989**, *NAJFM* 9:219 [snippet]: structures with 40, 150 and 350 mm interstices. Bluegill of 50–100 mm and >150 mm consistently chose **40 mm** gaps. **Bass < 300 mm TL used 40 and 350 mm gaps; bass > 300 mm only 350 mm.** On any day 90% of bluegill were not in a structure.
- Bartholomew, Diaz & Cicchetti 2000 (*MEPS* 206:45) define Sp/Pr = mean space between structures ÷ the predator's limiting width [snippet]; threshold values not reached (429/403).

**Edges, patches, predator mode**
- No quantitative edge-distance decay curve was found.
- Trebitz et al. 1997 (*Ecol. Model.*) model bass taking bluegill effectively only along the periphery of dense beds [snippet].
- Jacobsen & Perrow 1998: with pike present, 90–92% of roach used open water by day but stayed ~**1 m from the macrophyte edge**; without predators 13% left the plants by day and 90% at night [secondary, [FAO review ch. 6](https://www.fao.org/4/X7580E/X7580E06.htm), read].
- Morrow et al. 1991: bass grew better at hydrilla edges and at intermediate-to-high density than in unbroken dense stands [secondary].
- Hosn & Downing 1994: fish inside macrophytes were near-randomly distributed; in open water they aggregated [secondary].
- Killgore et al. 1988: fish density in *Myriophyllum* beds did not vary with plant density; Dewey et al. 1997: summer bluegill abundance tracked vegetation biomass (Lake Onalaska, Mississippi River) [secondary, both in the [FAO review ch. 6](https://www.fao.org/4/X7580E/X7580E06.htm), no numbers given].
- Eklöv & Diehl 1994: pike are more efficient around refuges, perch in open water [snippet]. Persson & Eklöv 1995: piscivorous perch stayed in or near the prey refuge [snippet].
- Smallmouth bass generally avoid plant-dominated shallows (Dibble's management chapter) [secondary]; Weaver et al. 1997 found them on bare ground (in `macrophytes.md`).
- Chain pickerel, George Lake, Kejimkujik (soft, coloured; 61 fish of 31–62 cm tagged): habitat use differed among individuals, with summer migrations out of the lake and winter inactivity; no habitat numbers in the abstract [primary, abstract PDF] ([Sauriol, Nilsen & Avery](https://merseytobeatic.ca/sites/merseytobeatic.ca/files/agendafiles/Sauriol_Felixe.pdf)).
- Minnesota (44 lakes): emergent + floating-leaf cover was 66% lower along developed shores, and vegetation occurrence correlated with the relative biomass and mean size of northern pike, bluegill and pumpkinseed (Radomski & Goeman 2001, *NAJFM* 21:46) [abstract] ([MN WRL record](https://wrl.mnpals.net/node/2440)).

**Floating-leaved and emergent stands; shade**
- **Duffy & Jackson 1994**, *Proc. SEAFWA* 48:566, Mississippi (SURROGATE) [primary, per the delegated reading]:

  | Stand | Stems/m² (mean ± SD) | Surface cover |
  |---|---|---|
  | *Nymphaea odorata* | **19.3 ± 2.3** | 0.72 |
  | *Brasenia schreberi* | **208.8 ± 12.9** | 0.76 |
  | *Potamogeton nodosus* | 866.5 ± 75.1 | 0.93 |

  Pondweed held more and larger bluegill (≥ 48 mm); small bluegill (< 48 mm) catch did not differ among the three; condition did not differ.
- **Pedlow et al. 2006**, *JAPM* 44:26, Perch Lake, Michigan [primary, per the delegated reading]: milfoil-dominated littoral 411 stems/m², 214 g DW/m²; after removal 133–778 stems/m² and 40–64 g DW/m² (watershield, lily, *Chara*), *Chara* > 3,000 stems/m² at 3 sites. So ~2 vs ~12 stems per g DW by form (inf).
- Stahr & Shoup 2015, water willow (emergent; SURROGATE) [snippet]: lowered adult bass capture-to-attack ratio and captures; juvenile survival higher in vegetated trials.
- **Helfman 1981**, *Copeia* 1981:392 [secondary, textbook]: at 10 m horizontal visibility, a shaded observer detects sunlit objects at 12 m, a sunlit observer sees into shade at only 6 m. The advantage shrinks on overcast days, and lurking predators use shade too.
- Shirkey 1999, NIU thesis (bluegill vs largemouth bass) [abstract]: bluegill used shade > 70% of the time; > 90% when the bass was unshaded, ~60% when it was shaded. Vegetation was preferred to shaded open water (> 70%). Vegetation use > 50% at 1.5 and 340 lux, ~35% at 170 lux.

**Light and turbidity**
- Snickars 2004: the density effect appeared only in clear water [abstract].
- Reid et al. 1999: bass consumption unchanged to 37 NTU, clearly lower at 70 NTU [secondary]. Miner & Stein 1996: bass less active at 27–54 than 10–19 NTU [snippet].
- Carter et al. 2010, *TAFS* 139:353, **smallmouth bass**, 0–40 NTU: turbidity mattered more than cover; consumption fell as turbidity rose [snippet].
- Jacobsen et al. 2014, *J. Limnol.* 73:187 [primary, per the delegated reading]: 16-m² pools with a 5.2-m² dense artificial patch; perch 249 mm ate 2.1 ± 1.4 roach each at 12.5 roach/m² vs 0.4 ± 0.5 at 3.1/m²; the turbidity effect (25 vs 105 NTU) was marginal (p = 0.056).
- Hot Brook's water is low in turbidity (colour, not silt), so light acts through day/night, colour attenuation and shade (inf).

**The prey's price for hiding**
- Werner et al. 1983: one third of open-water feeding returns in vegetation; 27% less growth with bass present (in `macrophytes.md`).
- Manatunge et al. 2000 (*Env. Biol. Fish.*): a cyprinid planktivore fed less efficiently on *Daphnia* from 0 to 2,800 stems/m² [abstract].
- Wiley et al. 1984: bass grew best at intermediate biomass, 52 g DW/m² [snippet]. Bass growth declines above ~40% plant cover (Dibble's chapter) [secondary].

### Inferences
- **Interstices, not labels.** Dibble & Harrel's milfoil had few long mid-depth gaps (bass got in); Valley & Bremigan's milfoil canopy was dense at the top (a barrier). What protects is the gap structure in the prey's depth layer.
- **For the Hot Brook forms (inf):**
  - Lily, watershield and floating pondweed: petioles 19–210/m², far below the 50–250 step → stem refuge ≈ 0; overhead shade (Helfman's 2:1 detection) and the edge are the value.
  - Pickerelweed and bulrush: emergent stems in tens to a few hundred per m² (§3) → partial refuge in the 0–0.7 m band; the stiff culms of standing dead bulrush count in spring.
  - Large-leaf and ribbon-leaf pondweed: broad-leaved canopy, gaps ~15–35 mm where dense → the strongest refuge per stem, in the upper canopy.
  - Robbins' pondweed, *Nitella*, isoetids, moss: low turf (< 0.5 m) with dense small gaps → refuge only for fish hugging the bottom (killifish, young perch, sculpin-sized prey), little against a predator above the turf.
  - Bladderwort: fine, dissected and loose → refuge for larvae and invertebrates, weak against fish predators unless matted thick.
- **A refuge multiplier the sim could use (inf, from the delegated synthesis):** M = 1 − R_struct · B · W_edge · W_vis, with shade as a separate detection-distance term.
  - D_eff = stems in the prey's layer × A_form, A_form: simple/emergent stems 1.0; floating-leaf petioles 0.5; fine-leaved whole-column pondweed or stonewort 2.5; dissected canopy-former 2.5 in the canopy layer, 0.5 beneath it.
  - R_struct = R_max / (1 + e^(−(D_eff − D50)/s)): bass and perch D50 ≈ 175, s ≈ 25, R_max 0.95 (M ≈ 0.96 at 100, ≈ 0.10 at 250); pickerel D50 ≈ 600, s ≈ 100, R_max ≈ 0.6.
  - Optional: D50 × (predator TL / 300 mm)² for gap exclusion (Lynch & Johnson); M slightly > 1 near 100–150 stems/m² for small or ambush predators (Broderius).
  - B: the prey's hide output (structure protects users only).
  - W_edge: 0 at the bed edge rising to 1 by ~1 m (or one predator length) inside; no edge penalty for pickerel, plus an encounter bonus within ±1 m of the edge.
  - W_vis: 1 at ≤ 40 NTU, falling toward 0.5 by 70–100 NTU (Hot Brook: always ~1).
  - Shade: under floating leaves on a sunny day, a sunlit predator's detection distance × 0.5; relax to 1 when overcast or when the predator is also shaded.
- **Against the current sim (inf):** its 1/(1 + (S/150)³) is close to the bass logistic (½ at 150 vs 175, steep). It lacks: a separate, much higher threshold for pickerel; the per-form multiplier (lily stems count fully now, at `cover` 0.7–0.8); the layer (turf protects only at the bottom); the edge; and shade.

## 5. Hot Brook: what is known about its beds

All of this is in `Hot Brook populations and water/plant_species_hot_brook.md` and `macrophytes.md` §6; the structural points are gathered here.

- **Records.** The only plant records in either lake are *Nitella flexilis* (aff.) and *N. tenuissima* (aff.), collected on 15 July 2015 at Upper's south end, ~10 m from shore by the boat launch (R. S. Sleith & S. D. Gottschalk; NY, PH and MARY herbaria) [API, in `plant_species_hot_brook.md`]. Two species in one short stop suggest *Nitella* is not rare there.
- **No Lakes of Maine plant list.** The user checked on 2026-10-17: Lakes of Maine has no plant list for these lakes (user's memory note). This search's fetch of the page returned 403 and was not retried.
- **MDIFW sheets** (1949, revised 1965 and 2004) [read, in `macrophytes.md` §6]: shoreline "mostly rocky with some areas of sand … and mud but the lake bottom is mostly muddy"; a large sandy beach in Upper's NE; wetlands on Upper's S and W and Lower's N and E; "excellent spawning and adult habitat for bass, perch, and pickerel"; pickerel do well "as long as a lake contains relatively shallow, weedy habitat".
- **Surface-reaching vegetation from the aerial photos** (NAIP near-infrared; the project's `tools/hotbrook/shallows.py`) [inf, in `plant_species_hot_brook.md`]: **2.3 ha (0.6%) of Upper and 0.84 ha (0.3%) of Lower**. Every vegetated cell hugs the shore (median 15 m out; 90th percentile 45 m in Upper, 15 m in Lower) at model depths of 0.3–1.0 m. Clusters: Upper's south end by the inlet wetland (~96 of 184 cells), the coves between Upper's western points (38), Lower's east shore (17), its north end by the marsh (13) and its west shore (~14). Submerged beds are invisible in this method.
- **The user's observations** (memory; photos of 2024–2026): bulrush stands in shallow water in front of a sedge fringe; golden ribbon leaves (ribbon-leaf pondweed or bur-reed) and fine-leaved shoots in the brook's slow runs; a *Fontinalis*-like tuft on a riffle stone; a camp dock shore of cobble and gravel **with no rooted plants out to ~0.6 m**; shores "only a foot or two deep" that run out "to chest height before it gets deeper … where the water gets darker" (the photo shelf's edge, set at 1.3 m); sunfish caught in the cove between Upper's two long west-central points.
- **Not usable:** a July 2024 bass-forum post describing a lower lake "choked" with overlapping lilies and pondweed does not name the lake [snippet]; not attributed to Hot Brook.

### Inferences
- Hot Brook's surface-reaching beds (0.3–0.6% of lake area) sit at the very bottom of the Adirondack range for surface-mapped beds (3–39%; §1). That fits rocky, exposed shores and only a few sheltered coves. The lilies and emergents are a thin, patchy, shore-hugging fringe in the coves and by the inlet wetland.
- Most vegetated area must therefore be submerged and unseen: isoetid lawns on the sand and gravel shelf (0.2–1.1 m), pondweeds and bladderworts at 1–2.5 m on mud, and *Nitella* at 2–3.5 m. By §1's depth-band table that is ~20–25% of each lake's bed.
- The best cheap check: the user paddling the coves in August with a rake or a viewing bucket, noting per stop: depth, bottom type (rock, sand, muck), and what grows (none, lawn of rosettes, lilies, tall pondweed, low green turf).

## 6. Suggested rules for the sim

[inf] Proposals, to be walked through all four seasons before coding:
1. **Placement score per bed cell.** Light (the existing depth limit) × substrate fit (rosettes on sand or gravel; lilies, pickerelweed and pondweeds on muck; nothing rooted on cobble or boulder except scattered rosettes and moss) × exposure (wave-washed shores bare to ~0.6 m; sheltered coves vegetated from ~0.2 m) × the existing ice-scour band.
2. **Patchiness.** Seed beds as clumps from a heavy-tailed size distribution, linear along the shore. Target 1–2 large cove beds holding about half the vegetated area, a median bed of ~0.2–0.4 ha, and total cover per band as in §1. Check the result against ~20–25% of each lake's bed vegetated and lily/emergent area ≈ 0.3–0.6%.
3. **Refuge.** Replace the single stem rule with the §4 multiplier:
   - per-form architecture: lily petioles × 0.5; pondweed × 2.5;
   - the layer: turf protects only fish at the bottom;
   - a pickerel threshold ~600 stems/m² against bass and perch ~175;
   - an edge ramp over ~1 m;
   - shade under floating leaves halving a sunlit predator's detection distance.
