# Chain pickerel young of the year: diet by length, first-summer growth, prey, habitat, mortality (2026-10-18)

Companion to `pickerel_population_dynamics.md` and `esocid_first_year_survival.md` (same folder), which already hold the survival,
density and stock–recruitment numbers. This note adds the diet ontogeny, monthly growth, prey sizes and the habitat of age-0 pickerel.
Fallback species are labelled **[grass]**, **[redfin]** or **[pike]**. Anything unlabelled is chain pickerel (*Esox niger*).

**Tags.**
- [primary]: I read the full text of the source being cited. Old scans were read as OCR; numbers were checked against the prose.
- [secondary]: the number comes from a source I read that cites the original. Both are named.
- [abstract]: abstract only.
- [snippet]: search-engine snippet only.
- [inf]: my inference.

TL = total length, SL = standard length, FL = fork length. For pickerel, TL ≈ 1.12–1.15 × SL [inf, typical esocid ratio].

## Q1. Diet by length: when do they start eating fish, and when do fish dominate?

### Chain pickerel
- **Scott & Crossman 1973** (FRB Canada Bull. 184, pp. 370–373; built on northern-US populations and the Wich & Mullan 1958, McCabe 1958 and Carlander 1969 syntheses) [primary]:
  - newly hatched fish feed only on plankters for a week or more;
  - then they eat a wide variety of invertebrates, "mostly immature aquatic insects and some small fish";
  - after **102–152 mm (4–6 in)**, fish are the principal food, but crayfish are still eaten.
- **Meyers & Muncy 1962** (Severn River, MD, brackish; *Chesapeake Sci.* 3:125–128) [secondary via Crossman & Lewis 1973 annotation; snippet; not read]:
  - 220 pickerel, all but 8 young of the year;
  - invertebrates were the major food below **80 mm SL**, fish most of the diet above it;
  - they fed day and night;
  - growth was greater than in northern freshwater populations, and the main foods were different.
- **Flemer 1959** (Tuckahoe Creek, VA; *Virginia J. Sci.* 10:263) [secondary, Crossman & Lewis annotation]:
  - 64 fish;
  - **below 74 mm**: insects in 48 % of stomachs, fish in 30 %;
  - **above 74 mm**: fish 63 %, insects 16 %.
- **Raney 1942** (a small New York pond — probably Lincoln Pond, since McCabe 1958 tabulates 104 young from there [inf]; *J. Wildl. Manage.* 6:58–66) [secondary, Crossman & Lewis]:
  - **104 young pickerel** in summer: **insects were 64 % of food volume**;
  - 145 older fish (≥ 1 yr) with food: fish in 47.6 % of stomachs, crayfish in 44.8 %;
  - young bullheads were eaten more often than the more abundant sunfish and perch.
  - McCabe 1958 tabulates the stomach contents of these 104 young fish (not read).
- **Hunter & Rankin 1939** (*Copeia* 1939:194–199; locality not given in the annotation, probably Connecticut [inf]; chain and redfin pickerel combined) [secondary, Crossman & Lewis]:
  - **below 152 mm**: mainly Crustacea; above 152 mm, mainly fish;
  - the earlier literature had insects as the commonest food of young pickerel.
- **Kendall 1917** (*The pikes*, US Bur. Fish. Doc. 853, pp. 27–28; Maine and New Hampshire stomach records) [primary; anecdotal]:
  - **Sebago Lake**:
    - two fish of ~2¼ in (~57 mm) held small insect larvae and small crustaceans;
    - one < 2.5 in (< 64 mm) held a young sucker ~½ in (13 mm);
    - one 3.2 in (81 mm) held a pumpkinseed 0.9 in (23 mm), swallowed head first;
    - one 4.7 in (119 mm) held only insect larvae and amphipods;
    - one 5.8 in (147 mm) held one tiny fish.
  - **Umbagog Lake**: "many young pickerel ranging from 2 to 4 inches" (51–102 mm) fed **exclusively on Entomostraca and insect larvae**.
  - **Eight fish of 4.25–6.37 in (108–162 mm)**, caught together (place not stated): 6 held fish, and 4 of the prey fish were young pickerel. A further lot gave the prey-size records in Q3.
  - **Freeport, ME**: three adults of 11–15½ in held only aquatic insect larvae.
- **Foote & Blake 1945** (Babcock Pond, CT; *J. Wildl. Manage.* 9:89–96) [secondary, via Mitchell et al.; not read]:
  - the insect-to-fish transition spans **15–40 cm**;
  - Mitchell et al. contrast this with Meyers & Muncy's predominance of fish above 8 cm.
- **MDIFW Chain Pickerel Assessment** (2001, updated 2008) [primary for the earlier helper, quoted in `Hot Brook populations and water/fish_numbers_and_new_species.md`; the PDF returned 403 to me]:
  - young of the year reach "4 to 5 inches" by September–October;
  - they turn "strongly piscivorous" at **7–9 in**.
- **Nova Scotia** (Mitchell, LeBlanc & Heggelin, c. 2011, NS Fisheries report; sampled 2010) [primary]:
  - 17 pickerel of 14.5–54 cm;
  - 10 of 17 (58.8 %) held insects (mayfly nymphs, up to 11 in a stomach; Odonata); 2 (11.7 %) held fish;
  - these lakes had been depleted of small fish by the pickerel.
- **Agency summaries** [secondary, no source given]:
  - North Carolina WRC: insects and crustaceans until ~4 in (102 mm);
  - Texas PWD: "almost exclusively other fish" at ~3 in (76 mm), a southern population.

### Fallbacks with diet by length class
- **[grass] Kleinert & Mraz 1966** (Pleasant Lake, WI; Wis. Cons. Dept Tech. Bull. 37, p. 28 and Table 7; 351 fingerling stomachs; empty stomachs only at 30–80 mm, 11 of them) [primary]:
  - **9.5–15 mm**: mainly cladocerans (*Simocephalus*, *Bosmina*) and copepods (*Cyclops*), occasionally ostracods.
  - **15–40 mm**: cladocerans and copepods still eaten, with chironomid ("tendipedid") larvae, Odonata nymphs and some fish.
  - **40–80 mm**: zooplankters rare; almost entirely chironomid larvae, Odonata and Ephemeroptera nymphs, small fish and *Hyalella* (amphipod).
  - Table 7 (OCR, alignment uncertain): at 10–33 mm a stomach held a few cladocerans and ~1–18 copepods.
  - Stomach counts stayed similar from year to year although May slough cladocerans ran 0.2, 10.2 and 38.4 per L and copepods ≤ 37 per L. Franklin & Smith 1963 saw the same in pike. Young pickerel were not zooplankton-limited at these densities [inf].
  - 12 pike fingerlings of 12.5–20.8 mm ate the same food as grass pickerel of the same length.
- **[grass] Crossman 1962** (Jones Creek, ON; ROM Contrib. 55; 387 stomachs) [primary]:
  - **20–50 mm**: Cladocera, Amphipoda, Ostracoda, Odonata; less often Diptera, Plecoptera, Hemiptera, Isopoda.
  - **50–100 mm**: fish appear, but the diet is mainly Trichoptera, Odonata and crayfish. **The smallest fish-eater was 54.0 mm TL.**
  - **Above 100 mm**: almost entirely fish and crayfish; dragonfly nymphs still taken by the largest.
  - Up to 57 % of stomachs were empty in a month, and all October stomachs were empty.
- **[redfin] Scott & Crossman 1973** [primary]: young of the year under 50 mm eat only invertebrates: cladocerans, amphipods, isopods, and dragonfly, mayfly and caddisfly nymphs. Larger fish eat mostly fish.
- **[grass] Midkiff 1997** (MS thesis, Marshall Univ., West Virginia) [abstract; the full text sits behind a Cloudflare check and was not opened]:
  - cladocerans and copepods dominate in larvae;
  - juveniles eat large aquatic insect nymphs (odonates, corixids) and small fish;
  - growth **1.10 mm/d**.
- **[pike] Hunt & Carbine 1951** (Houghton Lake, MI; *Trans. Am. Fish. Soc.* 80:67–83) [abstract]:
  - 551 young pike of 11–152 mm;
  - a definite succession: Entomostraca → insects (chiefly Chironomidae) → vertebrates (fish, tadpoles);
  - smallest cannibal 21 mm; 13.3 % of 354 pike ≥ 21 mm had eaten pike;
  - **yellow perch the most important fish predator of young pike.**
- **[pike] Inskip 1982** (USFWS HSI FWS/OBS-82/10.17, citing Hunt & Carbine 1951, Franklin & Smith 1963 and Frost 1954) [snippet of the scan]:
  - feeding begins ~10 d after hatching at 10–12 mm;
  - **fish are most of the diet by 50–60 mm, typically 4–5 weeks after hatching** in the northern Midwest.
- **[pike] Scott & Crossman 1973** [primary]:
  - larger zooplankton and some insects for 7–10 d after yolk absorption, then small fish;
  - fish predominate by **2 in (50 mm)**;
  - optimum prey size is ⅓–½ of pike length.
- **[pike] Skov et al. 2003** (*Hydrobiologia* 506:481–487; 936 age-0 pike of 2–18 cm, a small eutrophic Danish lake) [abstract]:
  - micro-crustaceans → insects → macro-crustaceans → fish;
  - **fewer than 50 % were piscivorous until 10.1–13.7 cm**, despite abundant 0+ roach, which used anti-predator behaviour.
- **[pike] Bry et al. 1995** (*J. Fish Biol.* 46:99–113; outdoor ponds with no other fish) [abstract]:
  - by weight: micro-crustaceans at 13 mm → chironomid larvae at 74–99 mm → macro-crustaceans at 121–139 mm;
  - cannibalism first appeared at 74 mm.

### Synthesis [inf]
- **Zooplankton** dominates from first feeding (~10 mm) to ~15–20 mm, is still eaten to ~40 mm, and has gone by ~40–50 mm.
- **Insects** (chironomid larvae, then odonate, mayfly and caddis nymphs) and **amphipods** (*Hyalella*; Kendall's Maine brook fish) dominate from ~20 to ~80 mm.
- **First fish** at ~20–55 mm. The sucker in a < 64-mm Sebago fish and the fish in K&M's 15–40-mm class put a Maine onset at **~40–60 mm**, i.e. **late June–mid July** on the growth path below.
- **Fish dominate** where small fish are plentiful, at **~80–100 mm TL** (Maryland, Virginia; Kendall's 108–162-mm fish: 6 of 8 had eaten fish).
- **Pike are faster**: fish dominate at 50–60 mm.
- **Invertebrates stay important for years** in clear, soft, fish-poor northern waters. The Maine assessment says 7–9 in; Connecticut 15–40 cm; Nova Scotia insect-dominated adults; Massachusetts pickerel ≤ 30 cm ate 40–65 % crayfish (already in `species.js`).
- A Hot Brook young of the year in its first fall (100–127 mm) should therefore be a **mixed feeder**: roughly half fish by mass when small fish are at hand, mostly insects and amphipods where they are not.

**Maine monthly targets for age-0 chain pickerel [inf]** (Hot Brook calendar: ice-out ~23 Apr; spawning at 8–11 °C in late April–early May; hatch in 6–12 d; first feeding ~mid-May):

| Date | TL (mm) | Daily growth |
|---|---|---|
| mid-May (first feeding) | ~10 | — |
| end of May | 13–18 | 0.3–0.5 mm/d (cold, larval) |
| end of June | **25–40** | ~0.5–0.7 mm/d |
| end of July | **55–75** | ~0.9–1.1 mm/d (warmest water; piscivory starting) |
| end of August | **80–105** | ~0.9–1.0 mm/d |
| end of September | **100–130** | ~0.6–0.8 mm/d (cooling) |

- That is a season mean of ~0.65–0.9 mm/d over ~138 d.
- Growth is food-driven. Carbine's minnow ditch put fish 33 mm ahead in a month. Underhill 1949 stresses large differences between years and water bodies [secondary].
- So **3 cm is reached in late June, not early June, and 8 cm in mid-to-late August.** The hazard path in `pickerel_population_dynamics.md` (3 cm on 8 Jun, 8 cm on 15 Jul) is ~3–5 weeks ahead of the New York data.

## Q3. Prey fish and prey:predator length ratio

**Prey eaten by young pickerel**
- **Maine and New Hampshire** (Kendall 1917) [primary]: a young white sucker (13 mm), a pumpkinseed (23 mm), young pickerel (52–76 mm), a shiner (64 mm), "small minnows", a hornpout (brown bullhead, 38 mm) and a small chub.
- **[grass] Wisconsin** (K&M) [primary]: blackchin shiner, lake chubsucker and smaller pickerel.
- **[grass] Ontario, all sizes** (Crossman 1962) [primary]. Prey by number of stomachs: central mudminnow 89, golden shiner 19, grass pickerel 9, white sucker 9, pumpkinseed 6, common shiner 5, creek chub 1, yellow perch 1, johnny darter 1. That is 9 of 22 available species; mudminnow was preferred over more abundant fish.

**Other ratios**
- [grass] K&M [primary]: a 37-mm fingerling (probably fall-hatched) held 13 pickerel fry; with 22 fry sampled alongside, they measured 6.5–11.5 mm, giving **0.18–0.31**.
- [grass] Crossman [primary]: the largest prey was a 134-mm golden shiner in a 228-mm FL fish (~0.5 of TL).
- [pike] Bry et al. 1992 (*Env. Biol. Fish.* 35:75–84; pond juveniles) [abstract]:
  - mean cannibal prey:predator length **66.2 %**;
  - mouth size set the maximum.
- [pike] Fingerling cannibals took prey of 42–73 % of their own length (Zaikov et al. 2006) [snippet].
- [pike] Optimum prey size ⅓–½ of pike length (S&C) [primary].
- Chain pickerel "does not hesitate to seize a fish at least half as large as itself" (Kendall) [primary].

**Rule [inf]:** age-0 pickerel take elongate prey (pickerel, shiners, suckers) up to **~0.5–0.7 of their length**, and deep-bodied centrarchids up to **~0.3**. This is consistent with gape limitation by prey depth.
- The sim's pickerel `gapeR` 0.13 against pumpkinseed `depthR` 0.38 allows pumpkinseed up to 0.34 × TL, which fits Kendall's 0.28.
- Against pickerel `depthR` 0.14 it allows up to 0.93 × TL; the observed maximum is 0.71 [inf].

**Hot Brook prey for 40–120-mm pickerel, June–September [inf]:**
- white sucker larvae and young of the year (inshore schools, June);
- yellow perch young of the year (littoral by July, ~20–40 mm);
- pumpkinseed and redbreast young of the year (from July, 10–25 mm);
- banded killifish;
- fallfish and dace young of the year in and near the brook;
- dispersing smallmouth fry (~25–30 mm, once guarding ends);
- younger pickerel.

## Q4. Habitat of age-0 pickerel by size
- **Hatch to ~10 mm:** the fry sink and attach to vegetation by an adhesive snout gland for about a week, until the yolk is absorbed (S&C [primary]).
- **Very young fish:** "find their way into the shallowest waters and mouths of brooks entering the lake" (Kendall 1917, Maine [primary]). This is relevant to the Hot Brook connecting brook and its mouth pools [inf].
- **[grass] Wisconsin nursery** (K&M [primary]):
  - young are caught at the east edge of a shallow slough with a moss bottom, where eggs and fry had been densest;
  - by early summer the slough is "choked" with vegetation;
  - young of the year stay in the nursery bay and slough through the first summer, "showing little dispersal" — 95.8 % of fish under 7 in came from the 15-acre vegetated East Bay in 1963;
  - the electroshocker could not work water < 2 ft deep or dense weeds, "areas where fingerling pickerel are abundant".
- **[grass] Ontario** (Crossman 1962 [primary]):
  - fish **≤ 4 in (102 mm) lived in the stream's vegetation**, larger ones in the pond expansions;
  - they move very little and only short distances;
  - by day: stationary, almost motionless in **shoreline vegetation ~3 ft (0.9 m) from shore, facing shoreward**, for ≥ 30 min at a time;
  - **at night, 2–3-in (51–76-mm) fish held beside stones right at the shore in 3–5 in (8–13 cm) of water**;
  - fry are wary and fast by day, but can be caught by hand at night.
- S&C [primary]: young of the year live in the shallows along shore, older fish in deeper water [grass]. Chain pickerel are "solitary fish, hiding almost motionless in the vegetation most of the time"; in summer adults hold a station or territory.
- MDIFW [via earlier notes]: pickerel are associated with weed beds, lily pads and bulrushes; largemouth bass prey on young pickerel confined to weed patches.
- **[pike]**:
  - fish under ~14 cm stay in dense vegetation that larger pike cannot reach (Grimm, via `esocid_first_year_survival.md`);
  - Upper Mississippi backwaters (Holland & Huston 1984, *N. Am. J. Fish. Manage.* 4:514–522) [snippet]: spring catches in submerged vegetation ~3× those in emergent and > 10× those in unvegetated water; by late summer they shifted to less vegetated, better-oxygenated water;
  - St Lawrence River nursery bays in fall (Walton-Rabideau et al. 2019, *Ecol. Freshw. Fish*) [primary abstract and partial read]: age-0 pike gathered in the **deepest, most heavily vegetated** part of the bay, and in the deepest parts as water levels fell;
  - Houghton Lake: few pike left the ditches below 20 mm; once all had passed 20 mm, departure was unrelated to size (Carbine 1941 [primary]).
- **Solitary from small sizes [inf from observations]:**
  - no source describes schooling at any size;
  - fish are seen singly in vegetation from the fingerling stage (Crossman);
  - cannibalism from ~21 mm (pike) and ~37 mm (grass pickerel) enforces spacing;
  - but they **aggregate in good nursery patches**: K&M's dip-net "blind stabbing"; Kendall's 8 fish caught "at the same time and in the same place".
  - The sim should draw them as dispersed individuals at nursery-patch densities, never as schools.

## Q5. Mortality, swim-up to fall
- **No published daily mortality or survival estimate for age-0 chain pickerel was found.** This matches the earlier helper's search. Use the pike and grass pickerel numbers in `esocid_first_year_survival.md` and the targets in `pickerel_population_dynamics.md`:
  - swim-up → fall ~1–2 %;
  - larval weeks Z ≈ 0.06–0.12/d;
  - juvenile weeks Z ≈ 0.012–0.025/d.
- **Causes, chain pickerel:**
  - **Largemouth bass:** in experimental Illinois ponds, "pickerel fry and fingerlings are highly vulnerable to predation by largemouth bass". Age-0 and age-1 pickerel "compete poorly" with largemouth of the same age and "may be more compatible with smallmouth" (Buck & Thoits 1967, IL DJ report, via Crossman & Lewis [secondary]). Hot Brook's bass are smallmouth [inf: a lower predation load on young pickerel than in largemouth lakes]. MDIFW also calls largemouth serious predators of young pickerel [via earlier note].
  - **Other predators (S&C [primary]):** basses, sunfishes and yellow perch; also frogs, mergansers, grebes, loons, kingfishers and herons. Fish over ~150 mm in acid water have few predators but other pickerel.
  - **Cannibalism:**
    - Kendall's Maine/New Hampshire lot: of 8 fish of 108–162 mm, 6 held fish, and **4 of those prey were young pickerel**. A second lot had 4 more pickerel-in-pickerel cases. These are anecdotal samples, probably from crowded patches;
    - Pennsylvania hatchery: cannibalism noted once fingerlings reach 2 in (Anon. 1968b, via Crossman & Lewis [secondary]);
    - S&C: in crowded situations "considerable cannibalism may take place".
    - Contrast [grass]: 7 of 351 young-of-year stomachs (2 %) in Wisconsin; 9 of 387 in Ontario; "not extensive" (K&M; Crossman) [primary].
  - **Stranding and nursery loss** [grass]: falling water cut the slough off by early July and the stranded fingerlings died, a 3–4× smaller year class (K&M [primary]). S&C list stranding by receding water as a limiting factor for chain pickerel too.
- **[pike] stage pattern:**
  - Bry et al. 1995 ponds [abstract]: mortality peaked at **13–27 mm** (early larva), **46–99 mm** and **121–139 mm**, the latter two tied to cannibalism developing from 74 mm. Biomass rose from 19.8 kg/ha at 46 mm to 181.8 kg/ha at 121 mm, then levelled.
  - Bry et al. 1992 [abstract]: at ~100 mm, cannibals were **7.8 %** of juveniles at low density and **41.3 %** at high density (15.5 % and 65.9 % of biomass).
  - Hunt & Carbine 1951 [abstract]: competition, predation (yellow perch first) and cannibalism caused the high mortality in the first few weeks.
  - Franklin & Smith 1963 [abstract]: two critical periods, embryo (fertilization → blastopore closure) and hatch → end of alevin. Growth and food in the nursery did not appear to affect survival; stable water levels for ≥ 3 months after spawning did.

## For the sim [inf]
Current pickerel settings in `src/species.js`, also used by `src/w3/fish3.js`: `Lzoo` 4, `Lpisc` 8, `gapeR` 0.13, insects below 3 × `Lpisc` = 24 cm.

1. **`Lzoo` 4 cm fits** the literature: zooplankton is gone by ~40 mm.
2. **`Lpisc` 8 cm is late for the onset but right for dominance.**
   - First fish come at ~4–6 cm (Maine, Wisconsin, Ontario).
   - If `Lpisc` is a hard gate, **5–6 cm** matches the onset. That would move the insect cut-off to 15–18 cm, still inside the MDIFW / S&C range (fish principal by 10–15 cm; "strongly piscivorous" at 18–23 cm).
   - Alternatively keep 8 cm and accept that the young-of-year switch is delayed ~3–4 weeks.
   - Judge with the young-of-year fate audit, ≥ 3 seeds.
3. **Growth checkpoints:** 25–40 mm at end of June; 55–75 at end of July; 80–105 at end of August; 100–130 at end of September.
   - Fry reaching 8 cm before ~1 August grow faster than New York or Maine fish.
   - Fry still below 8 cm at the end of September are too slow, unless food is limiting.
4. **Habitat:** age-0 fish should stay in the weedy littoral within a few metres of shore, ≤ ~1 m deep, solitary but patch-aggregated. The brook mouth and its shallows should be a nursery. Move to cover at night in very shallow water (Crossman).
5. **Prey ratio:** keep depth-based gape limitation. Observed maxima are ~0.3 (sunfish) and ~0.7 (pickerel) of predator TL.
6. **Cannibalism:** density-dependent; ~2 % of stomachs in uncrowded grass pickerel, but common in Kendall's crowded Maine/New Hampshire samples (most of the fish prey were pickerel). This is consistent with the 10–30 % share of age-0 deaths targeted in `pickerel_population_dynamics.md`.
