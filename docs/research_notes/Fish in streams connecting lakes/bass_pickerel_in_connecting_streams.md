# Smallmouth bass and chain pickerel in small lake-connecting streams, and predators at stream mouths and outlet pools

*Collected 2026-10-19 for the Hot Brook brook, about 200 m × 8 m, joining Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake. It has a pool of about 1.1 m at Upper's outlet, a run of about 0.5 m and riffles of about 0.3 m. The earlier brook note gives about 150 m for the length. Using 200 m, the brook covers about 1,600 m².*

*This note does not repeat earlier work. The lake-side seasonal habits of these species are in [perch_pickerel_smallmouth_pumpkinseed.md](../Seasonal%20fish%20habitat%20use/perch_pickerel_smallmouth_pumpkinseed.md). The brook's riffle refuges, the rule that large bass sit in pools deeper than 50 cm (Power 1987), Schlosser's 1987 refuge experiment and the low-flow sequence are in [brook_food_web.md](../Whole%20lake%203D%20fish%20simulation/brook_food_web.md) §4–5.*

## 1. Smallmouth bass in lake inlets, outlets and connecting streams: spawning runs, summer residence, feeding visits, winter return

### Takeaway
- **Lake smallmouth often move into tributaries or connecting rivers in spring to spawn.** Within one population, river spawners and lake spawners coexist. Males keep to one or the other for life and re-nest within 20 m of their old nest.
- **Some tributary spawners stay in the stream all summer,** downstream of the nest sites (Lake Ontario). Others go back to the lake. In the ship canal off Lake Washington, 82% of bass moved into the lake between June and October. Fish under 350 mm were the likeliest to stay in the channel.
- **In streams, adults are pool-bound in summer:**
  - 76% are recaptured in the same pool;
  - net movements are under 1 km, often under 200 m;
  - median linear home range is 0.3 km in a tributary;
  - depth is 0.7–1.8 m and focal velocity under 0.20 m/s.
- **Use of depth by age matches the brook's habitat units almost one-to-one:** age-0 at about 0.34 m (riffles and margins), juveniles at about 0.57 m (the run), adults at 0.85 m or more (the 1.1 m pool).
- **Stream bass leave for winter habitat once the water falls below about 15.6 °C, especially on rain and rising flow.** Young-of-year and age-1 fish do not make those migrations.
- **The angler's question ("live there, or only spawn?") is answered by "both, in different individuals".**

### Cited findings

**Spawning runs from lakes into tributaries and connecting rivers**
- **Lake Ontario tributaries** (a SUNY record, probably the New York shore; radio telemetry with temperature-sensing tags, 1985–86) [snippet of the published record; direct fetch 403]:
  - 56 stream-spawning smallmouth and rock bass were tagged near spawning areas in two tributaries. Some were released at home and some were displaced into the lake.
  - About **25% of displaced smallmouth** (42% of rock bass) homed to their tributary capture sites.
  - Smallmouth that homed to, or were released in, tributaries **"usually set up home ranges downstream of spawning areas and often stayed in tributaries all summer"**. Rock bass did not.
  - Fish that did not home dispersed along the shore and settled into small lake residence areas, often near their home tributary or another one.
  - Both species occupied 20–22 °C water when it was available.
  - Smallmouth were **most active in May and least in July**, and **more active in the lake than in tributaries**.
  - *J. Freshwater Ecology* 4(4), Dec 1988 (authors not shown in the snippet) — [SUNY SOAR record](https://soar.suny.edu/entities/publication/cef37268-63b2-4fbf-9e14-87e9fea7c510)
- **Millers Lake and the Mississippi River, Ontario** (a shallow warmwater tributary of the Ottawa River) [abstract/project summary]:
  - **Setting:** a 45-ha lake, 25 m deep at most, and a 1.5-km river section of **8 pools separated by riffles and rapids**. A 2-m waterfall upstream blocks passage; the downstream rapids are a lesser barrier.
  - **Fish:** 29 adults tagged from May 2000, 285–486 mm TL (mean 372).
  - **Two tactics:**
    - lake residents all year;
    - "potamodromous" fish that moved **lake → river spawning habitat in spring and returned to the lake before winter**.
  - **Fidelity:** most males were faithful to river or lake for reproduction across their lives, and **about half of repeat spawners nested within 20 m of their previous nest**.
  - River and lake spawners differed in age and size at maturity and in nesting success; the summary does not give the direction.
  - Barthel et al. 2008, *J. Fish Biol.* 73:829 — [QUBS summary](https://research.qubs.ca/projects/divergent-life-histories-among-smallmouth-bass-inhabiting-a-connected-river-lake-system)
- **Lake Erie, NY** [agency; snippet]:
  - Smallmouth "start moving into tributary streams in mid to late April and will stay through May".
  - The seasonal sequence is tributaries (April–May) → harbours and canals (May) → open-lake reefs and shoals (May–June).
  - Named runs: Cattaraugus, Eighteen Mile, Canadaway and Chautauqua creeks.
  - [NYSDEC Lake Erie smallmouth](https://dec.ny.gov/things-to-do/freshwater-fishing/places-to-fish/great-lakes-niagara-river-st-lawrence-river/lake-erie-smallmouth-bass-fishing)
- **Erie County, PA** [press fishing reports quoting PFBC; snippet]: "creek-spawning" smallmouth had laid eggs, and stream spawners "continued their run" on select tributaries in May–June 2022. — [Pittsburgh Post-Gazette report](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/pittsburgh-post-gazette/20220612/283845807050007)
- **Upper Mississippi River, MN (river, not lake)** [agency; snippet]:
  - Spring runs of up to 29 km to spawning areas, and fall runs of up to 27 km.
  - Spawning sites had slow current over gravel and sand.
  - Only one tracked fish appeared to spawn in a tributary.
  - Summer home ranges were **0.4–19.0 ha**. Riffles, backwaters and tributaries were used less than eddies, pools and runs.
  - [MN DNR telemetry report](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/areas/fisheries/saukrapids/mississippiriversmbtelemetry.pdf)
- **Review (Lyons & Kanehl 2002)** [via a forum quoting the review; the original was not read]:
  - Many populations make spring spawning migrations, "usually to smaller tributaries".
  - "Nearly all stream dwelling smallmouth bass populations are largely sedentary during the summer."
  - Fall migrations in northern Wisconsin covered **35–109 km**, beginning **below 60 °F (15.6 °C)**, "particularly when accompanied by rainfall and increased flows".
  - **"Young of the year and age 1 fish did not migrate."** Many adults returned in spring to the area they used the previous summer.
  - [paflyfish thread quoting Lyons & Kanehl 2002, AFS Symp. 31:149–160](https://paflyfish.com/threads/stream-smallmouth-bass-migratory-behavior.14680)

**A channel between two lakes** (surrogate system: Lake Washington Ship Canal, WA, introduced smallmouth) [snippet of the published abstract and AFS 2011 abstract]
- **Tagging:** 72 bass were tagged. Of the 57 tracked through the seasons, **82% migrated from the canal to Lake Washington sometime between June and October**.
- **Size:** **smaller bass (< 350 mm) were more likely to overwinter in the canal.**
- **Return:** bass came back into the canal **from March to mid-April**.
- **Isolation:** the authors suggest canal bass may be reproductively separate from bass that live in the lake all year.
- [Tabor, Celedonia et al. 2012, *Northwest Science* 86:131 (BioOne; did not render)](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/Northwest-Science/volume-86/issue-2/046.086.0205/Seasonal-Movements-of-Smallmouth-Bass-in-the-Lake-Washington-Ship/10.3955/046.086.0205.full)

**Summer residence in streams: pools, site fidelity, home range**
All items below are from Hafs 2007's literature review and results [full text]. [Hafs 2007 MS thesis, Arkansas Tech](https://www.bemidjistate.edu/directory/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2023/02/Hafs-A.W.-2007.-Smallmouth-bass-survival-movement-and-habitat-use-in-response-to-seasonally-discontinuous-surface-flow.-Ma.pdf)
- **Homing and pool fidelity:**
  - **Larimore 1952** (Jordan Creek, IL): bass of 180–380 mm displaced 1–1.3 km showed homing behaviour and "a strong tendency to remain in home pools".
  - **Fajen 1962**: 433 recaptures of 180 fish over 127 mm in two small Ozark streams. Bass "normally restricted their movements to one distinct pool".
  - **Munther 1970** (Snake River, ID/OR; 122–480 mm): **99 recaptures (76%) were in the same pool**. Of 31 found elsewhere, 22 had moved under 1.2 km, 3 had moved 1.6–2.8 km and 6 more than 2.8 km.
- **Home range:**
  - **Bare 2005** (59 radio-tagged fish, Arkansas): median 95%-kernel linear home range was **0.3 km for Bear Creek (a tributary) residents**, 0.7 km for Buffalo River residents and 3.2 km for fish using both.
  - **Lyons & Kanehl 2002** (5 fish, Otter Creek and the Pecatonica River, WI): no fish moved more than **200 m** up or down from its original location in summer.
  - **VanArnum et al. 2004** (Kentucky): in 2000, 20 of 39 fish moved under 2 km, 13 moved 4–10 km and 6 more than 15 km. In 2001, all 15 moved under 4 km.
- **Depth:**
  - Bare 2005: adults most often at **1.4–1.8 m in the Buffalo River and 0.8–1.0 m in Bear Creek**.
  - Todd & Rabeni 1989 (Jacks Fork River, MO; 34 fish): **0.7–1.0 m** at all times of day and all seasons. They used rootwads by day and boulders by night in warm seasons, and boulders almost only in cool seasons.
  - **Aadland 1993 (six Minnesota streams): adults at a mean of 0.85 m, juveniles 0.57 m, age-0 0.34 m.**
- **Velocity:**
  - Todd & Rabeni: adults preferred **< 0.20 m/s**.
  - Aadland: adults at a mean of 0.24 m/s.
  - Fore et al. 2007 (Oklahoma, fish of 100 mm or more): 0.82 m deep and 0.17 m/s.
  - Rankin 1986 (Michigan): < 0.15 m/s.
  - Lab: about 0.10 m/s.
- **Survival:** annual survival in streams is **84–89% with light or no fishing** and 44–58% with heavy fishing (Reed & Rabeni 1989, a review of 15 studies).

**Juveniles and dispersal from natal tributaries** (James River, VA, otolith strontium plus genetics; river system) [abstracts; snippet]
- **Fish:** movement histories of 220 fish were reconstructed.
- **Movement:** **half moved between rivers** at some point in life. Most left their natal river **in their first year, a few months after spawning**.
- **Distance:** adults were caught on average 8.3 km from their natal tributary, and parent–offspring dispersal was 7.7 km.
- **Lakes versus rivers:** the authors suggest lake populations disperse over smaller scales than river populations because their seasonal habitats are closer together.
- [Humston et al., VT thesis/abstract](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/106777); [VCAFS 2020 slides](https://oldunitsstaging.fisheries.org/va/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2020/02/4-1_Humston_VCAFS2020.pdf)

**Stream nests and floods** [primary review; snippet]: production and survival of young smallmouth depend on different factors in lakes and streams. Stream nests "are threatened by disruptive flooding during the spawning season". — [Forbes 1981 (WI DNR review)](https://sitesreservoirproject.riptideweb.com/references/REF17/Appx_12B_FisheriesImpactAssessment/Forbes_1981.pdf)

### Inferences
- **"Live in the brook" or "only spawn there" is a false choice.** Coexisting resident and migrant tactics within one population (Barthel 2008) and summer residence by tributary spawners (Lake Ontario 1988) mean Hot Brook should hold:
  1. **a few lake bass that spawn in the brook in May–June and some of which stay all summer**, downstream of their nests;
  2. **sub-adults that use it as a summer range**; small fish were the ones that stayed in the LWSC channel, and Aadland's juvenile depths match the run;
  3. **lake residents that only visit to feed**.
  
  Fidelity is individual and lasting, so in the sim a fish's brook use could be a heritable or learned trait, not a population-wide schedule.
- **The whole brook is about one stream home range.** At 200 m long it equals the Wisconsin summer range (no fish beyond 200 m) and is below the 0.3-km median for a small tributary. A resident brook bass would treat the entire brook, plus probably the lake water at either mouth, as its home range.
- **Depth sets which sizes can live where:**
  - The 1.1-m outlet pool is the only adult-depth habitat (adults sit at 0.7–1.8 m, and large bass in pools deeper than 50 cm per Power 1987).
  - The 0.5-m run suits juveniles, and the 0.3-m riffles suit age-0.
  - This matches the angler: big bass in the deep pool, smaller bass staying in the brook.
- **Calendar for the sim** (my scaling of the cited timings to the Maine calendar):
  - In Lake Erie, bass enter tributaries in mid–late April, roughly 3–4 weeks before the May–June nesting.
  - Hot Brook nests at about 15–16 °C (existing note), which falls in late May on the sim's calendar, so the run into the brook should start in early-to-mid May. No temperature trigger for the run itself was found.
  - Spawners are in the brook from about late May to late June.
  - Summer residence runs from June to September.
  - The exodus to the lakes comes below about 15.6 °C or on autumn rain and rising flow, in late September or October.
  - **No adult should winter in a 1.1-m brook under ice:** lake bass are quiescent at wintering sites 2–14 m deep (existing note).
  - Young-of-year may stay longer because they do not migrate, but a brook that freezes towards the bed would force them into the pool or a lake. Whether they survive is unknown.
- **Activity:** bass were less active in tributaries than in the lake, and least active in July. Summer brook residents should be "sit-and-wait" fish, which suits the outlet-pool ambush described in §3.

### Gaps
- **No study of smallmouth in a short (≤ 1 km) brook joining two natural lakes was found,** in Maine, New England or anywhere else. Every number above comes from Great Lakes tributaries, a river–lake pair (Millers Lake), a ship canal or larger rivers.
- **Density of adult or juvenile smallmouth per m² or per pool** in small lake-connecting streams was not found.
- **The 1988 Lake Ontario tributary paper:** authors, distances from the lake and the number staying all summer were not read (403).
- **Lyons & Kanehl 2002, Barthel et al. 2008 and Tabor et al. 2012:** the full texts were not read. The direction of river-versus-lake spawner differences in age, size and success is unknown.
- **Nest-site numbers for small-stream smallmouth** (depth, velocity, substrate, distance to cover) were not retrieved for streams. The lake values are in the existing note.

## 2. Chain pickerel (and pike and grass pickerel as surrogates) in small streams and lake outlets

### Takeaway
- **Direct data on chain pickerel in streams are thin.** Agencies place them in pools of creeks and in sluggish, vegetated streams with wood. They leave stream channels in floods.
- **In Nova Scotia, some lake-tagged pickerel made "extended summer migrations beyond" their lake,** out through connecting waters. That is the only telemetry hint of summer stream use, and it has no numbers.
- **The surrogates point the same way: a mostly sedentary majority, with a mobile, larger minority.**
  - Grass pickerel in an Ontario drain: most moved under 500 m, 16% moved more than 500 m (up to 13.5 km), and the mobile fish were larger.
  - Northern pike in a small river: stayed within about 1 river mile of the capture pool, moving pool to pool through riffles, most active after dusk.
- **Pike make mass spring spawning runs into tiny lake inlets.** In one 0.25-mile ditch, 438 adults ran up over 24 days, mostly at night. Some lingered up to about 7 weeks, and 7,239 fry drifted back to the lake.
- **Piscivores rarely follow migrating prey into streams in winter:** under 2% of pike and perch did so.

### Cited findings

**Chain pickerel habitat in streams**
- **West Virginia** [agency; snippet]: "prefers small stream pool habitats", and also uses backwaters and floodplain wetlands with abundant vegetation and wood. — [WVDNR](https://wvdnr.gov/plants-animals/sportfish/chain-pickerel/)
- **Pickerel in streams more generally** [agency; snippet]: found in "relatively sluggish rivers and streams" with heavy vegetation. They prefer large streams, rivers, reservoirs and swamps with minimal flow. — [Outdoor Alabama](https://www.outdooralabama.com/pickerel/chain-pickerel); [ADW](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Esox_niger/)
- **Floods and spawning (Texas profile)** [snippet; cites a 1983 study]:
  - Pickerel **move out of stream channels during flooding** (into vegetated, quiet water).
  - Spawning runs from late winter to spring, usually at **8.3–11.1 °C**, with eggs scattered over vegetation or detritus, no nest and no care.
  - [Fishes of Texas: Esox niger](https://txstate.fishesoftexas.org/esox%20niger.htm)
- **Ambush cover:** pickerel ambush from undercut banks, logs and weeds. They become piscivorous at 18–23 cm (MDIFW). See [brook_food_web.md §4](../Whole%20lake%203D%20fish%20simulation/brook_food_web.md).
- **George Lake, Kejimkujik NP, Nova Scotia** (invasive pickerel) [abstract, full text of the 1-page abstract]:
  - 61 fish (31–62 cm TL) were acoustic-tagged, and 30 of them (tagged 2021) were tracked for 12 months.
  - Behaviour included **"extended summer migrations beyond George Lake"** and "periods of inactivity in the winter", with distinct habitat use by individuals.
  - No counts, distances or routes are given.
  - Sauriol, Nilsen & Avery 2024 — [abstract PDF](https://mtri.speciesatrisk.ca/sites/merseytobeatic.ca/files/agendafiles/Sauriol_Felixe.pdf)
- **Spread in Nova Scotia** [agency; snippet]: introduced to a few lakes in the 1940s, pickerel are now in more than 90 waterbodies. Spread happens through illegal stocking and "dispersal within watersheds … contingent on connectivity of adjoining or adjacent waterways". No rate of spread through streams is given. — [NS Fisheries report on pickerel impacts](https://novascotia.ca/fish/documents/special-management-areas-reports/impact-chain-pickerel.pdf); [DFO](https://www.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/publications/habitat/highlights-faitssaillants/nova-scotia-nouvelle-ecosse/managing-invasive-species-gerer-especes-envanhissantes-eng.html)

**Grass pickerel (*E. americanus vermiculatus*) [surrogate], Beaver Creek watershed, Niagara Peninsula, ON** (agricultural drains; 2009–2013; about 2,000 fish PIT-tagged; mark–recapture plus fixed antennas over 37.3 km²) [snippet of the article; Springer redirected to a login]
- **Most moved within 500 m; 16% moved more than 500 m.** Movement distances of mobile fish ranged 0.43–13.5 km (median 1.89 km).
- **Mobile fish were larger** than stationary ones.
- **Sites with higher abundance had more mobile fish,** which suggests density dependence.
- Present riverscape barriers may prevent long dispersals.
- *Movement Ecology* 2023 — [doi 10.1186/s40462-023-00420-2](https://movementecologyjournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40462-023-00420-2)

**Northern pike (*E. lucius*) [surrogate]**
- **Spawning run into a lake inlet ditch: Houghton Lake, MI, 1939** [full text, OCR] — [Carbine 1940, Mich. IFR Report 583](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR0501-RR0600/RR0583.pdf)
  - **The ditch:** the main ditch is about **¼ mile (400 m)** long, entering North Bay, with side ditches up to 1¼ miles. Spring high water floods the surrounding marsh.
  - **The run:**
    - Spawners ran up over **24 days**, at most **53 on any day**, and more than 25 a day on only 5 days.
    - They came mostly during the first 4 and last 10 days of the run.
    - **Most entered between 18:00 and 03:00, peaking 21:00–midnight.**
    - The total was about 428–438 fish. Spawning was observed in water **4–12 in (10–30 cm) deep** in flooded grass.
  - **Return to the lake:**
    - **35 days** passed between the first spent pike coming down and the last leaving. Most went back in the first 12 days, and stragglers stayed until 30 May.
    - "adult pike do not return to the lake immediately after spawning."
  - **Young:**
    - **7,239 young pike** from 108 females and 247 males returned to the lake, drifting "head or tail first or sideways" just under the surface, more on bright days.
    - More than 6,000 were back by 1 June, at under 2.5 in (6 cm), after growing about 2 mm/day.
    - The ditches **dried on 23 July**. The largest young pike left by then was 9 in (23 cm).
  - **Predators and losses:**
    - About 10 adults were unaccounted for, perhaps "eaten by other pike, birds, turtles".
    - **Minnows and young yellow perch ran up the ditch "at about the time the pike eggs are hatching"**; why was not determined.
    - Many pike "perish when left stranded by declining water levels and drying marshes."
- **Pike in a small river (Yampa River, CO)** [agency report; snippet]:
  - Most stayed within the river mile where they were tagged, with movements mostly under ½ mile, sometimes from one pool through a riffle to another.
  - **Activity peaked after dusk, late evening to midnight.** One fish moved about 0.2 mile upstream by 22:45 and was back at its capture spot by 11:00 the next day.
  - [Colorado CWCB document](https://dnrweblink.state.co.us/CWCB/0/doc/98734/Page40.aspx)
- **Small home ranges in a lowland river (UK)** [snippet]: seasonal home ranges were **0.02–0.95 ha in winter and 0.02–0.59 ha in spring**. Annual excursion distance rose with winter and spring home-range size. — [Bournemouth record](https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/journal-article/9581)
- **River Ourthe, Belgium** (6 radio-tagged pike, 30-km reach) [snippet]: upstream spawning runs began **8 February–30 March at about 6.7–8.7 °C**, over **0.75–15.7 km**. Fish stayed **5–25 days** at the spawning sites, then went back downstream. — [ORBi](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/6137)
- **Spawning-site fidelity** [snippet]: of 1,900 pike marked at two spawning sites on Kabetogama Lake, MN, 23% were recaptured on spawning grounds 1–3 times over the next 4 years (Miller et al. 2001). — [afs.confex 2012 abstract](https://afs.confex.com/afs/2012/webprogram/Paper10558.html)
- **Piscivores rarely follow prey into streams** (a shallow Danish lake and its three streams; PIT telemetry, October–June) [abstract]:
  - About 40% of roach and 55% of white bream moved into the streams in winter, against **fewer than 2% of pike and perch**.
  - Cyprinid migration ran from late October to May, in bursts in December, January and April.
  - Skov et al. 2008, *Ecol. Freshw. Fish* 17:406 — [DTU Orbit](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/inter-and-size-specific-patterns-of-fish-seasonal-migration-betwe/)

### Inferences
- **Model chain pickerel in the brook as summer and early-autumn visitors and short-term residents, not breeders.**
  - Their brook habitat is the pool and run margins with wood, undercut banks or plants: low velocity and cover.
  - They should avoid riffles, as all *Esox* avoid flow.
  - Residence should be days to weeks for most, with a larger-bodied mobile minority making longer excursions between the lakes. That matches the angler's "passing through and hanging out" and the George Lake summer excursions.
- **Spring use of the brook by pickerel is possible but would be brief.**
  - It is plausible only if ice-out high water floods vegetated brook margins at 6–11 °C.
  - The template is the Houghton Lake ditch: nights, 10–30 cm water, days to weeks, young drifting back to the lake.
  - Hot Brook's cobble riffles and short run make true flooded-grass spawning habitat unlikely unless its banks flood.
- **Pickerel are more nocturnal and crepuscular in streams than bass.** Use the pike surrogate here: activity peaking after dusk, with dawn and dusk feeding per the agencies.
- **Pickerel should not winter in the brook.** The surrogate rule is that winter piscivores stay in the lake (under 2% migrate). The existing note has pickerel active and shallow in the lake under ice.

### Gaps
- **No telemetry, mark–recapture or density data for chain pickerel in any stream or lake outlet was found.**
- No residence time in a connecting channel is known.
- No Maine stream records were found.
- The George Lake study's summer-excursion details (how many fish, where, when) are unpublished in what was found.
- **No evidence was found of chain pickerel spawning runs into inlet streams.** The spawning evidence is all from flooded lake margins and marshes.

## 3. Predators at stream mouths, outlet pools, confluences and fishways

### Takeaway
Wherever migrating or drifting prey are funnelled, piscivores gather and feed hard:
- **Lake outlets:** more than 90% of bull trout at the Chilko Lake outlet held smolts. The first river segment below the lake is the highest-risk reach.
- **Fishway entrances:** these held the most large predators, which stayed for up to 90 days.
- **Deep pools:** predation on fry was higher in pools deeper than 2.5 m than in riffles.
- **Impoundments in Maine:** chain pickerel and smallmouth bass were the **only** predators of tethered smolts, with 23% predated within 1 hour. Risk tripled from 5 to 15 °C and was above average within 40 m of shore.

Prey defend themselves by:
- moving in **brief, flow-triggered pulses**: more than 80% of 320,000 juvenile alewives left a Connecticut lake in 3 pulses of 1–2 days;
- **swamping** predators: smolt survival improved at high smolt density;
- **travelling at night**.

No warmwater northeastern study measured bass or pickerel density, or their consumption, at a lake-outlet pool against the open lake.

### Cited findings

**Lake outlets** (surrogate: char and sockeye, BC)
- **Chilko Lake outlet** [abstract]: 22 bull trout were sampled "at the edge of Chilko Lake and the Chilko River" during the smolt run.
  - **20 of 22 (> 90%) had food,** and virtually all identifiable prey were outmigrating sockeye smolts.
  - **Stomach contents were 0–12.6% of body mass.**
  - The authors call the feeding "nearly exclusive and intense", consistent with high disappearance of smolts leaving the lake.
  - Furey, Hinch, Lotto & Beauchamp 2015, *J. Fish Biol.* — [USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/extensive-feeding-sockeye-salmon-oncorhynchus-nerka-smolts-bull-trout-salvelinus)
- **The same system** [thesis abstract; snippet]:
  - Acoustic telemetry identified **the first river segment below the lake as "a high-risk landscape associated with poor survival"**.
  - Smolts passing at **higher densities of other smolts survived better** (predator swamping).
  - Bull trout were most active at night and stayed close to a counting fence that funnels smolts into high densities.
  - Smolts migrated at night, presumably to reduce risk.
  - [Furey PhD thesis, UBC](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/24/items/1.0307167)
- **Babine Lake** (artificial streams; rainbow trout eating sockeye fry) [thesis abstract; snippet]:
  - Fry mortality was **inversely related to water velocity and turbidity**.
  - Fry with prior predator experience escaped better.
  - Predators also "modify and enhance downstream movement" of fry (Delaney 1979).
  - [Ginetz thesis, UBC](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/831/items/1.0101880); [Delaney 1979, UBC](https://open.library.ubc.ca/collections/831/items/1.0094749)

**Pools as fry-predation hotspots** (Cedar River, WA; surrogate) [snippet]
- Torrent sculpin predation on sockeye fry was "substantially higher in **large, deep pools (> 2.5 m)** than in either riffles or slow-water secondary habitats" in February–March.
- Sculpin preyed best in darkness, and city lighting increased predation in the field.
- [Tabor & Peters 2016, *Northwestern Naturalist* 97(3)](https://bioone.org/journals/Northwestern-Naturalist/volume-97/issue-3/NWN15-09.1/Diet-of-the-Torrent-Sculpin-Cottus-rhotheus-in-the-Cedar/10.1898/NWN15-09.1.full)

**Fishways and dam tailraces**
- **Lajeado Dam fish ladder, Brazil** [snippet of abstract]:
  - **The ladder entrance (downstream) held the most large predators** and was the only region with relevant non-fish vertebrate predators.
  - Tagged predators were detected **predating along the ladder for up to 90 days**.
  - The authors attribute this to crowding of prey in a restricted channel plus injuries.
  - Agostinho et al. 2012, *Neotrop. Ichthyol.* — [SciELO](https://scielo.br/j/ni/a/FZrX3DpHSRyXnyZ4WNLjbwR/?lang=en)
- **John Day Reservoir, Columbia River** (April–August 1983–86) [snippet of abstracts]:
  - Averaged over sizes and months, **smallmouth bass total daily ration was 28.7 mg/g** (all prey), about twice that of pikeminnow (14.1), walleye (14.2) and channel catfish (12.6).
  - Juvenile salmonids were only **4%** of smallmouth diet by weight, against 67% for pikeminnow.
  - Pikeminnow caused 78% of salmonid losses, walleye 13% and smallmouth 9%.
  - Vigg et al. 1991; Poe et al. 1991; Rieman et al. 1991, *TAFS* 120 — [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/rates-consumption-juvenile-salmonids-and-alternative-pray-fish-northern-squawfish); [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/estimated-loss-juvenile-salmonids-predation-northern-squawfish-walleyes-and-smallmouth)

**Maine: chain pickerel and smallmouth bass as ambushers of migrants**
- **Weldon Dam reservoir, Penobscot River, spring 2022** (390 tethered Atlantic salmon smolts) [abstract]:
  - Smolts "were exclusively predated by" **chain pickerel (n = 43) and smallmouth bass (n = 42)**.
  - **23% (95% CI 15–29%) were predated within a 1-h deployment.**
  - Predation probability rose **from 10% to 33% as water warmed from 5 to 15 °C.**
  - **Smolts within 40 m of shore had above-average risk.**
  - Mensinger, Casey, Mortelliti & Zydlewski 2025, *CJFAS* — [USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/smallmouth-bass-micropterus-dolomieu-and-chain-pickerel-esox-niger-identified-atlantic)
  - A related UMaine thesis [snippet] attributes **71% of freshwater tethered-smolt predation to smallmouth and 27% to chain pickerel**. — [UMaine ETD 4046](https://digitalcommons.library.umaine.edu/etd/4046) (mapping of this figure to this record not verified)
- **Older Maine context** [snippet]: "dead water areas and pools in lower rivers" were where chain pickerel and largemouth bass could ambush migrating Atlantic salmon juveniles or smolts. — [KRIS Sheepscot hypothesis page](http://www.krisweb.com/krissheepscot/krisdb/html/krisweb/analysis/hypoth_6_exotics_sheep.htm)

**Juvenile alewives leaving lakes** (an emigration through outlets well documented in New England)
- **Bride Lake, CT, 2006** [abstract]:
  - About **320,000 juveniles emigrated from mid-June to mid-August**, and **more than 80% left in three pulses lasting 1–2 days each**.
  - The pulses coincided with **rain, brief drops in temperature and brief rises in discharge**.
  - Migration was **around dawn early in the season, then at night**.
  - Migrants were 1–12 mm longer and in better condition than non-migrants.
  - Gahagan, Schultz & Gherard 2010 — [UConn record](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/eeb_articles/23)
- **Maine DMR** [agency; snippet]:
  - Juvenile alewives feed brook trout, salmon, perch, **pickerel and bass**.
  - **American eels "gorge on juvenile alewife leaving lakes in the fall."**
  - Seaward migration runs from mid-July to early December.
  - [Maine DMR alewife](https://www.maine.gov/dmr/fisheries/sea-run-fisheries/sea-run-species/alewife)
- **Two Cape Cod ponds (Santuit, Coonamessett), 1994** [snippet]:
  - Herring were the most important fish prey by number. They were eaten mainly by **largemouth bass under 300 mm, after mid-August**.
  - These diets were sampled in the lake, not at the outlet.
  - [UMass ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/entities/publication/4d3e28c4-39f4-46db-8efe-6192fc40f7f0) (mapping of this summary to this record not verified)
- **Penobscot** [snippet]: juvenile herring were **19% (SE ± 6%) of smallmouth stomach mass in the freshwater tidal reach but rarely seen upstream**. — [USGS: river herring and smallmouth diet](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70206132) (mapping not verified)

**Prey escaping lake predators into streams** (surrogate, Scandinavia)
- More than 2,000 roach were tagged over 4 years. **The risk of being eaten (by cormorants) rose with time spent in the lake in winter,** and migrating into streams reduced it. [snippet] — Skov et al. 2013, *Biol. Lett.* ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3639769))
- Pike and perch rarely followed (§2).
- Resident roach had fuller stomachs than migrants, so migrating cost them food. [snippet] — Chapman et al. 2013 ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0061223.html))

### Inferences
- **The angler's observation is the classic outlet funnel.** Big bass in the 1.1-m pool at Upper's outlet, "waiting for schools of small minnows", matches three documented patterns:
  1. piscivores concentrate where prey are funnelled from a lake into a stream (Chilko);
  2. the deepest pool is the hotspot (Cedar River, pools deeper than 2.5 m; bass in pools deeper than 50 cm, Power 1987);
  3. shoreline proximity raises risk (Maine, within 40 m).
- **Pool attractiveness should rise with prey flux,** not be a fixed property. Fish passage through the brook (young fallfish, shiners, dace, young-of-year schools) should come in **pulses** tied to rain and discharge, temperature drops, dawn and night (Bride Lake). Predators that learn the pool pays should gather there in pulses, as bull trout tracked smolt density.
- **Per-capita risk should fall with school size** (swamping, Furey) and **with flow and turbidity** (Ginetz). Shallow riffle crossings and night crossings should be safer from visual bass, but not from pike-like dusk hunters or eels.
- **Feeding rate at the funnel:**
  - A bull trout at the outlet carried up to 12.6% of its body mass in prey.
  - A smallmouth's average daily ration is about 2.9% of body mass per day (28.7 mg/g/d).
  - So one outlet "binge" can equal about 4 days of normal ration (my cross-species arithmetic).
  - In the sim, an outlet bass could fill its gut in one prey pulse, then sit and digest. That matches anglers seeing fish "waiting".
- **Temperature gates the hazard.** Maine pickerel and bass predation tripled from 5 to 15 °C. Outlet ambush should be negligible in early spring and late autumn and strongest in summer.

### Gaps
- **No northeastern warmwater study measured bass or pickerel numbers, residence or consumption at a lake-outlet pool or stream mouth against the lake.** The "how much higher than the lake" ratio is unknown. All aggregation evidence is from salmonid outlets, fishways, dams or reservoirs.
- **No data on minnow or young-fallfish passage rates through a short connecting brook** (schools per day, school size, season) were found.
- The Columbia tailrace-versus-reservoir consumption contrast exists in the Northern Pikeminnow Management Program reports but was not extracted.

## 4. How water level and flow (including dry years) change predator use of such streams

### Takeaway
- **Flow sets connectivity, refuge and prey supply.**
  - **High water** (ice-out, storms) carries prey pulses through outlets and floods margins that pike-like fish spawn in. It cues smallmouth autumn migrations and displaces stream fry.
  - **Low water** isolates pools. Bass then move away from drying reaches: 10% moved more than 1 km, and movement fell from 270 m in May to 54 m in July. Bass crowd into remnant pools, and some die there or are taken.
- **Prey emigration from a lake stops when the outlet dries,** so juvenile alewives are trapped in their natal pond. In a dry summer the outlet-pool ambush therefore loses its prey flux exactly when predators are most crowded.

### Cited findings
- **Illinois Bayou, AR** (60 radio-tagged adult smallmouth; May–October 2006; seasonally discontinuous flow) [full text] — [Hafs 2007](https://www.bemidjistate.edu/directory/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2023/02/Hafs-A.W.-2007.-Smallmouth-bass-survival-movement-and-habitat-use-in-response-to-seasonally-discontinuous-surface-flow.-Ma.pdf)
  - **Habitat loss:** riffles and runs dried "completely", leaving disconnected remnant pools. **Wetted area fell more than 55%** in some sections.
  - **Movement:**
    - Mean net movement was 458, 212 and 429 m in the three forks.
    - **6 of 58 fish (10%) moved a net distance of more than 1 km**, and 44 of 58 moved less than 500 m in total.
    - Average daily cumulative movement was 2.9–7.4 m.
    - Movement was larger and more variable where more wetted area was lost, and fish moved away from drying reaches.
  - **Survival (May–September):**
    - 67.1% in the most-dried, most-fished fork, against 78.9–84.2% in the others.
    - That gives an estimated annual survival of 56% and 66–71%.
  - **Deaths:**
    - Two fish were trapped when their pools isolated in late June; their transmitters were recovered on the dry pool beds on 9 August.
    - Three more vanished from isolated pools, probably taken by predators or anglers.
    - "Fish congregating in shrinking remnant pools are more susceptible to predators."
  - **Sub-adults:** the author hypothesises that sub-adult mortality is higher from being trapped before home pools are established, and from cannibalism by larger fish in home pools.
- **Published version** [snippet]: mean movement **fell from 270 m in May to 54 m by July**, with fish moving farther in streams losing more wetted area. — [Hafs et al. 2010, *NAJFM* 30:604](https://academic.oup.com/najfm/article/30/2/604/7846726)
- **Smallmouth migrations triggered by rain and rising flow** in autumn, below 15.6 °C (Lyons & Kanehl via forum, §1). Stream nests are "threatened by disruptive flooding" (Forbes 1981, §1).
- **Pike spawning ditch drying, Houghton Lake** [full text]:
  - Spring high water flooded the ditch and marsh. "During July … the water level falls … and the ditches dry up"; in 1939 that happened on 23 July.
  - Many pike "perish when left stranded by declining water levels and drying marshes".
  - In high water, a wave-built barrier at the ditch mouth diverted outflow (and young pike) to the river 6 miles below the lake.
  - [Carbine 1940](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR0501-RR0600/RR0583.pdf)
- **Juvenile alewives trapped by low flow** [news, quoting the UConn PI]:
  - Juveniles "are having difficulty leaving the pond they were born in during some periods of the summer because the stream dries up". Low flows "appear to have one of the largest potential impacts" on out-migration (2021–22 study).
  - At Bride Lake, the emigration pulses followed rain and discharge rises (§3).
  - [Long Island Sound Study](https://lispartnership.org/?p=30640)
- **Chain pickerel leave stream channels during floods** [snippet] — [Fishes of Texas](https://txstate.fishesoftexas.org/esox%20niger.htm)
- **Escapement from reservoirs** (review of 57 papers) [snippet]:
  - Annual escapement ranged **0–100%**.
  - It was higher from spillways and surface-release outlets than from other outlet types.
  - **Smaller-bodied fish escaped at higher rates.**
  - [Reservoir Fish Escapement in North America review](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/reservoir-fish-escapement-in-north-america-a-historical-review-an/)
- **Lake Mattamuskeet, NC** [snippet]: telemetry tested whether largemouth bass use of canals connected to the lake is triggered by fluctuating lake levels. No result was found. — [NCWRC report](https://ncwildlife.gov/media/3109/download)
- **Invertebrates under low flow:** riffle invertebrates and the refuge for small fish collapse first (Walters & Post 2011 in [brook_food_web.md §5](../Whole%20lake%203D%20fish%20simulation/brook_food_web.md)).

### Inferences
- **Wet spring or storm (high flow):**
  - Prey pulses pass through the brook, at dawn and night and on rain, and outlet-pool ambushers gather.
  - Margins may flood, opening brief pike- or pickerel-style spawning.
  - Bass nests in the run risk washout.
  - Per-fish predation risk falls with turbidity and velocity (Ginetz).
- **Normal summer:**
  - Resident bass hold the pool. Juveniles hold the run, and age-0 and dace the riffles.
  - Pickerel visit, and prey passage is moderate.
- **Dry summer, as Upper falls toward its sill:**
  - Discharge goes to zero and the riffles dry first. The brook becomes the outlet pool plus perhaps run puddles.
  - **Prey stop passing,** because emigration needs flow (Bride Lake), so the outlet ambush stops paying.
  - Bass in the brook should either retreat to a lake through the last wet connection (the "move away from drying" rule, with roughly 10% making long moves) or be **trapped in the pool**.
  - Trapped fish face crowding with prey and with each other, warm lake-surface water, night-time O₂ sag, cannibalism of sub-adults, and terrestrial and avian predators (heron, otter, mink, eagle). The Hafs deaths and disappearances are the template.
  - Survival over the summer should drop by about 10–15 points against a flowing year (67% against 79–84% in Hafs).
- **Recovery:** autumn rain reconnects the brook, and the cue (rain plus water below 15.6 °C) sends bass back to the lakes for winter.

### Gaps
- **No study of predator use of a lake-connecting brook as lake level changes was found,** in Maine or anywhere else.
- **Hot Brook's own stage, sill elevation and dry-year discharge are unknown.** The brook_food_web.md gap stands.
- **No temperature or O₂ thresholds were extracted for bass trapped in remnant pools.** The Hafs thesis has temperature loggers, but its pool-specific DO and temperature extremes were not pulled.
