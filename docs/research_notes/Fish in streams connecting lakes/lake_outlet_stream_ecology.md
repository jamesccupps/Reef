# Ecology of short lake-outlet streams that connect two lakes (the setting for Hot Brook's fish)

Scope: what a short (~150–200 m × ~8 m) brook between two shallow, unstratified, tea-coloured warmwater lakes gets from the upstream lake (food, heat, flow), how its habitat sets fish numbers, and what happens when a dry year nearly stops it. This **extends** `docs/research_notes/Whole lake 3D fish simulation/brook_food_web.md`, which already holds the outlet filter-feeder gradients (Valett & Stanford, Robinson & Minshall, Eriksson, Demi et al. in Maine), the per-100-m zooplankton and phytoplankton stripping rates, drift numbers, dace energetics, the Power/Schlosser depth-refuge rules and the Walters & Post low-flow invertebrate results. Those are cited here only by reference ("BFW §n"). Fish movement through the brook (transit times, pickerel/bass at the mouths, passage depths, wintering cyprinids) is in `movement_between_lakes.md` ("MBL Q2") and is not repeated.

Region tags: **[NE]** northeastern North America; **[ME]** Maine; **[N-temp]** other north-temperate North America; **[EU]** Europe; **[S]** southern/southeastern US; **[W]** western North America; **[subarctic]**. "[snip]" = number taken from a search-result summary, page not opened; "[abs]" = abstract only; "[ft]" = full text read.

## 1. What the upstream lake exports, who catches it in the outlet, and how much reaches fish

### Takeaway
A lake outlet is a conveyor of lake food, and the catch is front-loaded: lake crustacean zooplankton and large particles are stripped within the first 10–200 m (95% of exported zooplankton biomass gone within 500 m in alpine outlets; 47–75% of cladocerans and copepods within 200 m in Polish outlets of Hot Brook's size), mostly **by fish by day**. Attached filter feeders sit just below the outlet, and native unionid mussels can form outlet **hotspots of up to 290 ind/m² (14.7 kg/m²)** that peak just below the lake and decline downstream as a power function. Outlet richness comes from food quality plus hydraulic and thermal stability, not simply from more seston. A 150–200-m brook is therefore an outlet reach along its whole length, and fish in it should eat lake zooplankton by day, outlet invertebrates (caddis, blackflies, drift) at dusk and night, and should share a bed with dense mussels.

### Cited Findings
**The review hypotheses (Richardson & Mackay 1991)**
- The review itself (Oikos 62:370–380, "Lake outlets and the distribution of filter feeders: an assessment of hypotheses") again could not be retrieved; only its bibliographic listing was found. — [Springer bibliography listing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00016829) [snip]
- Citing papers use it for the generalisation that "dense assemblages of filter-feeding invertebrates are often found at lake outlets". The hypotheses discussed around it are (a) seston quantity falling with distance (the Sheldon & Oswood 1977 log-linear model), (b) seston *quality* falling with distance, (c) current velocity and substrate, and (d) lake buffering of discharge and temperature (stable substrate). — [Eriksson 2001, Ann. Limnol. 37:199](https://www.limnology-journal.org/articles/limn/abs/2001/03/limno20013p199/limno20013p199.html) [abs]; [Valett & Stanford 1987](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/food-quality-and-hydropsychid-caddisfly-density-in-a-lake-outlet-/) [abs]
- **Food gradient test [EU, Austria]:** below Lunzer See (Unterer Lunzer Seebach), seston C/N ratios and plankton densities "did not change significantly over the sampled stretch" and failed to explain the observed filter-feeder gradient, i.e. hydraulics, not food decline, set it there. — [Univ. Vienna thesis](https://utheses-gateway.univie.ac.at/api/document/get/file/64080) [snip; PDF returned 401]
- This agrees with the Maine result that filter-feeder biomass did not track seston quantity in six lake–outlet pairs (BFW §1, Demi et al. 2012).

**Unionid mussels: outlets as hotspots (new to this project)**
- **[EU, Poland/Germany] A 35-year study (1984 and 2019) of an undisturbed stream flowing through five consecutive lakes:**
  - unionid densities were highest just downstream of the lakes, reaching **290 ind/m² (14.7 kg/m²)** in 2019, and declined with distance downstream following a **negative power function**; the pattern was spatially consistent and persisted over 35 years;
  - spent-shell aggregations of up to **17 kg/m²** built shell and mussel beds below the lakes;
  - an estimated **1.9 × 10⁶ mussels in 3.2 km** of stream **filtered a volume equal to the whole stream discharge ~2.5 times a day**;
  - the native community resisted invasive *Dreissena* for decades; the authors recommend prioritising lake-outlet streams for conservation.
  
  — ["Lake-stream transition zones support hotspots of freshwater ecosystem services: evidence from a 35-year study on unionid mussels" (2021), INIS record](https://inis.iaea.org/records/8bv40-we758) [snip; page returned 402 on fetch]
- **Same shape for an invasive filterer [N-temp, Michigan/Indiana]:** zebra mussel densities declined exponentially from **>1,000/m² at a lake outlet to ~10/m² within 1 km** below the colonised lake, with isolated mussels up to 12 km downstream (lake-stream source–sink dynamics). — [Horvath et al. 1996, JNABS, UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/zebra-mussel-dispersal-in-lake-stream-systems-source-sink-dynamic/) [snip]
- **[S. America, flagged] Outlets hold a distinct fauna:** in an Andean lake-district study, 7% of macroinvertebrate taxa occurred only in outlets (5% only in inlets, 22% only in tributaries). — [ANID record](https://investigadores.anid.cl/es/public_search/work?id=673039) [snip; attribution of the numbers to this record not verified]

**Zooplankton export and fish as the main sink**
- **[W, alpine Colorado] 12 lakes:** 95% of exported zooplankton biomass lost within 500 m, highest loss rate in the first 10 m; large taxa lost fastest; export much higher at night (diel vertical migration); **fishless lakes exported 5× more zooplankton biomass** because lake fish suppress large zooplankton; and **fish in outlet streams directly consumed macroinvertebrates, lowering macroinvertebrate biomass** in streams with fish. — [Detmer, McCutchan & Lewis 2017, Inland Waters 7:440](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/68860) [abs]
- **[EU, Poland] Outlets of Hot Brook's size (4–6 m wide, 0.2–0.4 m deep):** outlet sections carry dense zooplankton washed from the lake's upper layers, which "feed young planktivorous fish, mainly cyprinids"; the largest reductions occur in the first **0.2 km**; light conditions control the reduction (greatest by day). — [PLOS ONE 2016, DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/6d97345e65df4690807c46c80fe60a7a) [snip]; the per-taxon 200-m reductions (60.6–74.7% large cladocerans, 62.2–68.2% copepods; 15–31% at night) are in BFW §1. On this pass the PMC full text served a browser-verification page and was not re-read.

**Why outlet reaches are productive (mechanisms with numbers)**
- Hydropsychid density at a Montana lake outlet was **6,500/m²** (1,427/m² at 200 m; 209/m² at 3.4 km), attributed to "stable substrate, discharge and temperature regimes" plus high-quality food (BFW §1).
- Newfoundland outlets were **warmer and richer in nutrients** than other stream sites, and hydropsychid distribution was "strongly influenced by lake outlets" (BFW §1, Fietsch 2006).
- Outlet hydrographs are damped and late [ME]: Clayton Stream at the Clayton Lake outlet still ran **2.4× its mean flow in May and 1.05× in June**, against 0.6–0.75× in June at unregulated streams. — [USGS NWIS monthly statistics, via `Maine lake shores and catchment/weather_and_catchment_hydrology.md`](https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/stat/?format=rdb&sites=01012515,01035500&statReportType=monthly&statTypeCd=mean&parameterCd=00060)

### Inferences
- **Fish are the dominant zooplankton sink in Hot Brook, by day.** Both lake-outlet studies that separate the sinks (Polish outlets, Detmer) point to fish, not to filtering insects. The brook's lakes hold fish, so (per Detmer) the export is smaller and smaller-bodied than from a fishless lake. In the sim, the day-time catch should go to visual planktivores holding in the mouth pool and run (young sunfish, young bass, perch, fallfish and dace young, golden shiner-type schools passing through), with the per-100-m stripping rates of BFW §1 as the upper bound on what they can take.
- **Night is for insects.** Lake zooplankton export peaks at night but fish catch little of it then; invertebrate drift peaks after dusk (BFW §3). A brook fish's diet should switch from zooplankton (day) to drift and benthos (dusk/night).
- **Mussels are a first-class outlet consumer.** The user reports "lots of mussels" in the brook. The 2021 outlet-hotspot result gives a mechanism and a magnitude: if Hot Brook's 1,200–1,600 m² of bed held even 10–50 *Elliptio*/m² (my assumption, far below the 290/m² hotspot), that is ~12,000–80,000 mussels. At a clearance of order 1 L/h per mussel (assumed, not sourced here), they would clear ~0.3–2 million L/day, i.e. ~1–8% of a 0.3 m³/s summer discharge per pass. So mussels would trim the phytoplankton export modestly at normal flow but **dominate seston removal at drought flows**, when discharge falls 10–50-fold and residence time rises.
- **Where in the brook:** filterers just below the lip where current reaches ~0.3 m/s (BFW §1); mussels from the mouth pool tail downstream through the run, densest nearest Upper; zooplankton-feeding fish in the mouth pool and at the head of the first riffle.

### Gaps
- Richardson & Mackay 1991 full text, Wotton's outlet blackfly numbers and Morin's seston budgets remain unretrieved (also BFW gaps).
- No Maine or New England measurement of *Elliptio* density, size structure or clearance at a lake outlet was found; the 290/m² hotspot is European (likely *Unio*/*Anodonta*, species not confirmed from the snippet).
- No study measured how much of the outlet zooplankton export is eaten specifically by sunfish, young bass or chain pickerel; the fish identified as the sink were cyprinid fry (Poland) and trout (Colorado).
- No diet study of fish in a lake-connecting brook versus the same species in the adjacent lake was found.

## 2. Thermal regime of a short outlet brook below warm shallow lakes

### Takeaway
A short outlet stream runs at the lake's surface temperature: lakes warm their outlet streams by **~3.4 °C on average in August** relative to their inflows (southern Quebec), and the warm lake signal fades only slowly downstream over kilometres, through shade and diffuse groundwater, not abruptly. Over ~200 m with a transit time of minutes, Hot Brook is effectively Upper Hot Brook Lake's surface water: ~24 °C at the summer peak, with the lake's own diel swing. Lakes of Upper's and Lower's size (~3–4 km²) sit at the 3.2-km² change point between the ~1 °C daily swing of large lakes and the 4–7 °C of small ones; my estimate for the brook is ~1.5–3 °C a day. It is warmwater habitat, not coolwater: a Maine fisheries survey describes a warm pond outlet as offering "little brook trout habitat".

### Cited Findings
- **[NE, southern Quebec] Lakes warm their outlets:** in August 2020, reservoirs behind small surface-release dams warmed the stream below them by **3.7 °C** on average relative to upstream; **natural lakes of similar size warmed outlet streams by 3.4 °C** (difference not significant). In August, the main determinants of stream temperature were drainage area, **distance to an upstream water body**, and the proportion of the watershed in water bodies. — [Auffray, Senécal, Turgeon, St-Hilaire & Maheu 2023, Sci. Total Environ. 869:161445](https://espace.inrs.ca/id/eprint/15420/) [abs]
- **[W, British Columbia] Lake-headed vs headwater streams:** in 20 streams, downstream *cooling* was common below lakes (warm outlet water losing heat and gaining cold groundwater), while downstream *warming* was common in headwater streams; temperature increases after streamside logging were smaller in lake-headed streams. — [Mellina et al. 2006 follow-up of Mellina et al. 2002, BC Water Tool record](https://www.bcwatertool.ca/info-sources/omineca/result.php?q=MjM1NA==) [snip; record returned 404 on fetch]
- **[EU, northern Sweden] Virtual experiment:** summer stream-temperature response to harvesting was ~0.5–1.5 °C higher in a stream *without* a headwater lake than in one with a lake (the lake buffers), though the lake stream responded more strongly during rain events. — [Hydrological Processes 2022, openpolar record](https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.1002%2Fhyp.14593) [snip]
- **[W, Washington] Fibre-optic profile below Lake Isabella (Mill Creek):** daily maximum temperature and daily temperature variability "progressively decreased downstream" over ~2.2 km of cable (1-m resolution, Aug–Sep 2020); no abrupt cold inputs >2 °C; some **deep pools were cooler** than other locations; the warm lake discharge was buffered by riparian shading and diffuse groundwater. — [Gendaszek et al. 2022, USGS SIR 2022-5063](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20225063) [abs]
- **Lake diel range sets the outlet's diel range:** across 100 lakes, area alone explained 83% of mean summer diel surface-temperature range (DTR); below a change point of **3.2 km²**, DTR "increases sharply and predictably with decreasing lake area"; most small lakes have summer DTRs of **4–7 °C**, large lakes ~**1 °C**. Light attenuation mattered only in the least transparent waters (Iz < 30%); mixed-layer volume predicted DTR better than lake volume. — [Woolway et al. 2016, PLOS ONE (PMC4811584)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4811584/) [ft]
- **[ME] Warm outlets and coldwater fish:** an MDIFW survey sheet describes a pond outlet as "warm and marshy, providing little brook trout habitat", although its mouth held some spawning area. — [MDIFW Mountain Pond (T10 R10) survey](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/piscataquis/mountain_pond_t10r10.pdf) [snip]
- **[ME] The Hot Brook lakes:** MDIFW survey sheets (surveyed August 1949, revised 1965 and 2004) list Upper Hot Brook Lake at 912 acres, max depth 18 ft, and Lower at 713 acres, max depth 13 ft; principal fisheries smallmouth bass, white perch and chain pickerel; Upper's outlet flows into Lower, and the "only access" to Lower is "through the inlet stream from the upper lake". — [MDIFW Upper Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_upper.pdf); [MDIFW Lower Hot Brook Lake](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/washington/hot_brook_lake_lower.pdf) [snip; both PDFs returned 403 on fetch]
- **[S, Arkansas, for drought pools] How hot shallow stream pools get:** in summer drying, stream temperatures were Haw Creek mean 22.1 °C (range **13.8–31.0 °C**), Hurricane Creek 20.5 °C (14.6–28.3), Indian Creek 22.5 °C (**10.6–32.0**). — [Dekar & Magoulick 2007, Ecol. Freshw. Fish](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf) [ft]

### Inferences
- **The brook is lake water.** At ~0.2–0.3 m/s, water crosses 200 m in ~11–17 minutes. Even a strong midday net heat gain (assume ~500 W/m²) warms a 0.3-m water column by only ~1.4 °C per hour (500 / (1000 kg/m³ × 4186 J/kg/K × 0.3 m)), so ~0.3 °C at most over the transit. Set brook temperature = Upper's surface mixed-layer temperature, with no downstream gradient at normal flow.
- **Diel swing.** Upper (3.7 km²) sits just above Woolway's 3.2-km² change point and Lower (2.8 km²) just below it. A summer DTR of ~1.5–3 °C for the brook (between the 1 °C of large lakes and the 4–7 °C of small ones) is my estimate; the tea colour raises it only if the water is dark enough that <30% of light reaches the reference depth.
- **At drought flow the brook detaches from the lake thermally.** At ~0.02 m/s (drought discharge ~0.02× mean, per `weather_and_catchment_hydrology.md`), transit takes ~3 hours and shallow riffle water can gain ~2–4 °C by mid-afternoon and lose it at night; isolated pools behave like small ponds with 4–7+ °C daily swings, and Arkansas pools reached 31–32 °C. Expect brook afternoons of ~27–29 °C in a hot dry August, against 24 °C in the lake (estimate).
- **Fish implications.** At 24 °C the brook is near the preferred temperatures of the warmwater/coolwater fish already in the lakes (sunfish, smallmouth, pickerel, perch, fallfish) and unsuitable for brook trout. Deep pools (the 1.1-m mouth pool) are slightly cooler (Mill Creek) and become the afternoon thermal refuge in a drought.
- **Winter.** The outlet carries 0–4 °C lake surface water from under the ice. Flowing water thins ice (Rice Lake inlet, MBL Q2), so the brook's riffles may stay open longer than the lakes.

### Gaps
- No temperature record for Hot Brook or any small Maine lake-to-lake connector was found. Auffray et al.'s distance-decay of lake warming was not extracted (abstract only).
- No study measured the diel temperature range of a short outlet versus its lake directly; the inheritance rule above is inferred.
- Mellina et al. 2002/2006 downstream cooling rates (°C per 100 m) were not retrieved (record 404).

## 3. Habitat structure and fish carrying capacity: pools vs riffles, and the brook vs the lakes

### Takeaway
In small streams, fish numbers are set by pool depth and area, by cover, and by predator-mediated habitat sorting: richness rises with pool area and depth, small fish thin out in deeper pools (where piscivores sit), and large fish need pools ≥0.5 m (BFW §4). Warmwater pool-riffle streams carry **~100–840 kg/ha of fish**, with centrarchids (smallmouth, sunfish, rock bass) typically **5–28%** of the weight. Streams next to a lake hold extra species as **young of lake species** dispersing from the lake, so the brook's small bass and sunfish are best modelled as lake-born juveniles plus summer sojourners. No study comparing fish density in a connecting stream with its lakes was found.

### Cited Findings
**Pool size, depth and fish [S, Arkansas; flagged]**
- In 3 Ozark creeks (mean widths **5.4–8.2 m**, like Hot Brook), 17 pools (2002) and more (2003) during summer drying had volumes **9.2–226.3 m³** and maximum depths **0.32–1.75 m**. Total fish density and creek chub and stoneroller density were **negatively related to maximum pool depth** in both years; in the drier year, species richness was **positively related to pool area**. The authors interpret the depth effect as small fish avoiding larger predatory fish in deep pools (Power 1987; brassy minnows displaced from pools into riffles by smallmouth bass, Schlosser 1988), or as fish being concentrated as pools shrink. — [Dekar & Magoulick 2007, Ecol. Freshw. Fish](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf) [ft]
- Same paper: "Larger pools acted as refugia for more species compared with smaller pools that were less likely to persist", supporting Schlosser's (1987) hypothesis that richness increases with depth, habitat heterogeneity and temporal stability. — same source [ft]
- Power 1987 (bass in pools >50 cm on 72/75 pool-dates; fish >3–4 cm avoid <20 cm water) and Schlosser 1987 (with bass, juveniles restricted to riffles and raceways) are in BFW §4.

**Lake proximity adds lake species' young [N-temp, Minnesota]**
- On the Kabetogama Peninsula (Voyageurs NP), streams and collapsed beaver ponds had the highest species richness; the extra, less abundant species were mostly **small size classes of large-lake species (black crappie, smallmouth bass, yellow perch, burbot)**. Sites closer to Kabetogama Lake had higher richness, which the authors attribute to dispersal of early life stages from the lake. Four small species (northern redbelly dace, brook stickleback, finescale dace, fathead minnow) were common everywhere. — [Schlosser & Kallemeyn 2000, Ecology 81:1371](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70022221) [abs]
- **[N-temp] Connectivity raises richness:** across 559 lakes and 854 streams, connectivity had a positive effect on species richness in both lakes and streams and helped explain composition. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10296844) [snip]
- **[EU, Spain; flagged] Below a lake:** fish abundance was correlated with summer oxygen concentration and fell at downstream sites; downstream richness was higher because of the nearby Ter River. — [Vila-Gispert et al. 2002](https://um.es/documents/4874468/18084291/vila-gispert-etal-2002.pdf/33b053ea-c788-40c8-a40a-26b80f6e88f8) [snip]

**Standing crops of warmwater stream fish (for scale)**
- **[N-temp, Indiana] Big Walnut Creek:** pools above a dam held ~**365 lb/acre (~410 kg/ha)**, pools below ~**750 lb/acre (~840 kg/ha)**, the difference mostly carp and gizzard shad; **centrarchids made up 5–28% of the weight, chiefly smallmouth bass, longear sunfish and rock bass**. — [Proc. Indiana Acad. Sci.](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/ias/article/download/6856/6863/12978) [snip]
- **[S, Virginia] Powell River:** smallmouth bass **2.6 kg/ha** and rock bass **2.2 kg/ha**, lower than most streams. — [VT thesis](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/8ebe311a-bade-4959-8fbe-0f2ae453af62) [snip]
- **[N-temp, Michigan; unverified]** Michigan smallmouth streams: total fish ~489–752 kg/ha with smallmouth 2–12% and pike 0.3–2% of biomass; another stream's mean standing crop (excluding minnows, darters, mudminnows) was 101 lb/acre (range 18–338); a third gave bass standing crop ~14.9 kg/ha, bass production 13.6 kg/ha/yr and **crayfish 43 g/m²**. — [MSU theses, e.g. d.lib.msu.edu/etd/43721](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/43721/FULL_TEXT/download) [snip; which thesis gave which number was not pinned, and one text was OCR-garbled]

### Inferences
- **Brook capacity, rough scale.** At 100–840 kg/ha, a ~1,200–1,600 m² brook carries roughly **12–135 kg of fish** in total (my arithmetic), of which centrarchids (sunfish + small bass) might be 5–30%: on the order of **1–40 kg, i.e. tens to a few hundred sunfish and small bass**. Set it by pool and run area, not riffle area: Hot Brook's 1.1-m mouth pool and 0.5-m run are the only water deep enough for >10-cm fish by the Power rule.
- **Who sits where (fits the angler's report):** pickerel and bigger bass in the mouth pool (>0.5 m, cover); sunfish in pools with overhanging alders (MBL Q2: pumpkinseed home ranges "mainly pools with overhanging trees"); small bass at run and pool tails; minnow schools and young fish on riffles and margins, passing through. Deep-pool fish density per m² should be lower for small fish and higher for large fish.
- **The brook's young bass and sunfish are lake-born.** Following Schlosser & Kallemeyn, seed the brook's juvenile centrarchids and perch by dispersal from Upper (and up from Lower), not by in-brook spawning alone.
- **Brook vs lake per area.** No paired study exists. Lake fish biomass per hectare (from the project's lake calibration) can be compared with the 100–840 kg/ha stream range; a brook fed by lake zooplankton, drift and mussel-bed invertebrates should sit at the upper half of the stream range in summer and near zero for large fish in winter (few piscivores winter in streams, MBL Q2).

### Gaps
- No density or biomass of fish in a short lake-to-lake connecting stream, or a brook-vs-lake comparison, was found for any region.
- No northeastern pool/riffle fish standing-crop data for small warmwater streams (Maine, NH, NY, Ontario) were found.
- No study of cover (alder overhang, wood, ribbon-leaved plants) as a carrying-capacity driver for sunfish or pickerel in small streams gave numbers.

## 4. Dry years: when the outlet nearly stops

### Takeaway
A natural lake outlet is a threshold ("fill and spill"): once evaporation draws the lake below the outlet sill, outflow ceases, and summer rain may not restore it. The brook then becomes a chain of shrinking, warming pools. In northeastern droughts (2016, 2020) streams hit record lows, fish crowded into isolated pools, oxygen fell, and fish kills followed. In drying pools, density is set by what each pool can hold ("the population was thereby reduced to correspond to the carrying capacity of each pool"); small fish are pushed into the shallows by bass, and terrestrial predators (unidentified in the Arkansas experiment; herons and kingfishers in Power 1987) then take the larger fish there. Recovery is fast where a source is next door: in a warmwater stream that dried in 1953, **21 of 29 species were back within two weeks** of full flow and 25 by the end of the first summer, while a few species took about two years (longear sunfish) to more than four (two small minnow-like species). For Hot Brook, both lakes are permanent sources, so recolonisation after a dry summer should take days to weeks.

### Cited Findings
**How outlets stop [subarctic Canada; flagged]**
- In a northern Canadian lake chain (2004), snowmelt raised the lakes above their outlet elevations to generate outflow; continued summer evaporation drew storage below the outflow thresholds, so outflow ceased; "summer rainfall … was insufficient to overcome storage deficit to re-establish flow connectivity among all lakes". The authors model the chain by fill-and-spill: antecedent storage, water balance and outflow thresholds. — [Woo & Mielko 2007, Hydrol. Process. (McMaster record)](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1727611) [abs]; [Mielko 2006 MSc thesis](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/handle/11375/21135)

**Northeastern drought evidence [NE/ME]**
- In 2016 many northeastern waterways set record low flows; in Maine, the **Saco River at Cornish had its lowest 7-day average streamflow in a 99-year record** (late September). Extreme drought (D3) appeared in parts of New York and New England in August 2016. — [NRCC 2016 drought assessment](https://www.nrcc.cornell.edu/regional/drought/pubs/assessment_2016.pdf) [snip]
- Connecticut closed parts of the Farmington River to fishing after several fish kills (2016), apparently a first; low, warm water pushed trout and salmon into deep pools and spring holes. — [AP via Fox News, Aug 2016](https://www.foxnews.com/us/new-england-drought-means-bolder-bears-stressed-fish.print) [snip]
- MassWildlife: as surface and groundwater flow drop, stream fish are squeezed into shrinking, isolated pools; warm stagnant pools lose dissolved oxygen, and trapped fish become easy prey. — [Mass.gov, "Fish, wildlife and drought"](https://www.mass.gov/news/fish-wildlife-and-drought) [snip]
- [ME] In August 2020, Maine DEP reported Aroostook County stream flows well below the August median and low lake and pond levels, and warned that withdrawals could dewater stream segments and cause fish kills. — [Maine DEP news](https://maine.gov/dep/news/news.html?id=3157594) [snip]
- [NE, Connecticut] Low summer flow cut riffle insect biomass but not pool insects, hitting filterers hardest (R² 0.90 with % riffle), with drift rising immediately after flow reduction (BFW §5: Walters & Post 2011; Dewson et al. 2007).

**What happens in the pools [S, Midwest, Plains; flagged]**
- **Concentration:** below an overflow dam on the Neosho River at Iola, Kansas (October 1953), the river had ceased to flow and a pool of **less than one acre** held an estimated **40,000 fish**, about 30,000 of them channel catfish. "The population was thereby reduced to correspond to the carrying capacity of each pool in the stream bed." The upper Neosho dried completely in 1956 except for one large pool. — [Deacon 1961, Univ. Kansas Publ. Mus. Nat. Hist. 13(9)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34787/34787-h/34787-h.htm) [ft, first ~55% read]
- **Predation in pools:** in the same drought, clearer water in the remaining pools exposed spawning sites and increased predation on young channel catfish; the piscivorous flathead catfish persisted better than channel catfish, its piscivory "may have favored their survival" in concentrated pools. — same source [ft]
- **Bass push prey shallow:** smallmouth bass added to pools during summer drying pushed small minnows and small and large crayfish into shallow habitat within the pools (Magoulick 2004, as described in Dekar & Magoulick 2013). — [Dekar & Magoulick 2013, Southeast. Nat. 12:197](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70150315) [abs]
- **And then terrestrial predators take them:** in 24 artificial 10-cm-deep pools in a drying Arkansas creek, terrestrial predators reduced central stoneroller survival, and **larger individuals were more susceptible**; small crayfish had low survival at all depths, large crayfish were taken much less, especially over larger substrate. — same source [abs]
- **Drought survival is species-specific:** in mesocosm drought experiments, survivorship was lower under the drought treatment for blackstripe topminnow and **longear sunfish**, with no difference for other species; bigeye shiners showed no residual drop in fat stores despite lower condition right after the drought. — [Matthews & Marsh-Matthews, in *Community Ecology of Stream Fishes* (AFS)](https://fisheries.org/?p=62730) [snip]
- **Refuges and recolonists:** pools, perennial reaches and off-channel habitat are the main drought refuges and the source of recolonists; refuges relieve extreme conditions and supply source populations when conditions improve (Magoulick & Kobza 2003, as cited; BFW §5).

**Recovery**
- **[N-temp, Illinois] Smiths Branch, a small warmwater stream:** after the 1953–54 drought most pools dried in fall 1953 (survivors in the few remaining pools were removed with rotenone for the study). Fish first moved into only the first few pools above the stream mouth until full flow returned; **within 2 weeks of full flow, 21 of the 29 regularly occurring species had moved into most of the stream**; 25 had entered by the end of the first summer. The **longear sunfish was not taken until fall 1955**; blackstripe topminnow and hornyhead chub had not returned by September 1957. Fish crossed **very shallow riffles during two brief periods of slight flow**. Invertebrates re-established fast enough to feed the incoming fish; the earliest abundant insects were apparently adults that had flown in from other streams. — [Larimore, Childers & Heckrotte 1959, Trans. Am. Fish. Soc. 88:261](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1994573246) [abs]
- **[S, Mississippi] After severe drought:** post-drought fish and crayfish assemblages differed from pre-drought ones at sites that had dried, but not at sites that kept flowing; repopulation came from both immigration and reproduction. — [USFS Treesearch record](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/20833) [snip; attribution to this record not verified]
- **Invertebrates:** recovery after drought is rapid for macroinvertebrates with drying-resistant stages or high mobility. — [Boulton 2003, Freshwater Biology (UNE record)](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/59) [snip]
- **[N-temp] Kansas, slower:** after flow returned in 1957 "large areas [were] unoccupied by fish"; orange-throated darters were not common until the third summer; the Neosho madtom was common again by 1959; gravel chub recovered slowly. — [Deacon 1961](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34787/34787-h/34787-h.htm) [ft]
- A Connecticut lake-outlet alewife run held fish in the lake in a drought year (MBL Q2).

### Inferences
- **A dry-year sequence for Hot Brook** (adds to BFW §5's invertebrate sequence):
  1. **Late July–August, Upper falls toward its sill.** Outflow ∝ (stage − sill)^1.5 (a broad-crested weir form; my assumption for a natural cobble lip), so the last few centimetres of head carry most of the drop in discharge. At ~0.02× mean flow the riffles are the "walk-through, almost out of the water" reaches the angler describes.
  2. **Riffle passage closes first** (MBL Q2 depth rule): deep-bodied fish (bass, bullhead, white perch, big suckers) stop crossing, then all fish. Each fish ends up wherever it was: Upper's mouth pool, the run, or Lower's outfall. Small fish can still cross "very shallow riffles" during brief flow pulses (Smiths Branch), so a summer storm should briefly reopen passage for minnows and young fish.
  3. **Pools concentrate fish and predators.** Prey crowd into the 1.1-m mouth pool and the run, where pickerel and bass already sit. Bass push small fish into the pool margins, where herons, kingfishers, mink, raccoons and eagles (the user has seen bald eagles) take the larger ones. The sim should raise piscivore encounter rates with prey density in the pool, plus a bird/mammal hazard for >3–4-cm fish in water <20 cm.
  4. **Heat and oxygen.** Once flow stops, the mouth pool no longer gets fresh lake water. Its afternoons warm toward 27–30 °C and its nights lose O₂ (respiration by crowded fish, mussels and decaying plants with no reaeration from riffles). Saturation at 28 °C is only ~7.8 mg/L (standard tables), so a crowded, still pool can go hypoxic by dawn: add a dawn-hypoxia hazard scaled by fish and mussel biomass per pool volume.
  5. **The lake subsidy stops.** No outflow means no seston, zooplankton or drift delivery; filterers and mussels starve or are exposed (BFW §5). Mussel exposure mortality is a gap.
  6. **Recovery in days to weeks.** When autumn rain refills Upper over its sill (October–November high flows; `weather_and_catchment_hydrology.md`), recolonisation should follow the Smiths Branch timetable: most species back within ~2 weeks, from both lakes, with insects returning first as flying adults. Sunfish may lag (longear took 2 years in Illinois), but Hot Brook's sunfish have a lake next door.
- **Disconnection is a lake-level threshold, not a stream property.** Model it in the lake's water balance: brook discharge = f(Upper stage − sill), 0 below the sill. Lower then gets no inflow from Upper, so Lower's own level falls faster; its outlet (to Baskahegan Stream) may stop too.

### Gaps
- No study measured fish or invertebrates in a **lake-outlet** stream through a disconnection and reconnection; the cases above are tributary and mainstem streams, mostly southern or Plains.
- No Hot Brook sill elevation, stage record, or normal summer lake-level drop was found; no measured water-level declines for unregulated Maine lakes in recent droughts (2016, 2020, 2022, 2025) were found.
- No measured temperature or DO series from an isolated pool below a warm lake outlet was found.
- Unionid (*Elliptio*) mortality from exposure or hypoxia during outlet drying was not found.
- Unverified (search summary, source not pinned): a Florida karst (solution-hole) study found native small fish in shallow holes that dry annually while predatory species dominated deeper holes, with "tremendous losses of fish biomass" once water fell below 46 cm of the ground surface. Possibly [USGS pub 70026854](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70026854); check before use.
- Matthews & Marsh-Matthews (2003, Freshwater Biology) and Magoulick & Kobza (2003) full texts were not retrieved.

## 5. Beaver dams and debris in a connecting brook (brief)

### Takeaway
Beaver dams are leaky, flow-dependent barriers: passage over them improves at high flow and for larger fish, and gets worse at low flow (juvenile salmonid passage over beaver-dam analogues fell from **~76% in early summer to ~21% in late summer**). In a drought the same dams and their ponds hold water and act as refuges. In a lake-to-lake brook, a dam would split the brook into a pond (slack, warm, silty, losing the outlet's filter feeders) and a riffle reach below, and would decouple passage from lake level.

### Cited Findings
- **[EU, Scotland] Brown trout:** the barrier effect of beaver dams got worse under **low flow**; high flows and larger fish improved upstream passage; low flows can hold fish below dams and delay spawning. — [Needham et al. 2025, Univ. Southampton record](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/500916) [snip]
- **[W, California] Juvenile coho over beaver-dam analogues:** passage fell from ~**76%** early in summer to **21%** late in summer, which the authors attribute partly to fish behaviour, not just the structures. — [Pollock et al., Scott River](https://scottriver.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Do-beaver-dam-analogues-act-as-passage-barriers-to-juvenile-coho.pdf) [snip]
- **Review:** many native fish that evolved with beavers pass dams readily, especially at higher spawning-season flows (lists Lokteff, Roper & Wheaton 2013, "Do beaver dams impede the movement of trout?"). — [Utah State Univ. low-tech restoration review](https://lowtechpbr.restoration.usu.edu/resources/Topics/02_Science/fish) [snip]
- **[N-temp, Minnesota] Beaver mosaic near a lake:** upland beaver ponds held more fish than lowland, collapsed ponds or streams; collapsed ponds and streams were richest; **geological barriers at drainage outlets reduced abundance and richness**. — [Schlosser & Kallemeyn 2000](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70022221) [abs]
- Beaver ponds can sustain water and fish habitat in drought and low flow (advocacy fact sheet, not peer-reviewed). — [Battle River Watershed Alliance](https://www.battleriverwatershed.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/MIR_BeaverAndFish_FactSheet_MAY2020_FINAL_ART.pdf) [snip]

### Inferences
- **Optional "beaver year" event for the sim:** a dam at the brook's lower end raises the run into a pond. That adds pickerel, sunfish and bullhead habitat and loses dace and filterer riffle. It blocks upstream passage for large fish at summer low flow but passes small fish and everything at spring and autumn high flow. Because the dam holds water above it, the brook's pool stays wet in a drought.
- **Debris jams** (alder, wood) should act as small barriers at low flow and as cover for pickerel and sunfish at all flows.

### Gaps
- No study of beaver dams on a warmwater lake-to-lake connector, or of passage by sunfish, bass, pickerel or minnows over beaver dams, was found. Lokteff et al. 2013's results were not retrieved.
- Whether Hot Brook has had beaver dams is unknown; ask the angler.
