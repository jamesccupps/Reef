# Fish movement between connected lakes through short connecting streams (for Upper ↔ Lower Hot Brook Lake)

Target system: Upper (~375 ha, 5.5 m max) and Lower (~280 ha, ~4 m max) Hot Brook Lake. Both are shallow, unstratified, tea-coloured and warmwater. They are joined by a ~200-m, ~8-m-wide brook of riffles and runs, with a ~1.1-m pool at Upper's outlet. In dry years the brook nearly dries.

This note builds on `docs/research_notes/Whole lake 3D fish simulation/movement_between_lakes.md` (cited below as **"the earlier note"**) and does not repeat its sources. Those sources are: Chizinski 2016 (Riley Creek pike and trap counts), Marcy-Quay & Kraft 2021 (sucker commuting), Parsons & Reed 2005 (Minnesota bluegill/crappie chain), Okoboji and Seattle perch, Millers Lake smallmouth, Rideau, Mattamuskeet, Skov (Danish roach), and the alewife outlet pulses. Where a new source confirms or contradicts the earlier note, this note says so.

## Q1. Tagging, telemetry and mark–recapture studies of movement between connected lakes: who moves, what fraction, when, how far, and why

### Takeaway
- **In a natural northeastern lake chain, true lake-to-lake movement is rare and is dominated by white suckers and brown bullheads.** In the Adirondack Moose River lakes, 43 of 4,649 recaptured fish (0.9 % of recaptures, ~0.13 % of all tagged fish) had changed lakes. 31 of those 43 were white suckers and 8 were bullheads. Only 2 yellow perch, 1 pumpkinseed and 1 rock bass moved.
- **Movement rises steeply with how connected a lake is.** In a Wisconsin chain, walleye movement was 11 % from lakes with 1 connection, 21 % with 2 and 50 % with 3. Esocids (muskellunge) moved far more than percids.
- **Chain pickerel make summer excursions out of their lake.** Telemetry of 30 chain pickerel in George Lake, Nova Scotia shows "extended summer migrations beyond George Lake" and winter inactivity. This is the only chain pickerel inter-water telemetry found.
- **Within a lake or stream, most sunfish, rock bass and bullheads hold small home areas for years, and the larger fish are the ones that stray.**

### Cited Findings

**Adirondack lake chain: full numbers (Daniels et al. 2008, *Northeastern Naturalist* 15(4):577–588)** [ft of the journal HTML page]
- **Setting:** Moose River watershed, Herkimer Co., NY. Monitored since 1995.
  - Fish were tagged in **Moss, Rondaxe and Dart lakes** (mean **63 ha**, mean **3.5 km apart**). Big Moose and Cascade lakes were also sampled.
  - Inlet and outlet stream reaches of **50–100 m** were sampled in summers 1998, 2001 and 2004.
- **Tagging:**
  - **33,875 fish tagged**, and **4,649 recaptured at least once**. The page states "20.4 %" as the recapture rate, but 4,649/33,875 = 13.7 %. The 20.4 % may count repeat recaptures; I could not resolve it.
  - Minimum tagged sizes: brown bullhead > 80 mm SL, white sucker > 90, rock bass > 75, pumpkinseed > 75, yellow perch > 90.
- **Movers:**
  - **43 fish moved between lakes (≈ 0.9 % of recaptures).**
  - **White sucker 31** (≈ **2.7 % of sucker recaptures**), **brown bullhead 8**, **yellow perch 2**, **pumpkinseed 1**, **rock bass 1**.
- **Direction:**
  - **Upstream (24 fish):** 15 suckers, 6 bullheads, and 1 each of rock bass, pumpkinseed and perch.
  - **Downstream (15 fish):** 13 suckers, 1 bullhead, 1 perch.
  - **Between the two upstream lakes via Rondaxe (5 fish):** 3 suckers, 1 perch, 1 bullhead.
  - So the moves were **not mainly downstream drift**.
- **Time at large before turning up in another lake:**
  - range **21–3,251 days**, mean **~715 days**;
  - most **117–1,808 days**;
  - fastest **21 days**: two suckers tagged in late spring 1997.
- **Stocked bass as a spread test:**
  - **Largemouth:** 500 + 500 were stocked into Rondaxe in spring 1998–99. They were caught in **Dart by 2000**, in the **streams by 2001**, in **Moss by 2003**, and **upstream of Moss by 2004**. That is a spread of **one lake per ~2–5 years**.
  - **Smallmouth** stocked into Rondaxe in 1997 were caught there in 1997–2005 but **never in Dart or Moss**.
  - One **rainbow smelt** (106 mm) reached Rondaxe from Moss in 2000.
- **Communities:** the streams held **17 taxa** and the lakes **18**. Brook stickleback was found only in the streams; smelt and lake trout only in the lakes.
- — [Daniels et al. 2008, NENA page](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml)
- **This corrects the earlier note.** The earlier note (from the Audubon summary) says "~1 % of tagged fish". The paper's figure is **~0.9 % of recaptured fish**, which is **~0.13 % of tagged fish**, over a mean of ~2 years at large.

**Wisconsin lake chain: walleye and muskellunge (Weeks 2006, UW–Stevens Point MS thesis; Manitowish Chain, Vilas Co.)** [abs] [sur: walleye for perch/bass; muskellunge for pickerel]
- **Tagging:**
  - 10 connected lakes. **7,427 walleye** and **491 muskellunge** were T-bar tagged, and **34 walleye and 36 muskellunge** radio-tagged.
  - From May 2004 to Oct 2005, **23 %** of tagged walleye and **19 %** of tagged muskellunge were recovered.
- **Recovered in the tagging lake:**
  - walleye **85 %** (17–95 % by lake);
  - muskellunge **59 %** (0–92 %).
- **Spawned in the same lake (2004–05):**
  - walleye **87 %** (33–97 %);
  - muskellunge **55 %** (0–91 %).
- **Movement by the number of the lake's connections to the chain:**

  | Connections | Walleye | Muskellunge |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | **11 %** | 74 % |
  | 2 | **21 %** | 26 % |
  | 3 | **50 %** | 50 % |

  - The muskellunge row is inconsistent as reported; the abstract itself calls the musky pattern "less consistent".
- **Recommendation:** manage walleye lake by lake, and muskellunge as one chain-wide stock.
- — [MINDS@UW record](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/81151)

**Chain pickerel: the only telemetry with movement beyond a lake (Sauriol, Nilsen & Avery, Acadia University / Kejimkujik NP; conference abstract, Oct 2024)** [abs]
- **Tagging:** **61 pickerel (31–62 cm TL)** were acoustic-tagged in George Lake. **30**, tagged in 2021, gave **12 months** of preliminary data.
- **Results:**
  - "various movement behaviors between individuals and between seasons, such as **extended summer migrations beyond George Lake**, and periods of **inactivity in the winter**";
  - "distinct habitat use … between individuals".
- No fractions, distances or timing are given.
- — [Sauriol et al. abstract, MTRI](https://merseytobeatic.ca/sites/merseytobeatic.ca/files/agendafiles/Sauriol_Felixe.pdf)
- **Possible conflict:** a CBC report said Kejimkujik was still free of both smallmouth and pickerel, with smallmouth ~2 km from the park ([CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/nova-scotia/invasive-fish-funding-kejimkujik-national-park-1.4038651) [snip]). The report's date was not seen. It probably predates the pickerel's arrival in George Lake.

**Within-lake site fidelity: why most sunfish and bullheads do not cross** (lake studies summarised in Gerking 1953) [ft of Gerking; the primary papers were not read]
- **Douglas Lake, Michigan (Rodeheffer 1941):** "not a single fish of the **4,557 marked at Grapevine Point** from 1937 to 1939 were recovered at any other point in the lake."
- **Third Sister Lake, Michigan (Ball 1947; 10 acres):**
  - bluegill: **18 of 27** recaptures had moved **≤ 65 yd (~60 m)**, and 12 had not moved;
  - yellow bullhead: **19 of 27** within **100 yd**;
  - largemouth showed **no tendency** to stay in one place.
- **Lake Myosotis, New York (Shoemaker 1952; 90 acres):** **pumpkinseed and yellow bullhead had home areas**, with some evidence of homing.
- **Foots Pond, Indiana (47.5 acres):** white crappie in the two ends of the pond did not mix freely. Only **4 of 67** recaptured in the south had come from the north, and **3 of 22** the other way.
- — [Gerking 1953, *Ecology* 34:347–365](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf)
- **Clear Lake, Iowa:** displaced pumpkinseed, bluegill and green sunfish returned to their home areas more often than to other sites, over displacements of ≤ ~2,200 ft (~670 m). **Larger fish homed more strongly**, and home areas seemed to be held only for relatively short periods. — [CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/64833382) [snip; HTTP 403 on fetch]

**Drivers seen in the new sources**
- **Connectivity (number of connections):** walleye 11 → 21 → 50 % as connections rose from 1 to 3. — [Weeks 2006](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/81151) [abs]
- **Body size: larger stream fish stray more.**
  - Stray longear sunfish were larger than resident ("home") fish in all four years: mean fork length **104/103/111/113 mm vs 86/99/106/110 mm**.
  - Gerking: "the larger fish of a given species wandered more than smaller ones."
  - — [Gerking 1953](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf) [ft]
- **Not the usual spawning or feeding runs:** the Adirondack interlake moves were not tied to the usual upstream feeding or spawning migrations (earlier note, confirmed by the time-at-large spread of 21–3,251 days). — [Daniels 2008](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml)

### Inferences
- **[inf] Conflict with the earlier note's per-species "re-settle" rates.** The earlier note proposed re-settling rates of 10–25 %/yr for yellow perch and 5–15 %/yr for pumpkinseed and redbreast. Those came from the Minnesota bluegill chain (3–36 %/yr) and the Seattle urban-canal perch (80 %).
  - The only **natural northeastern** chain (Adirondacks) shows perch and pumpkinseed **almost never** changing lakes (2 and 1 of 43 movers), while suckers and bullheads made up 91 % of movers.
  - Possible reasons for the gap:
    - the Minnesota and Seattle connections are wide, navigable channels;
    - their recaptures came from anglers across the whole chain;
    - the Adirondack lakes are ~3.5 km apart.
  - Hot Brook's lakes are only ~200 m apart, but through a riffled brook.
  - **Suggested Hot Brook starting values:**

    | Species | Re-settle / yr | Note |
    |---|---|---|
    | White sucker | 3–10 % | |
    | Brown bullhead | 2–5 % | |
    | Yellow perch | 1–5 % | |
    | Pumpkinseed, redbreast | 1–5 % | most "brook use" is by brook residents (Q4), not lake-to-lake transfer |
    | Chain pickerel | 5–15 % summer excursions | ~2–5 % re-settle |
    | Smallmouth bass | 2–5 % | |

  - Validate these by checking that the two lakes' size and age structures can diverge.
- **[inf] Distance from the mouth matters more than lake area.** Most fish hold home areas of tens to hundreds of metres for years (Rodeheffer, Ball, Shoemaker, Clear Lake). Only fish whose home area overlaps Upper's outlet basin (the 5.5-m hole is by the outlet) or Lower's inflow mouth are realistic candidates to cross. This matches the earlier note's Mattamuskeet-based rule.
- **[inf] Large fish are the movers.** Weight crossing probability up with length within a species (Gerking strays; Clear Lake homing; Marcy-Quay commuters).
- **[inf] Pickerel:** the angler sees pickerel "passing through and hanging out" in summer. That fits the George Lake summer excursions and the high mobility of esocids in the Manitowish chain. Give pickerel a summer (June–Sept) excursion state, with brook sojourns of days to weeks, and near-zero movement in winter.

### Gaps
- **No telemetry or PIT study** of smallmouth, pickerel, white perch, redbreast, fallfish or dace moving **between two lakes joined by a short brook** in the northeast was found.
- George Lake (Sauriol) has only an abstract. The thesis was not found.
- Daniels et al. give no per-species sizes of movers and no annual rate.
- Weeks 2006: only the abstract. Channel types and lengths in the Manitowish chain are not on the record page.
- **Blocked or unretrieved:**
  - Clear Lake sunfish homing paper: HTTP 403;
  - Shoemaker 1952 and Rodeheffer 1941: not retrieved, seen only via Gerking;
  - Lake Ontario smallmouth displacement study (J. Freshw. Ecol. 1988: **26 %** of displaced smallmouth returned to their tributary capture sites [snip]): the SUNY repository record returned HTTP 403, so its link is not cited.

## Q2. Counts of fish passing through connecting channels and outlets (directional trap and weir counts) by species and season

### Takeaway
- **One-way outflow traps show the same picture each time:**
  - downstream passage is **highest in spring (April–May)**;
  - it comes in **pulses on rain and high-water days**;
  - it is dominated by **young and small fish of the abundant centrarchids**;
  - it varies **~50-fold between otherwise similar lakes**, from 1–6 to 85–223 fish per acre per season.
- **Not every species leaves in proportion to its abundance.** Bass were over-represented in the outflow, while golden shiners and goldfish "prefer to stay". Pumpkinseed left mostly in **September–October**.

### Cited Findings

**Four Maryland ponds: outflow catch-box counts by month and species (Elser, Proc. SE Assoc. Game & Fish Comm., pp. 174–184; study 1958–60)** [ft] [S, mid-Atlantic coastal plain]
- **Ponds:** 26–45 acres, 5–12 ft max depth, 2–4 ft mean, pH 6.0–6.5, soft water (14–22 ppm CaCO₃). These are close to Hot Brook's soft, shallow, warmwater type, though far smaller.
- **Method:** a ½-inch hardware-cloth catch-box across the tailrace caught everything that went over. Fish were fin-clipped and returned above the dam.
- **Urieville Lake 1958 (35 ac), April–October:**
  - **2,967 fish**: largemouth **1,131** (44 % legal, ≥ 9 in), bluegill **715**, black crappie **1,097**, others 24 (5 white perch, 19 pumpkinseed);
  - by month: Apr **517**, May **1,632**, Jun 299, Jul 175, Aug 52, Sep 177, Oct 115.
- **Wye Lake 1959 (45 ac):**
  - **6,195 fish**: largemouth **4,899** (only **3 % legal**, so overwhelmingly young fish), bluegill 1,292;
  - by month: Apr 465, **May 2,633**, Jun 601, **Jul 1,398**, Aug 804, Sep 104, Oct 174.
- **Wye Lake 1960 (March–August):**
  - **10,686 fish**: largemouth 4,184 (3 % legal), bluegill **5,978**, alewife ~468, 3 brown bullhead, 52 golden shiner;
  - **April alone: 6,528.**
- **Low-loss ponds:**
  - **Frazier's 1959: 220.** Of these, **53 pumpkinseed, 52 of them in Sept–Oct**, plus 1 redbreast in Oct. One more redbreast went out at Wye in July 1959;
  - **Frazier's 1960: 94;**
  - **Linchester 1959 (9 weeks): 34.** These included creek chubsucker, golden shiner, pumpkinseed, yellow perch, minnows and eel.
- **Per acre per season:**
  - high-loss ponds **85, 138 and 223**;
  - low-loss ponds **1–6**;
  - Lake Murphysboro, IL (160 ac; Louder 1958) averaged **21** (4–47);
  - Lake Loramie, OH (1,500 ac; Clark 1942) **19**: **57,408 fish in 2 seasons**, a minimum because of 1-inch mesh;
  - Little Grassy, IL (1,200 ac) **0.6**, also a minimum.
- **Flow:**
  - "the greatest loss is in the spring of the year, especially May";
  - "Of the bass leaving Wye Lake in July '59, **82 percent left during the high water from a single heavy storm**";
  - the Wye 1960 daily log shows 1,999 fish on 6 April after "heavy rain for 2 days, water 7 inches high over spillway", and 1,241 on 10 May after a 3-inch rise;
  - but on 13 July a 4-inch rise brought "no increase";
  - so flow "probably explains much of the day-to-day variation at any individual pond" but not the differences between ponds.
- **Outlet form:**
  - High loss went with **smooth, laminar flow over shaped-concrete spillways**. Low loss went with **turbulent drop-board weirs**.
  - Elser's hypothesis is that turbulence or sound at the lip repels fish.
- **Species bias:**
  - largemouth were "better represented in the catch box than in the lake";
  - goldfish, golden shiner, warmouth and (2 of 3 cases) white crappie "seem to prefer to stay in the pond".
- — [Elser, SEAFWA PDF](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/ELSER-174.pdf)

**Reservoir escapement review (Lewis et al. 2023, *NAJFM* 43:352–368)** [abs]
- **Coverage:** 57 papers (1942–2021) on 49 species.
- **Findings:**
  - annual escapement ranged **0–100 %**;
  - it was **higher through spillways and surface outlets**;
  - **smaller-bodied fish escaped at higher rates**;
  - escapement was generally **positively related to discharge**;
  - escapement losses "can be comparable to natural and harvest mortality".
- — [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/reservoir-fish-escapement-in-north-america-a-historical-review-an/)

**Other outflow counts** [snip]
- **Elkhead Reservoir, Colorado (2003–04):** bluegill and black crappie escaped most often. Escapement peaked at high spillway discharge, with a diel pattern. — [CWCB record](https://dnrweblink.state.co.us/CWCB/0/doc/136308/Page2.aspx)
- **Par Pond and L Lake, South Carolina:** escapement was greatest in spring and early summer, and enhanced by high water and surface discharge. — same search [snip; source page not opened]

**Two-way trap counts in a connecting creek** (Riley Creek, Minnesota: bluegill 31–592, black bullhead 219–563, minnows up to 1,409 per site-year, and others)
- These are in the earlier note and are not repeated here.
- Lievense's (1947) inlet counts (654 pumpkinseed and 1,786 bluegill in 3 months) are in `habitat3d.md` §11.

### Inferences
- **[inf] Scale for Hot Brook's downstream flux (Upper → brook → Lower).**
  - Per-acre outflow rates span **1–6** (low-loss), **~20** (Murphysboro, Loramie) and **85–223** (smooth spillways) fish per acre per season.
  - Applied to Upper's ~925 acres, that gives roughly **1,000–5,500**, **~18,000**, or **80,000–200,000** fish a year.
  - Per-acre scaling from 26–45-acre ponds is unreliable. Flux should scale with the **population living near the outlet and with discharge**, not with lake area.
  - A natural, turbulent riffle outlet is closer to Elser's low-loss "turbulent" outlets. A **low-thousands-per-year downstream flux, mostly age-0 and age-1 centrarchids**, is a defensible starting point.
- **[inf] Timing rule.**
  - Put **~50–60 % of downstream passage in April–May**: Urieville 72 % and Wye 1960 ~85 % in Apr–May; Wye 1959 50 %.
  - Put the rest in **storm pulses**, where one storm can carry most of a month's movers (82 %).
  - Shift pumpkinseed to an **autumn (Sept–Oct) peak** (Frazier's: 52 of 53).
- **[inf] Species weighting.** Over-represent bass among the movers. Under-represent golden-shiner-like schooling cyprinids that stay in the lake. Among Hot Brook's minnows, that weighting is uncertain for fallfish (see Q3).
- **[inf] Direction.** These are **one-way** counts over dams. Hot Brook's brook lets fish return. The Adirondack and Riley Creek data show that **upstream** moves are as common as downstream ones once there is no barrier.

### Gaps
- **No two-way, species-by-month counts for a natural northeastern connecting brook** (no dam) were found.
- MDIFW weir work targets salmonids. Its by-catch counts are in `fallfish_spawning_brook.md`.
- Louder 1958 and Clark 1942 were seen only through Elser's summary table.
- The Par Pond and L Lake source page was not opened.

## Q3. Lake-outlet and inlet migrations: spawning runs into connecting streams, and downstream emigration of young fish

### Takeaway
- The spawning runs are already covered in other notes:
  - white sucker and fallfish (`fallfish_spawning_brook.md`, `habitat3d.md` §11);
  - pike (the earlier note);
  - smallmouth stream-spawners (Millers Lake).
- What the new sources add:
  - **white perch** spawn in lakes' **shoreline shallows or larger tributaries** at **~10–15.5 °C**, in April–May (New England);
  - **young fish are the bulk of downstream emigrants** (97 % of escaping largemouth at Wye were sub-legal);
  - movement into and out of small connecting streams follows **discharge and temperature**: in spring, fish enter just after ice break-up. Adults leave as discharge falls. Juveniles leave in a second pulse as water cools toward freeze-up.
  - **larval drift is a minor dispersal route in shallow, weedy lakes**, though large connecting channels export huge numbers of perch larvae.

### Cited Findings

**White perch (inland New England)** [snip; agency pages]
- **Massachusetts:** white perch make "short migrations from estuarine habitats up into freshwater portions of streams and rivers to spawn on sand and gravel bars". They also spawn in lakes and ponds "in shallow shoreline water or in the larger tributaries". Spawning is mostly April–May at **50–60 °F (10–15.5 °C)**. Eggs are scattered, with no care. — [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/white-perch)
- **Vermont:** mature adults "move up tributary rivers" to spawn, in late spring to early summer at ~50–60 °F. — [VT Fish & Wildlife](https://www.vtfishandwildlife.com/learn-more/vermont-critters/fish/white-perch)
- **Downstream outflow (Maryland):** white perch were rare: 5 in April at Urieville, and 2–3 in May and September at Frazier's. — [Elser](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/ELSER-174.pdf) [ft]

**Seasonal entry and exit of a small seasonally flowing connecting stream (Heim et al. 2016, *Env. Biol. Fish.* 99:49–65)** [abs] [sur: Arctic grayling, Alaska]
- **Method:** **1,035 fish PIT-tagged**, with stream-wide antennas.
- **Entry:** juveniles and adults peaked in **June, right after ice break-up**.
- **Adult exit:** adults left in **late June – early July**, and **the rate of leaving rose as discharge fell**.
- **Juvenile exit:** in **two peaks**. The later one came **just before freeze-up (September)**, included smaller juveniles, and rose sharply as **daily minimum temperature fell**.
- — [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70168356)
- **A related grayling stream–lake network:** **70 %** of fish visited two lakes, **8 %** used one lake and **22 %** used only stream reaches. — same search results [snip; authorship not confirmed]

**Young fish dominate emigration**
- **Wye Lake:** only **3 %** of 4,899 (1959) and 4,184 (1960) escaping largemouth were legal size (≥ 9 in).
- **Urieville:** 44 % were legal size overall, and **63 % in April** but 28 % in May. So adults went out early in spring, and the young later.
- — [Elser](https://seafwa.org/sites/default/files/journal-articles/ELSER-174.pdf) [ft]
- Smaller-bodied fish escape at higher rates across 57 studies. — [Lewis et al. 2023](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/reservoir-fish-escapement-in-north-america-a-historical-review-an/) [abs]

**Larval drift and export**
- **Lake Saint-Pierre, Québec (a large, shallow fluvial lake):** there was weak support for current-driven drift. **Yellow perch larvae were strongly associated with habitat at "unexpectedly small sizes"**, and drift played a minor role. — [Bertolo et al. 2012, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3502328) [snip]
- **Detroit River (a large Great Lakes connecting channel):** estimated **yellow perch larval export of 319 million (2014) to 690 million (2013)** per year. — [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70227003) [snip]. This is a very large system and only shows that perch larvae do leave lakes through outlets.
- **Pere Marquette Lake → Lake Michigan:** of six taxa, only yellow perch were transported in substantial numbers (**~0.75 million larvae**). — [MSU thesis](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/6499/FULL_TEXT/view) [snip; repository access-limited, not verified]
- **Lake Ontario tributaries:** 96 % of larval fish drift at night, with blacknose dace among the most abundant (already in `habitat3d_new_species.md`).

### Inferences
- **[inf] White perch in Hot Brook.** Give spawning white perch a **night run into the brook's run and riffle tail at 10–15.5 °C (early to mid-May at Hot Brook)**, alongside a larger lake-shallows spawning. Expect few white perch among summer passers: they were rare in outflow traps.
- **[inf] Exit rule for the brook (Heim surrogate).**
  - **Adults leave the brook as discharge falls in early summer.** Fish should not wait to be stranded.
  - **Juveniles that used the brook leave as night-time minimum temperatures fall in September–October.**
  - This also suits Hot Brook's dry years: most fish should already have left the riffle reaches as flow dropped.
- **[inf] Young-of-year emigration.** Model downstream drift of age-0 sunfish and bass out of Upper as:
  - **post-swim-up (late June–August) "drift-and-settle"**, with a small background rate;
  - plus **storm pulses**;
  - plus a **spring pulse of yearlings (April–May)**.
  - Perch larvae should mostly stay in the lake (Lake Saint-Pierre), with a minor outflow export.
- **[inf] The angler's "schools of small minnows passing through".**
  - The candidates are **young fallfish**, which use both lake margins and streams in the north, or **blacknose dace**. Dace are a stream species whose larvae drift at night and whose adults keep small ranges (`habitat3d_new_species.md`).
  - **Daytime schools moving between the lakes are more consistent with young fallfish**: dace drift is nocturnal and larval, and adult dace are riffle residents. This is an inference and was not tested.

### Gaps
- **No study of young-of-year emigration through a natural northeastern lake outlet** was found for sunfish, bass, perch, fallfish or dace. Only spillway catch-boxes and large-channel larval surveys were found.
- No **fallfish** tagging or telemetry study (lake ↔ stream, adult or juvenile) was found.
- **No inland Maine white perch spawning-run counts** were found. The MDIFW white perch plan returned HTTP 403 in the earlier note.

## Q4. Effects of reduced connectivity (low water, drying, beaver dams, culverts, intermittent connecting streams)

### Takeaway
- **Even at normal flow, a long shallow riffle is a behavioural boundary.**
  - Only **5 of 191 recaptured longear sunfish crossed a 74-m riffle in four years**, and only 2 of 19 smallmouth. Displaced fish crossed back within 2 weeks, so it is not a physical wall.
  - Stream sunfish and rock bass live in **30–60 m** home areas, and smallmouth and suckers in **60–120 m**, **for years**.
- **As a stream dries:**
  - pools shrink;
  - **fish density in pools peaks partway through drying**;
  - **total fish numbers fall to "the carrying capacity of each pool"**: one < 1-acre pool held ~40,000 fish;
  - richness depends on pool size in drier years;
  - small fish avoid deep pools that hold bass.
- **Water that passes underground through boulders is a complete barrier** to pike colonisation, and map-based connectivity predicted pike presence with 95 % accuracy.
- **In Maine, thoroughfares between ponds pass fish at high water. At low water they trap fish, and beaver dams in outlets become barriers.**

### Cited Findings

**Riffles, pools and home ranges in small warmwater streams (Gerking 1953, Richland and Stott's Creeks, Indiana)** [ft] [MW]
- **Richland Creek layout:** two pools separated by a **242-ft (74-m) shallow riffle**. Sections were 117–246 ft (36–75 m) long. Fish were marked 1948–49 and recaptured 1948–51.
- **Longear sunfish:**
  - **81.7 %** of recaptures were "home" fish;
  - **5 of 191** crossed the riffle in 4 years;
  - "the majority … confine their activities to about **100–200 feet**";
  - in Stott's Creek, **11 of 12 (≈ 92 %)** were home after one month (the paper prints 80.1 %, which does not match 11/12).
- **Rock bass:** only **1** crossed the riffle in 4 years.
- **Green sunfish:** **6 of 8** and **10 of 11** were home within a summer.
- **Smallmouth bass (106–305 mm FL, mean 179):**
  - only **2 of 19** crossed the riffle, so their home range is "about twice" the sunfishes' (**200–400 ft, ~60–120 m**);
  - in Stott's Creek **7 of 7** were home;
  - among fish marked in 1948 and re-marked in 1949, **3 of 4** recaptured in 1951 had moved to the upstream pool. Gerking suggests smallmouth range more widely as they age.
- **Suckers:**
  - golden redhorse: **4 of 28** crossed;
  - hog sucker: **3 of 14** crossed; in Stott's Creek **66 of 73 (90.4 %)** were home after a month.
- **Riffle as a measure of resistance:** **17 of 80 strays** of all species crossed the long riffle in 4 years.
- **Transplant test:** **26 of 35** transplanted longears had **gone back over the riffle within 2 weeks**. "The riffle was a **behavioral rather than a mechanical barrier**."
- **Overcrowding:** overcrowding pushed residents out (37/39 home at 2 weeks → 23/33 at 6 weeks). An emptied section was refilled from the neighbouring one (4/19 → 8/17 strays in).
- **Low water and rain:** in Stott's Creek "the pools are **almost isolated during low water**, but there are several opportunities each summer for fish to move because the creek **rises rapidly after a moderate rain**."
- **Conclusion:** a stream's fish population is "**a series of discrete, natural units**". The strays are "a reserve supply … which can move and repopulate decimated areas."
- — [Gerking 1953](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf)

**Fish crowding in drying pools**
- **Ozark creeks, NW Arkansas, 2002–03 (Dekar & Magoulick 2007, *Ecol. Freshw. Fish*)** [ft] [S]:
  - **Pools:** 17–22 pools, **0.32–1.75 m** max depth, **9.2–226 m³**. Mean widths 5.4–8.3 m and mean depths 0.20–0.37 m, the same scale as Hot Brook.
  - **Catch:** 14,135 fish of 22 species.
  - **Volume and density:** pool volume was lowest at the middle (August) sampling in **82 % / 68 %** of pools. **Density was highest then** in **47 % / 55 %** of pools.
  - **Depth and area:** total and minnow densities were **negatively related to maximum pool depth**, which the authors suggest may be "avoidance of larger predatory fish" (they also say it may simply be fish crowding as pools shrink). Richness rose with **pool area** in the drier year (2003).
  - **Cited earlier work:**
    - Magoulick 2000: densities of large fish (≥ 80 mm) and of **small (< 80 mm) sunfish including smallmouth bass** were "consistently and positively related to pool depth";
    - Schlosser 1988: brassy minnows were displaced from pools into riffles by **smallmouth bass**.
  - — [Dekar & Magoulick 2007](https://webapps.usgs.gov/mrgescp/documents/DekarandMagoulick_2007_Factorsaffectingfishassemblagestructureduringseasonalstreamdrying.pdf)
- **Ozarks (Hodges & Magoulick 2011):** pool-refuge use during drying was **species-dependent**. Creek chub supported the pool-refuge hypothesis, stonerollers partly, and bigeye shiner not at all. — [USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/refuge-habitats-fishes-during-seasonal-drying-intermittent-stream-movement-survival) [snip]
- **Kansas drought (Deacon 1961)** [ft, partial] [S plains; surrogate species]:
  - **Crowding:** in 1953 a pool of **< 1 acre held ~40,000 fish (~30,000 channel catfish)** after the river stopped. The drought "reduced [the population] to correspond to the **carrying capacity of each pool**." Clearer pool water **increased predation on young catfish**.
  - **Movement:** **2 of 6** recaptured black bullheads moved upstream **over riffles to the next pool** after 7–9 days. Green sunfish and spotted bass were "relatively sedentary".
  - **Recovery:** after flow returned (1957), several species took **2–3 years** to become common again (by 1959). The expanded habitat favoured spawning by "nearly the entire adult population" of catfish.
  - — [Deacon 1961, Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34787/34787-h/34787-h.htm)

**Beaver dams and passage**
- **Ipswich River, Massachusetts (Plum Island LTER):** **14 beaver dams** were studied against 9 controls. **10 were "relatively impermeable" at existing flow** and turned upstream pools into downstream runs or riffles. Results by species were not on the page. — [Smith, Mather et al. 2009 poster](https://asm2009.lternet.edu/2009/posters/do-beaver-dams-alter-coastal-ecosystem-structure-and-function-changing-fish-species-div/) [abs]
- **Scotland [EU]:** brown trout passed beaver dams more often at **high, rain-driven flows**, and **larger fish** were more successful. At low flow the dams were a "more significant obstacle". — [Guernsey Press report](https://guernseypress.com/news/uk-news/2025/03/13/brown-trout-can-pass-beaver-dams-study-finds) [snip]
- **Maine, MDIFW Little Fish Pond survey:** movement through the thoroughfare between Big Fish Pond and the smaller ponds is possible at **high water**. Young trout probably move into the smaller ponds and are "**trapped when water is low**", and "**beaver dams in the outlet** … would be barriers to fish movement during periods of **low flows**." — [MDIFW lake survey map](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/lake-survey-maps/somerset/fish_pond_little.pdf) [snip; HTTP 403 on fetch]
- **Maine, Manhanock and Harlow Ponds:** they are managed as one water because "fish pass freely under a bridge" in the causeway. Principal fisheries are chain pickerel, smallmouth and largemouth. — [MDIFW map](https://www.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/piscataquis/harlow_and_manhanock_pond.pdf) [snip]
- **Maine, Parker Pond ↔ Pleasant Lake thoroughfare:** "occasionally a brook trout, salmon, or cusk migrates up from Pleasant Lake". — [MDIFW map](https://www1.maine.gov/IFW/docs/lake-survey-maps/cumberland/parker_pond.pdf) [snip]

**Hard barriers and map-scale connectivity**
- **Northern Sweden pike (Spens, Lundqvist & Englund 2007, *J. Appl. Ecol.* 44:1127–1137)** [abs] [EU]:
  - **Model:** built on **87 lakes**. A map-derived measure of the steepest stream gradient from the nearest downstream pike source predicted pike presence in stream-connected lakes with **95.4 %** accuracy.
  - **Validation:** 49 introductions, 96 rotenone extirpations and 1,365 natural lakes gave **88.7–98.7 %** accuracy by basin, and **86.6 %** overall.
  - **The key barrier:** most lakes wrongly predicted to have pike were cut off by "**subsurface streamflow through bouldery areas**".
  - — [SLU record](https://research.slu.se/sv/publications/network-connectivity-and-dispersal-barriers-using-geographical-in/)
- **Passage-depth design guidance (engineering, not biology)** [snip]:
  - Wisconsin culvert guidance recommends a **6-inch (15-cm)** minimum depth, with a velocity–swim-distance chart including northern pike and white sucker — [UW-Extension Lakes "Fish Friendly Culverts"](https://www3.uwsp.edu/cnr-ap/UWEXLakes/Documents/ecology/shoreland/background/fish_friendly_culverts.pdf) [the link now redirects; snippet only];

**Winter refuge depends on connectivity**
- **Northern Wisconsin (Tonn & Magnuson 1982, *Ecology* 63:1149–1166)** [abs]:
  - 18 small lakes. **Centrarchid–*Esox* assemblages** occurred in lakes with high winter O₂, **or in low-O₂ lakes "when a stream or connecting lake offered refuge"**.
  - Where there was no refuge, low-O₂ lakes **lacked piscivores** and held *Umbra*–cyprinid assemblages.
  - Lake connectedness was related to summer richness in centrarchid–*Esox* lakes.
  - — [UAlberta ERA record](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/handle/123456789/15993)
- **Arctic Coastal Plain, Alaska (Laske et al. 2016, *Freshwater Biology*)** [abs] [sur, Arctic]:
  - **102 lakes**. A **permanent channel connection** was "an overriding factor": mean richness **3.6** (95 % CI 3.1–4.9) against **2.0** (1.7–3.3) without one.
  - In unconnected lakes, richness rose with in-lake winter refuge and with the potential for **ephemeral spring-flood connections**.
  - — [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70173403)

### Inferences
- **[inf] Brook residents.** The angler's "sunfish, and sometimes smaller bass, staying in the brook" matches Gerking.
  - A ~200-m brook with one deep mouth pool and runs can hold **resident pumpkinseed, redbreast and small smallmouth** for whole summers and successive years.
  - **Home areas:** **30–60 m for sunfish** and **60–120 m for small bass**, bounded by the riffles.
  - **Capacity:** the brook holds **~3–6 sunfish home areas and ~2–3 bass home areas** along its length.
  - **Crossing:** sunfish should cross the long riffle only rarely: **~1–3 % of residents a year** (5/191 recaptures in 4 years).
  - **Crowding:** when the brook is overcrowded, residents should be pushed out, and emptied reaches refilled from neighbours.
- **[inf] Big bass in the deep mouth pool.**
  - Large fish need depth: Magoulick's large-fish density rose with pool depth. Larger fish also stray more and range wider as they age (Gerking).
  - The 1.1-m pool is the brook's only deep water, so it should hold the **largest bass**, while small sunfish and minnows stay on the pool margins and riffle edges (Schlosser 1988 displacement).
- **[inf] The drought sequence for Hot Brook in dry years.**
  - **(1) Riffles cut first.** As riffle depth falls below ~**10–15 cm** (design guidance; I found no biological threshold for these species), **large, deep-bodied fish stop crossing** first.
  - **(2) Fish leave early.** Fish **leave the riffle reaches as discharge falls** (Heim), so few fish are stranded in the riffles themselves.
  - **(3) The pool crowds.** The **mouth pool's density rises** as its volume shrinks, peaking partway through the drought (Dekar & Magoulick). Predation by the bass and pickerel already in the pool rises, and small fish move to the pool edges.
  - **(4) The barrier closes completely.** Once surface flow disappears through the cobble, the brook is a **complete barrier**, like the Swedish "subsurface flow through boulders". The two lakes' fish are then fully separate until the autumn rains.
  - **(5) Movement resumes** on the first moderate rain that raises the riffles (Gerking: "rises rapidly after a moderate rain"; Elser's storm pulses).
- **[inf] Beaver risk.** A beaver dam on the brook would most strongly block **upstream** movement at **low flow**, and passage would resume at high flow, with larger fish first. If the sim adds beavers, gate passage by flow and length, not as a fixed wall.
- **[inf] Winter.** Hot Brook's lakes are shallow and tea-coloured, so the brook can be the **O₂ refuge that lets piscivores persist** through a hypoxic winter (Tonn & Magnuson). This supports the earlier note's and `habitat3d.md` §11's rule that hypoxic fish move toward the brook.

## Q5. Do connected lakes' fish communities behave as one metapopulation?

### Takeaway
- **Partly. Connected lakes share their species pool**: the same species are found in the lakes and their streams, and connected lakes are richer.
- **But they do not share individuals at rates that make them one freely mixing population.**
  - Lake-to-lake transfer is rare for most species (~0.1–1 % of tagged fish over years).
  - It is higher for suckers, bullheads and esocids, and much higher for well-connected lakes.
- **The working model in the literature:**
  - each lake (and each stream pool) is a **local population**;
  - occasional strays **rescue or recolonise** after local losses (winterkill, drought);
  - lake **extinction history matters more than isolation** for which species are present.
- **Recommendation for Hot Brook:** two linked populations with low, size-, species- and flow-dependent exchange, not one mixed pool.

### Cited Findings
- **Adirondacks (Daniels et al. 2008):**
  - lake and stream assemblages had the same species and richness, though relative abundances differed;
  - the authors argue lake fish should be managed as a "**watershed-wide metapopulation**";
  - they note that "even small movements can affect population structure, gene flow, recolonization and disease spread";
  - the per-lake transfer itself was ~0.9 % of recaptures (Q1).
  - — [NENA](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml) [ft]
- **South-central Ontario (Olden, Jackson & Peres-Neto 2001, *Oecologia* 127:572–585):**
  - **52 drainage lakes**, with **8 isolation measures** that included the "challenges" a fish meets moving between lakes;
  - at the **watershed scale**, community composition matched both **lake isolation and lake morphology**;
  - at the **individual-lake scale** the match varied widely;
  - isolation measures that account for the route did better than straight-line distance.
  - The search could not confirm that the lakes are in Algonquin Park.
  - — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s004420000620) [snip]
- **Wisconsin and Finland (Magnuson et al. 1998, *Ecology* 79:2941–2956):**
  - **55 Wisconsin lakes (0.2–86.9 ha)** and 114 Finnish lakes;
  - **extinction variables** (habitat severity, area, productivity) predicted richness and composition **better than isolation variables** (stream corridors, land barriers, source pools);
  - Wisconsin, with "a higher share of lakes without stream connections", showed stronger effects of horizontal barriers;
  - "the probability of an arrival of a new species is much less than that of an extinction".
  - — [UAlberta ERA record](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/handle/123456789/15898) [abs]
- **Wisconsin winter refuge (Tonn & Magnuson 1982):** piscivore assemblages persisted in low-O₂ lakes only where a stream or connecting lake gave refuge. — [ERA](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/handle/123456789/15993) [abs]
- **Wisconsin chain management unit (Weeks 2006):** walleye are lake-specific (85 % recovered in the tagging lake, 87 % spawn there). Muskellunge are chain-wide. — [MINDS@UW](https://minds.wisc.edu/handle/1793/81151) [abs]
- **Stream pools as local units (Gerking 1953):**
  - "a series of discrete, natural units rather than a single homogeneous, freely-mixing group";
  - strays are "a reserve supply … [that] can move and repopulate decimated areas".
  - — [Gerking 1953](https://www.fs.usda.gov/biology/nsaec/fishxing/fplibrary/Gerking-1953.pdf) [ft]
- **Genetics [sur]:**
  - **Lacustrine brook trout:** "very little genetic structure" among waterbodies, implying frequent movement where the setting allows; the authors favour watershed-scale management. — [Addison & Wilson 2010](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202010-19/Addison%20and%20Wilson%202010%20%20Panmixia%20in%20lacustrine%20brook%20trout.pdf) [snip]
  - **Burbot, Wind River basin, Wyoming (11,197 SNPs):** **lakes on the same tributary shared genetic clusters**, while different tributary systems were distinct, with differentiation tracking natural and human-made barriers, even though burbot are rarely caught in the connecting streams. — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70168353) [snip]
  - **Brown bullhead, Presque Isle Bay:** a single panmictic population (already in the earlier note).
- **Recolonisation speed:**
  - Introduced largemouth spread to the next Adirondack lake in **~2 years** and to the third in **~5 years**; introduced smallmouth **never** did. — [Daniels 2008](https://www.eaglehill.us/NENAonline/articles/NENA-15-4/18-Daniels.shtml)
  - Stream species took **2–3 years** to recover after a drought ended. — [Deacon 1961](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34787/34787-h/34787-h.htm) [S]
- **Invasive spread through connected waters (Nova Scotia)** [snip; advocacy and provincial pages]:
  - ~**326 waterbodies** have smallmouth and ~**166** have chain pickerel ([NS Salmon Association](https://nssalmon.ca/issues/aquatic-invasive-species/); not an official survey);
  - smallmouth use both lake and river habitat, which helps them spread through a watershed. Chain pickerel prefer densely vegetated lakes, which "slows but doesn't stop" their spread;
  - pickerel were illegally introduced into 95+ lakes ([NS LaHave River project 2019](https://novascotia.ca/fish/documents/2019_-_LaHave_River_Invasive_Species_Project.pdf));
  - **no spread rate in km per year was found**.
- **Crappie recruitment in a Minnesota chain:** larval catch failed to predict year-class strength because of "supplemental recruitment caused by emigration from other lakes" (Parsons et al. 2004; already in the earlier note). This is the one direct demographic sign of rescue between linked lakes.

### Inferences
- **[inf] Model Upper and Lower as two coupled local populations**, each with its own recruitment, density dependence and mortality, coupled by:
  - **(a) low adult re-settlement:** rare for perch and sunfish, more frequent for suckers and bullheads (Q1);
  - **(b) seasonal, mostly reversible excursions:** pickerel in summer, the pike-like spring run, sucker commuting, stream-spawning bass;
  - **(c) a pulsed downstream flux of young fish**, Upper → Lower, in spring and on storms (Q2–Q3);
  - **(d) brook residents:** a third, tiny local population in the pool and runs (Q4).
- **[inf] What the sim should show.**
  - The two lakes can **differ in size structure, abundance and year-class strength**. The angler reports smaller, fewer perch than fallfish and bass; the two lakes should be allowed to diverge.
  - They should **share the species list**.
  - **Recolonisation after a local loss** (for example, a winterkill of Lower's bass) should take **~2–5 years** through the brook, as Adirondack largemouth did. Recolonisation should **pause in drought years**, when the brook is a complete barrier.
- **[inf] Direction.**
  - Upper is upstream and its 5.5-m hole is by the outlet, so passive (young-fish) flux is **Upper → Lower**.
  - Active adult moves are **both ways in equal measure** (Adirondack 24 up vs 15 down).
  - The brook pool sits at Upper's outlet, so Upper's fish dominate the brook's resident and sojourn use.
