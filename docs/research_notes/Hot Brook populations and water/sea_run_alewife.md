# Sea-run (anadromous) alewife in Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake

**Scope.** This note covers what the sim needs in order to add *Alosa pseudoharengus* as a seasonal visitor to the two lakes:
- the adult run;
- the eggs, larvae and young in the lakes;
- their effects on zooplankton, nutrients, predators and competitors;
- the run sizes to expect here;
- the bioenergetics and tolerance parameters.

It does not repeat `drainage_species_and_eel.md` §4, which covers:
- alewives at Danforth since 2017;
- the 2023 Baskahegan fishway;
- the 2008 plan's 214,320 / 167,555 potential at 235 adults/acre and its 6/acre stocking;
- the state habitat layer.

Nor does it repeat `hot_brook_lake_records.md` (MDIFW's 2024 Lower chart listing "sea-run alewife").

## Takeaway

**They are here now.**
- MDIFW's 2024 chart for Lower lists sea-run alewife. Alewives have reached Danforth naturally since 2017, and the Penobscot run has grown to about 5.5–5.9 million river herring at Milford (2023–25).
- No count exists for Baskahegan Stream, Hot Brook or the lakes, and no stocking was ever done there.
- The **present-day default should be "alewives present, at a low colonising density"**, not zero (this updates the earlier note).

**Run size.**
- DMR's planning potential is **235 adults/acre (580/ha)**: about 209,000 for Upper and 161,000 for Lower on DEP acres.
- Mature restored Maine lakes realise roughly 100–230/acre (Damariscotta, Union/Graham).
- The whole Penobscot basin is at about 30% of its planned potential.
- A newly colonised lake about 200 km inland is far below that. **I suggest 15 adults/acre (≈13,000 Upper, 10,000 Lower) as the default.** The scenarios are:
  - 6/acre (restoration stocking);
  - 35/acre (DMR's sustainability floor);
  - 120 and 235/acre (restored, full).

**The adults.**
- **Size and age:** 260–320 mm TL and 150–350 g (Maine mean ≈ 230 g). Ages are 3–9, mostly first-time spawners at 4–5. Sex ratio is about 1:1, and 20–25% are repeat spawners.
- **Arrival:** they enter Maine waters from late April to early June at about 10–13 °C. Here I expect them from about mid-May into June.
- **Spawning:** at night in the shallows (0.15–3 m, mostly under 1 m) at 10.5–21 °C, peaking at 13–16 °C, a few days per group.
- **Feeding:** they do feed in the lake. At Lake George, Maine, 62–70% of stomachs held food: cladocerans, midges and *larval yellow perch* (35% of items in May 1992).
- **Leaving:** most survivors leave within 1–4 weeks; the outflow peak at Lake George came about 3–4 weeks after release.
- **Deaths:** freshwater adult mortality is 32–67% (Long Pond, Maine) to **66–100%, mean 90.7%** (Love Lake, Washington County, Maine).
- **Heat:** the adults' upper incipient lethal temperature is 23.5–24 °C, which the lakes reach in mid/late July.

**The young.**
- **Hatching and growth:** eggs hatch in about 3–12 days, depending on temperature. Larvae become juveniles at about 20 mm. Juveniles grow 0.3–1.4 mm/day (slowest when crowded) and are 40–100 mm by September.
- **Leaving:** they emigrate from **mid-July to early December**, in short pulses (more than 80% in 2–3 pulses of 1–2 days) cued by rain, flow rises and temperature drops. Larger, fatter fish go first.
- **Winter:** they cannot overwinter. No juvenile survived below 3 °C, and a 5 °C cold challenge kills them, so any left at ice-on die.
- **Production:**
  - emigrants per adult are 1.4–2.5 at the crowded Bride Lake (CT);
  - **12–3,209 emigrants per female at Love Lake, Maine**;
  - egg-to-emigrant survival is 0.0013–1%.

**Effects in the lake.**
- **Zooplankton:**
  - YOY become gape-able on prey over 1 mm by mid-June.
  - **Large cladocerans collapse from late May to mid-June and stay absent until the young leave** (October–November). They recover from December to early June.
  - Maine alewife lakes' summer cladoceran mean length is 0.35–0.52 mm, against 0.59–0.68 mm in a control.
- **Clarity:** Secchi depth and chlorophyll barely change in Maine studies.
- **Prey:**
  - smallmouth bass grow the same or better with alewives;
  - Maine white perch fed almost only on juvenile alewives by late summer;
  - YOY bass overlap with YOY alewives only where both eat sidid cladocerans.
- **Nutrients:** each adult carries about 0.42% P, ≈ 0.97 g P in a 230-g fish. Dead adults are a net P import, emigrants an export.

**Bioenergetics:** the published model is Stewart & Binkowski 1986 (FB4 "alewife"). I read its Table 1, and the values are given in FB4 notation in §5.

## 1. The adult run and spawning in lakes

### Cited findings

**Timing and temperature**
- **Maine DMR, River Herring Sustainable Fisheries Management Plan 2024** [FT] ([ASMFC copy](https://www.asmfc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ME_RiverHerringSFMP_2024.pdf)):
  - the commercial harvest usually starts in the last week of April, and many runs only in the first week of May;
  - timing is "progressively later" eastward;
  - the season closes June 5 (it can be extended to June 15);
  - bluebacks start around June 5.
- **Kircheis, Trial, Boucher et al. 2002, rev. 2004** [FT]: "Adults enter Maine rivers from early May to early June." Spawning temperatures are 12.8–15.5 °C. — *Introduction of anadromous alewives into Lake George, Maine*, MDIFW/DEP/DMR ([PDF](https://cybrary.friendsofmerrymeetingbay.org/pages/KirchiesAlewives%20LakeGeorge04.pdf))
- **Damariscotta 1980** [FT]: the commercial harvest ran 34 days, 4 May–6 June. — [Libby 1982, *Fish. Bull.* 80:902–905](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/fish-bull/libby%20(1).pdf)
- **St. Croix at Milltown, the next drainage east, 2023** [FT] ([SCIWC 2023 report](https://www.stcroix.org/_files/ugd/309339_e41787bf6f30419789c634dc464aa677.pdf)):
  - 17 fish on 20 April, then none until the run began on 3 May; last fish 30 June; total 841,357 (96 of 100 sampled were alewife);
  - the historic peak week is May 17–June 6, most often May 24–30; in 2023 it was May 31–June 6;
  - the peak day was 2 June (64,302 fish) at a fishway temperature of 22.5 °C; by 5 June it was 14.2 °C and 1,482 fish.
- **Temperature cues** (Greene et al. 2009, ASMFC Habitat Management Series 9, ch. 4 [FT review]; [PDF](https://www.cybrary.friendsofmerrymeetingbay.org/pages/hms9_diadro_habitat_2009_5.pdf)):
  - minimum spawning temperature 10.5 °C (Cianci 1965; Loesch & Lund 1977) [SEC];
  - upstream migration stopped at 21 °C in a Rhode Island stream (Cooper 1961) [SEC];
  - spawning stops entirely at 27.8 °C (Edsall 1970) [SEC];
  - migrants respond to temperature *changes* relative to a baseline, not to absolute values (Richkus 1974) [SEC].
- **Penobscot at Milford:** the run was slow at 48 °F (8.9 °C) in 2019, against 56 °F a year earlier (Valliere, DMR, in *Bangor Daily News*, 18 May 2019 [FT]). A warm-up from 49 to 64 °F (9.4 to 17.8 °C) brought about 500,000 fish in a day [SEC].
- **Diel timing:**
  - upstream movement peaks dawn–noon and dusk–midnight; midday on overcast days, and at night when the water is unusually warm (Richkus 1974; Rideout 1974; Jones et al. 1978, via Greene 2009) [SEC];
  - males arrive first (Fay et al. 1983 [FT review]; [FWS/OBS-82/11.9 PDF](https://njscuba.net/wp-content/uploads/biology/pdf/usfws_alewife_blueback_herring_0116.pdf)).
- **Duration:** in 20 Massachusetts lakes, migration lasted 29–66 days and spawning 43–76 days. Spawning began 7–28 days after the first migrants and continued 13–48 days after migration ended. — Rosset et al. 2017, *TAFS* 146:1101, doi:10.1080/00028487.2017.1341851 [ABS]

**Size, age and sex**
- **Damariscotta 1980** (Libby 1982) [FT]:
  - daily mean TL: males 292.7–305.2 mm, females 302.0–316.6 mm;
  - sex ratio 1:1 (365 males, 346 females);
  - ages 3–9, with ages 4–6 over 95%; in 1980, age 5 was 61% of both sexes;
  - length fell through the run (pooled slope −0.36 mm/day): the earliest fish are the largest and oldest.
- **Walton 1979 (Damariscotta), via Fay 1983** [SEC]:
  - mean weight 153 g (males) / 164 g (females) at age 3, rising to 325 / 356 g at age 7; one 8-year-old female weighed 455 g;
  - length at age 3–7(8): males 260, 278, 301, 315, 318 mm; females 273, 283, 309, 324, 333, 356 mm.
- **Maine summary** (Kircheis 2004) [FT]: spawners average 279–305 mm and about 230 g. Most are first-time spawners at 4–5, and repeat spawners reach 25%. DMR assumes 0.5 lb (227 g) per adult (SFMP 2024 [FT]).
- **ASMFC 2024, northern New England stock region**, von Bertalanffy growth on otolith ages, TL [FT] (River Herring Benchmark Stock Assessment, accepted 7 Aug 2024, asmfc.org):
  - females L∞ 300 mm, K 0.66, t₀ −0.39;
  - males L∞ 287 mm, K 0.70, t₀ −0.38;
  - mean Z 1.1/yr; none mature before age 3, all mature by age 6.
- **St. Croix 2023 sample:** mean FL 256.8 mm, mean weight 225.6 g, 42 males and 54 females [FT, a garbled table read by a research pass].
- **Sex ratio:** Bride Lake was 55.6% male (Kissil 1974). Percent male falls with age: 72, 64, 50, 34 and 0% at ages 4–8 (Marcy 1969). Both via Fay 1983 [SEC].
- **Length–weight, St. John River, New Brunswick** (Messieh 1977, via Fay 1983 [SEC]); FL in mm, W in g:
  - males: log W = 3.235 log FL − 5.420;
  - females: log W = 3.192 log FL − 5.294.

**Fecundity and eggs**
- **Maine:** 60,000–100,000 eggs per female, depending on size (Kircheis 2004 [FT]; Willis 2006 [FT]).
- **Other estimates** are higher:
  - Lamprey River, NH: 147,400 eggs at 24 cm to 332,500 at 34 cm (Sullivan 2017, UNH MS) [ABS];
  - a Connecticut model fit: F = 1,321 × mass (g) + 31,628, which gives ≈ 335,000 for a 230-g female (Post, CT Institute of Water Resources report 2004CT38B) [FT].
- **The often-quoted "60,000–467,000" was not verified.** It may come from Mullen et al. 1986, whose PDF returned 403.
- **Eggs:**
  - 0.80–1.27 mm (Mansueti 1962; Norden 1967, via Fay [SEC]); about 1.3 mm (Kircheis [FT]);
  - demersal and adhesive until water-hardened (under 24 h), then non-adhesive and free to drift (Fay [FT]);
  - they settle in 0.6–3 m of water (DMR web snippet [SEC]).
- **Batch spawning:** suggested (Kissil 1974 [SEC]). Blueback herring retain 10–30% of their eggs after spawning (Loesch & Lund 1977, via Fay [SEC]).

**Spawning sites in lakes**
- **In the lake, not the stream:** Maine runs spawn in the lakes. DMR's plan says outright "no spawning in the stream" for Webber Pond and Franklin, and none below the Damariscotta or Union fishways [FT].
- **Small streams are passable:** alewives use "streams only a few meters wide and a few centimeters deep" as well as ponds (Mansueti 1956, via Fay [FT review]). The 150-m connecting brook is no obstacle at normal flow.
- **Site and behaviour** (via Greene 2009 [SEC]):
  - water 15 cm to 3 m deep, typically under 1 m (Murdy et al. 1997);
  - substrate of sand, gravel, stone, submerged plants or detritus;
  - most spawning at night (Graham 1956);
  - one female with up to 25 males;
  - each wave spawns 2–3 days, older fish first;
  - by day, non-spawning adults sit in deeper water (Richkus 1974).
- **Belding 1921** [FT]: spawners circle pond edges over sandy shoals, by day and night.
- **Downeast lakes, 25 June–22 July 2005:** adults were netted at night in under 3 m of water, mostly in the cove nearest the outlet. — [Willis 2006, *St. Croix River Alewife–Smallmouth Bass Interaction Study*, Maine Rivers](https://www.cybrary.friendsofmerrymeetingbay.org/Alewife/MaineRiversStCroixReportFinal.pdf) [FT]
- **Total spawning time** for one migratory group is usually 5 days or less (Cooper 1961; Loesch & Lund 1977, via Fay [FT review]).

**Residence time, emigration and freshwater mortality of adults**
- **Lake George, Maine** (134 ha; about 2,000 adults released near the end of May each year) [FT]:
  - adult emigration "commenced in early June, peaked by the end of June or early July, and lasted until late fall (1991 and 1992) or winter (1993)";
  - 1991: 1,111 left June 2–July 6 (peak June 24), then 247 in August and 23 in September–October;
  - 1992: 737 left June 1–July 14 (peak June 22);
  - 1993, a low-outflow year: 306 left June 7–July 7 and 39 more by December 7;
  - weir totals were 1,382 of 2,030 (68%), 858 of 2,005 (43%) and 345 (17%).
- **Length of stay:**
  - Bride Lake: 3–82 days on the spawning grounds (Kissil 1974) [SEC];
  - Pentucket Pond, MA: 1–64 days, with longer stays going with earlier arrival and more offspring — Marjadi et al. 2023, *CJFAS*, doi:10.1139/cjfas-2022-0086 [ABS];
  - Rhode Island: most left within 5 days of spawning (Cooper 1961) [SEC].
- **Freshwater adult mortality** (via Fay 1983 [FT review]):
  - **Long Pond, Maine:** mean 41% (32–67%) over 6 years (Havey 1961) [SEC];
  - **Love Lake, Washington County, Maine:** mean 90.7% (66–100%) over 11 years (Havey 1973) [SEC];
  - **Bride Lake, CT:** 57.4% (1966) and 48.6% (1967) (Kissil 1974) [SEC].
- **Total annual mortality at Long Pond, Maine:** 78.6% from age 5 to 6 and 74.4% from age 6 to 7 (Havey 1961) [SEC].

**Adult feeding in the lake**
- **Lake George, Maine** (Kircheis 2004) [FT]:
  - 62%, 65% and 70% of adult stomachs held food (1991–93);
  - main items were Cladocera and Diptera, plus mayflies, copepods, caddis, beetles, odonates, molluscs and fish, mainly **larval yellow perch**;
  - perch were 35% of items in May 1992, and in May 1991 nine of 17 adults held 101 fish between them.
- **Downeast lakes** (Willis 2006) [FT]:
  - adults ate mostly zooplankton (Bosminidae, Daphnidae, Sididae, Holopedidae); in Gardner Lake, mostly mayfly larvae;
  - fish were under 0.15% of diet by importance index, and 7 of 70 fish held larval smelt or alewife.
- **Feeding pattern:** alewives feed in schools mostly by day; at night schools disperse and feeding is negligible or by filtering (Fay [FT review]).

**Adult mortality during the run**
- **Birds:**
  - Nequasset, 2024: up to 5 ospreys, 3 bald eagles and about 40 gulls a day, plus herons and cormorants; largemouth bass waited below the dam [FT, kennebecestuary.org daily log].
  - St. Croix, Grand Falls: 20–40 bald eagles a day in the 1980s–early 1990s; nesting eagles rely on the runs while raising nestlings — IJC St. Croix Board discussion paper, Oct 2005 [FT].
- **Low oxygen:** 2–3 mg/L for 16 h killed 33% of adults (Dorfman & Westman 1970, via Greene 2009) [SEC].
- **No quantitative data on in-lake predation of adults** (260–320 mm) by bass or pickerel. Snapping turtles ate netted adults (Willis 2006) [FT].

### Inferences
- **Arrival at Hot Brook.** The coast runs from late April to early June, with the inland St. Croix peak in late May. Hot Brook is about 209 km above tidewater (the Danforth dam distance, ASF 2021). The sim calendar reaches 10 °C on ≈ May 12 and 15 °C on ≈ May 26. I expect **first fish in mid/late May, a peak in late May–mid June, and the last arrivals by about 20 June**, when the lakes pass about 20–21 °C. This is inference: no Penobscot travel-time data exist above Milford.
- **The route.** Adults enter Lower from Hot Brook (≈ 3 km of outlet stream). Fish bound for Upper must cross Lower and climb the 150-m connecting brook. Spent fish and juveniles from Upper must pass back through Lower.
- **Where they spawn.** Both lakes are shallow, with a mean of 2.4–3.3 m and broad shallow shelves, so nearly the whole margin is potential spawning ground. Spawning should be at night, along the shore, over sand, gravel, plants and detritus, with drifting eggs.
- **Heat.** Adults still in the lake after mid/late July face the 23.5–24 °C limit (§5). Lake George adults lingered into autumn only in small numbers.

## 2. Eggs, larvae and juveniles in the lakes

### Cited findings

**Incubation and early life**
- **Edsall 1970**, *TAFS* 99:376–380, doi:10.1577/1548-8659(1970)99<376:TEOTOT>2.0.CO;2 [ABS] ([USGS record](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/effect-temperature-rate-development-and-survival-alewife-eggs-and-larvae)):
  - eggs hatched between 6.9 and 29.4 °C, with the best hatch (38%) near 17.8 °C;
  - incubation took 15 d at 7.2 °C, 3.7 d at 21.1 °C and 2.1 d at 28.9 °C;
  - unfed larvae survived 3.8 d at 10.5 °C, 7.6 d at 14.5–15 °C and 2.4 d at 26.5–28 °C;
  - no functional jaw formed at 10 °C or below (via Fay [FT]).
- **More incubation points** (via Fay [FT]): 178 h at 12.7 °C and 72 h at 23.8 °C (Kellogg 1982); 6 d at 15.5 °C and 3 d at 22 °C (Kircheis [FT]).
- **Egg survival:** mortality over the first 36 h ran from 22% (3.5–6 °C) to 66% (25.5–28.5 °C), and the egg upper lethal is 29.7 °C (Kellogg 1982), via Fay [FT].
- **Equation misprint (Derived).** The equation printed in Fay and ASMFC, "T = 6.335×10⁶ × t", has lost its exponent and does not reproduce Edsall's points. A research-pass fit to the 7 Edsall/Kellogg points gives **days ≈ 25.1·e^(−0.089·T °C)**:

  | °C | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 | 20 | 22 | 24 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | days | 10.3 | 8.6 | 7.2 | 6.0 | 5.1 | 4.2 | 3.5 | 3.0 |

- **Larvae and transformation** (Fay [FT]):
  - yolk-sac larvae are 2.5–5.0 mm TL at hatch, absorb the yolk in 2–5 d (mean 5.1 mm), and get a functional mouth at about 6 mm;
  - larvae are 4.3–19.9 mm SL; transformation is complete at about 20 mm TL; scales are complete by 45 mm.
- **Lab growth and survival:** first feeding 2 d after hatch at 20 °C; larvae grew 0.62 mm/d to 35.5 mm at 50 d, with instantaneous mortality 0.018/d and half of all deaths in the first 13 d. — Heinrich 1981, *Prog. Fish-Cult.* 43:3–7 [ABS]
- **Oxygen and pH** (Greene 2009 [FT review]):
  - eggs and larvae need at least 5.0 mg/L O₂ (Jones et al. 1988) [SEC];
  - pH 5.0–8.5 is optimal, but pH 5.5–6.2 acid pulses can kill more than 50% (Klauda 1987, 1991) [SEC].

**Habitat and diel behaviour**
- **Larvae** are positively phototropic and occur near spawning areas in shallow (under 2 m), sandy, warm water (O'Neill 1980, via Fay [FT]).
- **Juvenile diel pattern:**
  - toward the bottom by day and the surface at night (Loesch et al. 1982a, via Fay [FT]);
  - at night in Connecticut lakes, "alewife move up in the water column and spread out across the lake" ([CT DEEP SWG T-15-R-1 final report 2018](https://www.northeastwildlifediversity.org/sites/default/files/2023-09/t15r1-alewife-final-report-2018.pdf) [FT]);
  - at higher densities, anadromous YOY move inshore, and diets range from almost wholly pelagic to almost wholly littoral (Schielke 2011; Jones 2013, in CT DEEP 2018 [FT]);
  - in Maine they were seined by day in the shallow littoral or at the surface (Maine DMR 1992 KHDG report [FT], [PDF](https://lldc.mainelegislature.org/Open/Rpts/sh222_m2s26_1992.pdf); Kircheis [FT]).
- **Dark water:** across 29 northeastern lakes, adult density explained 66.6% of juvenile density. Juvenile density peaked near 1,000 adults/ha and fell above it, and it **fell with DOC**. — Devine et al. 2021, *TAFS* 150:207, doi:10.1002/tafs.10282 [ABS]

**Growth**
- **Bride Lake, CT, 2006** (29 ha; over 50,000 adults a year) — Gahagan, Gherard & Schultz 2010, *TAFS* 139:1069–1082, doi:10.1577/T09-128.1 [FT per a research pass; the [cybrary copy](https://cybrary.friendsofmerrymeetingbay.org/pages/Alewife%20Migration%20Factors.pdf) I read holds only the abstract and pp. 1069–1071]:
  - TL = 22.1 + 0.294 × age (d) (r² = 0.72);
  - non-migrants averaged ≈ 30 mm in mid-June, 38 mm in late July and ≈ 51 mm by 1 September;
  - migrants were 28–75 mm: on average 1–12 mm longer, 2–14 d older, 11% longer at age and 14% heavier at length than non-migrants.
- **Eleven lakes:** monthly growth was 0.56–1.41 mm/d, falling with juvenile density and rising with zooplankton biomass. — Devine et al. 2024, *Env. Biol. Fish.* 107:755, doi:10.1007/s10641-024-01565-8 [ABS]
- **Maine ponds 2008:** emigrants were 74.5 mm on 15 August (≈ 73 d old) to 105–122 mm in late September, about 1.0–1.2 mm/d (Monteiro Pierce et al. 2020, *CJFAS*) [FT, the table pairing is uncertain].
- **Damariscotta Lake** (oligotrophic) "produces small juvenile river herring", emigrating from early July "at total lengths as small as 42 mm". Escapement above 40–60/acre lowered yield per spawner (Walton 1987, *AFS Symp.* 1:451). — Maine DMR SFMP 2024 [FT]
- **Maine emigrants** are 32–152 mm, "depending on the availability of feed… the total numbers of young produced… and the length of time the fish remain" (Kircheis [FT]). Rhode Island emigrants were 30–105 mm TL (Richkus 1975, via Fay [SEC]).
- **Drought:** in Bride Lake's 2022 drought the seasonal length increase was about 50% smaller and condition fell. — Burgess et al. 2026, *Front. Mar. Sci.* 13:1721762 [SEC, via a fetch summary]

**Diet**
- **Lake George, Maine** (Kircheis [FT]):
  - YOY selected Cladocera most strongly;
  - 1992: copepods were 95% of items in June, then Cladocera 52, 81 and 77% in July–September; 1993: Cladocera 85–100%; Diptera are a minor share.
- **Post, Palkovacs, Schielke & Dodson 2008**, *Ecology* 89:2019–2032, doi:10.1890/07-1216.1 [FT]:
  - YOY are gape-limited until about June; once gape exceeds 1.0 mm (mid-June) they take large zooplankton;
  - anadromous YOY have wider gapes and gill-raker spacing, and "seldom miss" the largest prey;
  - *Mesocyclops edax* persists through summer as a staple prey.
- **Feeding modes:** particulate (size-selective), filter (non-selective, used more at night) and gulping (Janssen 1976, via Fay [SEC]). Feeding peaks 1–3 h before sunset, with a smaller peak about 2 h after sunrise (Weaver 1975, via Greene [SEC]).
- **Daily ration** has been measured only for YOY **blueback** herring: 438 g-cal/fish/d in July–August and 215 in October–November (Burbidge 1974, via Fay [FT]).

**Densities and production**
- **Bride Lake 1966** (Kissil 1974, via Fay [FT review]):
  - 184,151 adults spawned 2.05×10¹⁰ eggs, and 257,000 juveniles emigrated;
  - egg-to-emigrant mortality was 99.9987%: 2.88 emigrants per female, or 1.4 per adult;
  - Derived: ≈ 6,350 adults/ha and ≈ 8,900 emigrants/ha.
- **Bride Lake 2006:** at least 320,000 emigrants from mid-June to mid-August from 129,114 adults (Gahagan 2010 [FT]). Derived: at least 2.5 per adult and at least 11,000/ha, before the autumn emigrants. The lake's juveniles "decrease by two orders of magnitude between June and August" (Post, personal communication, in Gahagan [FT]).
- **Love Lake, Washington County, Maine** (≈ 672 ac, 272 ha, a near-twin of Lower in area) — Havey 1973, *TAFS* 102:434, via Fay [SEC]:
  - over 11 years, emigrants ranged from **220 to 439,062 a year**, or **12–3,209 per female**, or 0.09–21.5 kg of emigrant biomass per female;
  - emigrants correlated with escapement (log–log) and with the adult run 4 years later.
  - Derived: up to ≈ 1,600 emigrants/ha; the biomass per emigrant implies mean emigrants of ≈ 7 g (≈ 90–95 mm).
- **Survival summaries:** nursery survival is "between 0.0001% (Kissil 1974) and 1.0% (Havey 1973)" (Kissil's own figures give 0.0013%). Typically 1.0–1.5 surviving juveniles per adult (Cooper 1961; Kissil 1974). Both via Gahagan 2010 [FT].
- **Inshore night density, August 2017:** 0.75 fish/m² (Bride), 0.28 (Dodge Pond) and 0.10 (Rogers Lake) (CT DEEP 2018 [FT]). Derived: 1,000–7,500/ha inshore.
- **Mesocosms:** 4.8 YOY/m² cropped cladocerans to 0.24 mm by late August (Post, CTIWR 2004CT38B [FT]).
- **Not found:** hydroacoustic or per-m³ juvenile densities from any Maine lake.

**Emigration**
- **Timing in Maine:**
  - mid-July through early December (Kircheis [FT]);
  - DMR's web page: mostly late August–September after 2–6 months in fresh water, at 1.5–5 in [SEC];
  - Sebasticook 1992: juveniles passed Benton Falls 28 July–25 September, and alosids used a bypass in October–November (DMR 1992 [FT]).
- **Pulses:**
  - Bride Lake 2006: more than 80% left in **3 pulses of 1–2 d**, tied to the previous day's rain, brief temperature drops and brief discharge rises. Migration came around dawn early in the season and at night later (Gahagan 2010 [FT]).
  - Richkus 1975: waves lasted 2–3 d, with 60–80% of fish leaving on about 8% of available days, regardless of the size of the trigger. Very high densities can trigger early summer emigration of small fish (via Greene [FT review]).
  - Dark-moon nights are cues (Stokesbury & Dadswell 1989) [SEC]; migration peaked at midday and at the new moon (Yako et al. 2002, *Ecol. Appl.* 12:521) [SEC].
  - Large juveniles go first (Schmidt et al. 1988) [SEC].
- **Low flow holds them:**
  - Bride Brook dried 14–29 August 2006 (Gahagan [FT]); in 2022 the lake was disconnected 7 July–7 December, and fish massed at the outlet in October–November (Burgess 2026 [SEC]).
  - Maine: Little Pond, Damariscotta and Lovejoy Pond had poor or no passage at low flow (Kircheis; DMR 1992 [FT]).
  - In dry 1993, Lake George's alewives "did not emigrate until late winter" (Kircheis [FT]; it is unclear whether this means adults or young).
- **Overwintering:**
  - "Anadromous alewives do not appear to be capable of surviving overwinter in their natal lakes" (McCormick 1997, via Gahagan [FT]);
  - juveniles cooled from 15.6 to 2.8 °C over 15 d suffered over 90% mortality (Colby 1973), and 7 °C for 96 h killed 27–60% (PSE&G 1984);
  - no juvenile survives 3 °C or below (Otto et al. 1976); all via Greene [FT review].
  - Exceptions are estuarine (Chesapeake, Delaware) or hybrid anadromous × landlocked fish (CT DEEP 2018).

**Juvenile predators** (via Greene [FT review]; DMR 1992 [FT]):
- **Fish:** eel, white and yellow perch, pickerel, bass and pumpkinseed.
- **Others:** turtles, snakes, mink and birds.
- **Eggs:** eaten by yellow and white perch and by alewives.
- **At Maine dams in September–October:** gulls, cormorants, bald eagles and herons feed on the emigrants.
- **Eels:** "gorge on juvenile alewife leaving lakes in the fall" (Maine DMR alewife page, quoted in `../Fish in streams connecting lakes/bass_pickerel_in_connecting_streams.md`).

### Inferences
- **The young as a pelagic pulse.** In shallow, unstratified Hot Brook there is no hypolimnion to hide in. YOY should school in open water by day, nearer the bottom, and spread up and inshore at night.
- **Fewer young per adult.** The tea colour (DOC) and soft water argue for fewer young per adult than in clear coastal lakes (Devine 2021). The oligotrophic Damariscotta case argues for small emigrants (about 40–70 mm) leaving early.
- **Stragglers die.** With ice-on about 1 December and 5 °C reached about 10 November, every juvenile still in the lake by mid/late November should die there.

## 3. Ecological effects in the lakes

### Cited findings

**Zooplankton**
- **Post et al. 2008** [FT], 19 Connecticut lakes:
  - in anadromous lakes, large cladoceran biomass was high in April–May and fell sharply from late May to mid-June, and **no large cladocerans were found from the end of June to the end of October 2005**;
  - copepod length fell about 2 weeks after cladoceran length;
  - large zooplankton have a refuge from about December to early June;
  - by summer, total crustacean biomass was lower even than in landlocked-alewife lakes;
  - alewife phenotype mattered more than alewife biomass.
- **Maine DEP 2011, Webber Pond report** (Halliwell) [FT] ([Gulf of Maine KB file 9954](https://ww3.gulfofmaine.org/kb/files/9954/)). Mean July–September cladoceran length (mm), 2008 / 2009 / 2010:

  | Lake | 2008 | 2009 | 2010 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | China Lake (no alewives) | 0.59 | 0.68 | 0.63 |
  | Sebasticook | 0.52 | 0.72 | 0.35 |
  | Webber | 0.66 | – | 0.49 |
  | Threemile | 0.56 | – | 0.51 |

  - Sebasticook *Daphnia* shrank each month and rebounded slightly in September after the young left.
- **Lake George, Maine, at 6 adults/acre** (Kircheis [FT]):
  - Cladocera density fell and rotifers rose;
  - *Bosmina coregoni* went from 0.43 to 0.36 mm, and Calanoida from 0.86 to 0.94 mm;
  - the late-summer Cladocera peak was delayed to autumn;
  - *Daphnia catawba* and *D. dubia* recovered within 2–3 years after stocking stopped.
- **8 Maine lakes:** YOY cut cladoceran and copepod mean length from spring to summer, with weaker effects in more eutrophic lakes and no consistent cascade to phytoplankton. — Demi et al. 2015, *Hydrobiologia* 758:47–59 [SEC]

**Water clarity and algae**
- **Lake George** (Kircheis [FT]):
  - Secchi (5.2–6.6 m) and chlorophyll (2.7–4.5 µg/L) did not differ during alewife years;
  - summer epilimnetic TP was lower during alewives (7 vs 8 before and 9 after, µg/L);
  - O₂ was unaffected.
- **Connecticut:** anadromous lakes had higher summer chlorophyll per unit of spring TP than no-alewife lakes (P = 0.02), and more edible (under 30 µm) chlorophyll (Post 2008 [FT]).
- **Maine DEP 2011** [FT]: phytoplankton composition did not differ from China Lake. DEP's Barry Mower is quoted: "no change or a minor net decrease" in lake TP [SEC].

**Nutrients**
- **Walters, Barnes & Post 2009**, *CJFAS* 66:439–448, doi:10.1139/F09-008 [FT]:
  - adults are 2.49% N and 0.42% P of wet mass (from Durbin et al. 1979);
  - each fish excretes 0.00034 g P and 0.00395 g N per hour;
  - Bride Brook received 92–177 g P a year from runs of 67,000–129,000, about half by excretion and half by carcasses.
- **Excretion rates:** 2.17 µg P and 24.71 µg N per g wet mass per hour. — Post & Walters 2009, *TAFS* 138:264–268 [FT]
- **Model parameters** (Post, CTIWR [FT]): YOY are 0.43% P of wet mass; in-lake adult mortality 50–60%; adults stay about 2–4 weeks.
- **West, Walters, Gephard & Post 2010**, *CJFAS* 67:1211–1220 [ABS/SEC]:
  - a fish that dies adds 0.68 g P; a survivor adds 0.18 g P, two-thirds of it by excretion;
  - alewives supply 23% of Bride Lake's annual P load;
  - newly restored, small runs may export more P than they import.
- **Pausacaco Pond:** 0.43 g P, 2.7 g N and 16.8 g C per m² over 2 months, mostly from adult deaths, far exceeding juvenile export. — Durbin, Nixon & Oviatt 1979, *Ecology* 60:8–17 [ABS]
- **Net direction:** export at low escapement (high recruits per spawner), import at high escapement. — Barber, Gibson, O'Malley & Zydlewski 2018, *Mar. Coast. Fish.* 10:236–254 [ABS]
- **Alewife share of epilimnetic TP** (Thurrell 2022, USM MS thesis [ABS]):
  - under 5% in China, Highland and Togus;
  - Webber 8.7–23.9%; Carr Pond 11.4–93.7%.

**Alewives as prey**
- **Willis 2006**, Downeast/St. Croix lakes [FT]:
  - YOY smallmouth length and condition did not differ with or without alewives (YOY bass 37–106 mm in early fall);
  - adult bass growth at ages 1–5 was equal or higher in alewife years, significantly higher for at least one age interval in each of three lakes;
  - tournament weights did not differ (13 lakes).
- **Maine white perch:** in Biscay and North Ponds, adult white perch fed almost exclusively on juvenile alewives by late summer; Lake George white perch (no alewives) ate mainly Cladocera. — Moring & Mink 2002, *Hydrobiologia* 479:125–130 [ABS]
- **Largemouth bass:** herring were the most important fish prey by number for largemouth under 300 mm after mid-August, and bass that ate more herring grew better. — Yako, Mather & Juanes 2000, *TAFS* 129:77–88 [SEC]
- **New Brunswick:**
  - in a natural lake, small alewives were present all summer, made up a significant share of YOY bass diet, and the bass grew larger;
  - in a reservoir with a truncated run, alewives were bass-sized and competed with them.
  - Hanson & Curry 2005, *TAFS* 134:356–368, doi:10.1577/T03-067.1 [ABS]
- **Chain pickerel:**
  - at Lake George, pickerel looked heavier in alewife years (n = 9) (Kircheis [FT]);
  - where alewives are landlocked and year-round, pickerel shifted to open water in habitat, diet and body shape within about 300 years (12 lakes) — Brodersen, Howeth & Post 2015, *Nat. Commun.* 6:8115, doi:10.1038/ncomms9115 [SEC].
- **Birds:** eagles at Grand Falls disappeared after the 1995 fishway closures, and alewives were rarer in eagle nest remains in 2003–04 than in 1976–87 (IJC 2005 [FT]). Bald eagles are a user field observation at Hot Brook.
- **Mussels:** alewives host the alewife floater (*Utterbackiana implicata*); see `maine_freshwater_mussels.md`.

**Competition**
- **Bass:** YOY alewife and YOY smallmouth diet overlap exceeded 60% only in Meddybemps Lake, where Sididae were over 90% of both diets (Willis 2006 [FT]).
- **Lake George** (Kircheis [FT]):
  - no change in the size of smallmouth bass, chain pickerel, white perch, yellow perch, pumpkinseed, redbreast sunfish, brown bullhead or others;
  - adult alewives ate **larval yellow perch** in May.
- **Not found:** any study of YOY yellow perch or white perch growth with versus without sea-run alewives.

### Inferences
- **Zooplankton is the main lever.** Hot Brook's large cladocerans (the sim's `zooD`) should collapse from mid/late June until the young leave, then rebound from December to early June. That squeezes:
  - YOY yellow and white perch, pumpkinseed and fallfish, all midsummer zooplanktivores;
  - YOY bass early in the season.

  It also feeds every piscivore from August to November.
- **Spring perch larvae** take a direct hit from adult alewives in May–June.
- **Clarity** should change little at the run sizes suggested here.

The larval-stage parameters were not retrieved.

**Temperature tolerance** (Greene 2009 [FT review] and Fay 1983 [FT review]; primaries [SEC])
- **Adults:**
  - upper incipient lethal **23.5–24.0 °C** when acclimated to 10, 15 and 20 °C (Otto et al. 1976);
  - 29.8 and 32.8 °C at acclimations of 16.9 and 24.5 °C (Stanley & Holzer 1971);
  - lower incipient lethal 6–8 °C (acclimated 15–21 °C), and none survive below 3 °C.
- **Behaviour in the cold** (Colby 1971, 1973): feeding is disrupted below 6.7 °C and schooling below 4.5 °C.
- **YOY:**
  - upper incipient lethal ≈ 26.5, 30.3 and 32.1 °C when acclimated to 10, 20 and 25 °C;
  - CTM = 21.9 + 0.5 × acclimation temperature (28.3, 32.7 and 34.4 °C at 11, 19 and 25 °C), 3–6 °C above adults (Otto et al. 1976);
  - YOY prefer 26.3 °C (Kellogg 1982).
- **Larvae:** upper limit 31 °C; growth peaks at 29.1 °C; net biomass gain peaks at 26.4 °C (Kellogg 1982).

**Oxygen** (Greene 2009 [FT review])
- **Adults:** 2–3 mg/L for 16 h killed 33%; they survived 0.5 mg/L for 5 min if then given at least 3 mg/L (Dorfman & Westman 1970); recommended minimum 5.0 mg/L (Jones et al. 1988).
- **Juveniles:** minimum 3.6 mg/L (Jones 1988); stressed below 2.0 mg/L; found in the wild at 2.4–10 mg/L (Davis & Cheek 1966).
- **Eggs and larvae:** at least 5.0 mg/L.

### Inferences
- **Which parameters to use.** These are the only published alewife FB4 parameters, and they come from landlocked fish of 1–60 g.
  - **Young of the year:** use them as they stand (YOY temperature set), without the swimming multiplier under about 1 g.
  - **Sea-run adults (150–350 g):** they extrapolate the weight terms, but adults are in the lake only for weeks, so a coarse adult budget is acceptable. Cmax at 230 g ≈ 0.166 g·g⁻¹·d⁻¹ at 16–18 °C (Derived).
- **Heat in Hot Brook.** The lakes' July peak of 24 °C (warmer at the surface by day) is above the adult incipient lethal but well inside the YOY range. Adults must leave by mid-July; the young are comfortable.
- **Oxygen in Hot Brook.** The lakes are unstratified and well oxygenated in summer, so O₂ should rarely bind for alewives.
- **Acidity.** At pH 6.6–7.0 and alkalinity 5.5–8.4, the acid-pulse risk to eggs is low.

## Four seasons of alewives in the Hot Brook lakes

All dates are on the sim's calendar (`SEASON.TEMP_KNOTS`): 4 °C ≈ Apr 24, 10 °C ≈ May 12, 15 °C ≈ May 26, 20.5 °C ≈ Jun 18, 24 °C ≈ Jul 30, 16 °C ≈ Sep 25, 10 °C ≈ Oct 16, 5 °C ≈ Nov 10, ice-on ≈ Dec 1. Which statements are cited and which are inference is set out in §§1–5.

**Winter (December–April, ice).**
- No alewives: adults are at sea, and any young left at ice-on have died.
- This is the zooplankton's refuge. Large *Daphnia* and other cladocerans rebuild under and after the ice, and copepods dominate under ice. Large cladocerans reach their spring peak in April–May.

**Spring (ice-out ≈ Apr 23 → mid-June).**
- **Arrival.** The Penobscot run passes Milford in May. From about mid/late May, adults (260–320 mm, ≈ 230 g, males first and the biggest, oldest fish first) come up Hot Brook into Lower in daytime pulses on warming days. Some climb the connecting brook to Upper.
- **Eagles and ospreys** gather at the brook mouths and the outlet.
- **Spawning.** At 10.5–21 °C (best 13–16 °C, i.e. mid-May to mid-June here), groups of one female and many males spawn at night along the shallows over sand, gravel, plant beds and detritus.
- **Eggs** (60,000–100,000+ per female) stick briefly, then drift, and hatch in 6–10 days at these temperatures.
- **The adults' feeding.** Adults feed by day on cladocerans, midges and **yellow perch larvae**.
- **Leaving.** Spent adults begin leaving in early June. Many die in the lake (40–90%) and their carcasses fertilise the shallows.

**Summer (mid-June → August).**
- **Adults.** Departures peak in late June. By mid-July the lakes pass about 23.5 °C and any remaining adults die or leave.
- **Larvae (4–20 mm)** reach the 1-mm gape by mid-June and are juveniles (about 30 mm) by early July. Schools crop the large cladocerans, which collapse by late June. The young switch to copepods, *Bosmina*, nauplii and chironomids.
- **Daily rhythm.** By day the schools sit offshore and lower in the water column; at night they spread up and inshore.
- **Growth.** They grow 0.3–1.4 mm/day, slower when crowded or when the brook is dry. Numbers fall roughly a hundredfold from June to August.
- **First departures.** After mid-July rains, the first emigration pulses go out Lower's outlet, and Upper's young go down the brook. Each pulse is 1–2 days, at dawn early in the season.
- **Predators switch over.** From mid-August, bass under 300 mm, pickerel, white perch and yellow perch turn to the young alewives. Outlet and brook-mouth pools become ambush points.

**Autumn (September → ice-on).**
- **The main emigration.** It runs through September–October in rain- and flow-triggered pulses, now at night, at about 40–110 mm. The largest and fattest go first.
- **Predation.** Eels and birds take emigrants at the outlets.
- **Low outflow** (a dry autumn) holds the young back. They grow poorly and mass near the outlet.
- **Cold.** As the lakes cool past 10 °C (mid-October) and 5 °C (about 10 November), stragglers stop feeding and schooling. Those left at ice-on die.
- **Zooplankton.** It rebounds from late autumn, with the cladoceran peak delayed to autumn.
- **Phosphorus.** The net balance for the year is the P left by dead adults and adult excretion, minus the P carried out in emigrants.

**4. Eggs and larvae**
- Fecundity default **100,000 eggs per female** (Maine 60,000–100,000; size-based fits give up to about 335,000). Calibrate survival to emigrant numbers, not to eggs.
- Eggs are demersal and drift; incubation days = 25.1·e^(−0.089·T).
- Hatch at 3–5 mm, yolk sac 2–5 d, first feeding at about 6 mm, juvenile at 20 mm. Larval mortality is about 0.02/d in the lab.

**5. Young of the year**
- **Growth:** TL ≈ 22 + 0.29 × age in days at high density, up to about 1.0–1.4 mm/d at low density. Let FB4 with the YOY set produce this from food. Damariscotta-like oligotrophy gives small emigrants of 40–70 mm.
- **Length–weight:** ln W = −6.7091 + 4.3157 ln L (cm) below 43 mm; −4.72 + 2.96 ln L above. That gives about 0.14 g at 30 mm, 1.0 g at 50 mm, 2.8 g at 70 mm and 8.1 g at 100 mm.
- **Gape and diet:** no prey over 1 mm until about 25–30 mm (mid-June). Then size-selective particulate feeding on the largest `zooD`, then `zooC` and nauplii, plus chironomids. At night, filter feeding.
- **Daily rhythm:** pelagic, lower in the column by day; up and inshore at night.
- **Density dependence:** growth falls with YOY density and rises with zooplankton biomass (Devine 2024). Calibration targets:
  - summer night inshore density of about 0.1–0.75/m²;
  - **emigrants of about 2–50 per adult** (Bride 1.4–2.5 at extreme density; Love Lake 6–1,600 per adult);
  - emigrant density saturating at about 1,000–10,000/ha;
  - a roughly hundredfold decline from June to August.
- **Emigration:**
  - **Window:** mid-July to ice-on.
  - **Base rate:** a low daily probability (rising with fish length and condition, and through autumn), multiplied severalfold on a pulse day: the day after rain or a discharge rise, a temperature drop of 2 °C or more, or a dark moon. This should put more than 80% of leavers in 2–3 pulses of 1–2 days.
  - **Time of day:** dawn in July, night from August onward.
  - **Blocked** when outlet flow is near zero.
  - **Cold death:** below 5 °C a high daily mortality; everything left at ice-on dies.
- **Thermal and oxygen limits:**
  - YOY CTO 24, CTM 26 and CTL 29 °C for consumption;
  - upper lethal about 30–32 °C, never reached here;
  - O₂ stress below 3.6 mg/L.
- **Predators of the young:**
  - smallmouth bass (strongly from mid-August, bass under 300 mm), chain pickerel, white perch (late summer; can go almost entirely to alewives), yellow perch, pumpkinseed, eel (at the outlets in autumn) and birds (gulls, cormorants, herons, eagles, loon);
  - outlet and brook-mouth pools should draw these predators during emigration pulses (see `../Fish in streams connecting lakes/bass_pickerel_in_connecting_streams.md` §3).

**6. Zooplankton coupling: checks to run**
- With alewives on, `zooD` biomass and mean size should crash from late June to October. Summer mean cladoceran length should be about 0.35–0.5 mm against about 0.6–0.7 mm with no alewives (Maine DEP).
- `zooR` should rise, and `zooD` should recover from December to May.
- At 6/acre the effect should still show (Lake George).

**7. Nutrients (mass-conserving)**
- **Content:** each adult imports 0.42% P and 2.49% N of its wet mass (0.97 g P and 5.7 g N in a 230-g fish).
- **Excretion:** 2.17 µg P and 24.7 µg N per g per hour while resident, about 70–80% of the N as NH₄.
- **Carcasses:** route their P and N to `sedOM` and the water.
- **Export:** emigrants export 0.43% P of their wet mass.
- **Derived scale for Upper:**
  - at 35/acre, net adult import is ≈ 0.54–0.90 g P per adult, ≈ 17–28 kg P a year, against a water-column stock of ≈ 119 kg at 10 µg/L;
  - at 235/acre it is ≈ 110–190 kg;
  - a 3-g emigrant carries ≈ 13 mg P, so break-even is ≈ 40–70 emigrants per adult. The lakes should be net importers at these run sizes.

**8. Bioenergetics block (FB4 format, Stewart & Binkowski 1986)**
- **Adults:**

  `{ CEQ: 3, CA: 0.846, CB: -0.30, CQ: 3, CTO: 16, CTM: 18, CTL: 25, CK1: 0.17, CK4: 0.01, REQ: 1, RA: 0.00367, RB: -0.215, RQ: 0.0548, RTO: 0.03, RTL: 9, RK1: 5.78, RK4: -0.045, RK5: 0.149, ACT: 22.08, BACT: 0, SDA: 0.175, EGEQ: 1, FA: 0.16, EXEQ: 1, UA: 0.10 }`

- **`bioJuv`:** the same, with `CQ: 5, CTO: 24, CTM: 26, CTL: 29`, and RTO 0 (no swimming multiplier) for fish under about 1 g (Klumb 2003).
- **Energy density:** 4,185 J/g for YOY in early summer, rising to about 5,000 J/g by October. For adults use about 5,400 J/g in June, at the spawning low.
- **Gonads:** ♀ 10%, ♂ 5% of mass, shed at spawning.

**9. Other things to watch**
- **Yellow perch.** Alewife effects should be watched on yellow perch: adults eat perch larvae, and YOY compete for `zooD`. This is a possible mechanism behind the user's "perch small and few" observation, though so far untested.
- **White perch** should switch to alewives in late summer.
- **Bass growth** should be equal or better (the Maine checks).
