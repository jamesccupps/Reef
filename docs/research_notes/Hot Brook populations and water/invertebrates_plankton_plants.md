# Invertebrates, plankton and aquatic plants of small, shallow, tea-coloured, mud-bottomed warm-water lakes (Maine and the Northeast): densities and biomass for calibrating the Hot Brook model

Scope: numbers per m² (or per litre), biomass, life history and season for crayfish, freshwater mussels, zoobenthos, zooplankton, phytoplankton/clarity and macrophytes. The target is Upper and Lower Hot Brook Lake (Danforth, Washington Co., Maine: 280–360 ha, 4–5.5 m max, unstratified, rocky shores, muddy basins, ice ~Dec–late Apr). Compiled 2026-10-15.

Conventions and caveats:
- Region is flagged on every number: [ME] Maine, [NH], [CT], [MA], [RI], [NY-Adk] Adirondacks, [ON], [QC], [NB/NS] Atlantic Canada, [MI], [WI], [other].
- DW = dry weight, WW = wet weight. 1 µg DW/L = 1 mg DW/m³. 1 kg/ha = 0.1 g/m².
- Arithmetic labelled "(my arithmetic)" is mine, from the cited numbers.
- Search-result summaries that I could not open in full are marked "(snippet)".
- lakesofmaine.org (which holds the Hot Brook water-quality summaries and plant lists) sits behind a bot-verification wall. It blocked WebFetch, curl and the browser, and I did not try to get past it. Every Hot Brook-specific value below is therefore inferred from other lakes.

## 1. Crayfish: virile crayfish (*Faxonius/Orconectes virilis*) densities, size, life history, winter, diet and predation

### Takeaway
The only whole-lake virile-crayfish census found is from three small Michigan lakes (sand/organic/peat bottoms, 1.4–1.9 ha). It gives whole-lake densities of:
- **0.1–0.4 adult females/m²**;
- **13–14 hatchlings/m²** in June, falling to **1.2–1.5/m²** by September and **0.3–0.4 yearlings/m²** the next August;
- a total biomass of **4.6–21 g/m²** (46–213 kg/ha) with **P/B ≈ 0.9–1.5/yr**.

Local densities on cobble or in weed beds are several-fold higher than these whole-lake averages. Rock beats sand and mud as daytime shelter. Virile crayfish:
- mature in their 2nd summer and live about 3 years (ages II–III breed);
- mate in August–September and carry eggs over winter, which are laid in spring and hatch about June;
- are inactive in deep water in winter;
- are mainly scavenger-omnivores;
- are eaten chiefly as age-0 by fish.

### Cited Findings
**Maine status and habitat**
- [ME] Virile crayfish is the only crayfish found statewide. It is native and "prefers stream and lake habitats with rocky bottoms. Is also found in deep waters." — [Maine Aquatic Biodiversity Project (MABP), Ch. 6, Table 6.6.1](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_6_6.5to6.10.pdf)
- [ME] Of 96 Maine lakes with crayfish data, 83 hold one species and 12 hold two. The commonest pair is rusty + virile. White river crayfish (*Procambarus acutus*, introduced) is restricted to Downeast Maine. Rusty, calico and obscure crayfish are southern/central. — same source
- [ME] In stream surveys virile crayfish made up 94% of crayfish caught (from the earlier note). — [Marquis, Wilson & Gallagher, GSA NE 2009](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2009NE/webprogram/Paper155692.html)

**Densities and biomass (lakes)**

Three Michigan pothole lakes [MI], from [Gowing & Momot 1974, Michigan DNR Fisheries Research Report 1811](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1811.pdf):
- **Lakes:** West Lost (1.40 ha), North Twin (1.90 ha), South Twin (1.60 ha). Mean depths are 7.3–8.5 m. The shoal (<1.2 m) is only 11.0–15.3% of the bottom. Bottoms are sand to 3 m, organic matter to 6 m and pulpy peat deeper. Logs are abundant and the only submerged plant is yellow water-lily.
- **Adult females (spring 1966):** 5,586 in West Lost, 4,342 in North Twin, 3,121 in South Twin. In 1968: 2,008 (West Lost) and 2,227 (North Twin). Per area this is ≈0.40, 0.23, 0.20, 0.14 and 0.12 females/m² (my arithmetic).
- **Hatchlings:**
  - June 1966: 345,773, 111,589 and 101,745 per lake, equal to 16.1, 3.8 and 4.1 kg/ha;
  - June 1968: 132,098 and 136,902 per ha (≈13–14/m²);
  - September 1968: 15,055 and 11,953 per ha (≈1.2–1.5/m²).
- **Yearlings (August 1969):** 3,899 and 3,168 per ha (≈0.3–0.4/m²).
- **Age-0 mortality:** 0.60–0.91 from June to September, then 0.74 from September to the following August.

Other sources:
- [MI] In the three lakes, biomass was 46–213 kg/ha, production 60–142 kg/ha/yr and P/B 0.94–1.53. Mortality, not growth, drove year-to-year differences. — Momot & Gowing 1977, J. Fish. Res. Board Can. 34:2041 (abstract seen via search; also cited in `whole_lake_energy_flow.md`)
- [other, river] In the Yampa River, CO (invasive), virile crayfish were 6.4/m² (2005) and 9.3/m² (2006), at 9.0 and 15.8 g/m². That is 122 kg/ha, equal to all other macroinvertebrates plus fish combined. — [USFWS Ecological Risk Screening Summary, virile crayfish](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf)
- [other] Method bias: in one comparison, quadrat sampling gave about 6.4 crayfish/m² against 16.7/m² by SCUBA mark–recapture (species not named in the snippet). In 31 lakes, 1-m² quadrats were the efficient choice above 0.3 crayfish/m². — [Rosewarne PhD 2013 (UK, review)](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/7807/1/Rosewarne_PJR_Biology_PhD_2013.pdf) (snippet); "Estimating crayfish density in lakes using quadrats" (ResearchGate record, 403)
- [other] A generic statement: "in many lakes crayfish dominate the annual production of the benthic biomass", at up to 1,000–1,400 kg/ha and 15/m². — search snippet, source not identified; treat as an upper bound only.

**Habitat and substrate (Wisconsin lakes, *O. virilis* with *O. rusticus* and *O. propinquus*)**
- [WI] In experimental pools, all three species preferred cobble by day, with muck–macrophyte as their second choice, and spread evenly across habitats at night. When the invaders were present, *O. virilis* shifted out of cobble into muck–macrophyte. — Hill & Lodge 1994 (summary via [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df7ed70f3e94b9e5d93318)) (snippet)
- [WI] Trout Lake plus 21 lakes: *O. virilis* was "restricted to macrophyte" where rusty crayfish held the cobble, and across lakes its abundance was unrelated to cobble and rose with fish abundance. — Ecology 2003 field study (snippet, via the same search)
- [WI] At equal size, *O. virilis* was more vulnerable to fish predation than either invader, and was excluded from shelters when shelter was limiting and fish were present. — Garvey, Stein & Thomas 1994 (snippet, [AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df247c63b8185d9cab1012))
- [ME] MABP habitat note: virile crayfish "prefers … rocky bottoms" and also occurs in deep water. — [MABP Ch. 6](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_6_6.5to6.10.pdf)

**Size, growth, maturity, lifespan**
- [MI] Age-0 carapace length (CL) was about 6 mm when released from the female in early July, about 14 mm in September and about 21 mm by the next June. Berried females were 20–36 mm CL. Spring adult females were ages II and III, with age II dominant (e.g. 5,289 II vs 297 III in West Lost, 1966). — [MI DNR RR1811](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1811.pdf)
- [MI] Mean attached eggs per female were 25.7–149.1 across lakes and years (SE 3–8). — same source, Table 11
- [general] Maximum lifespan is about 3 years. Total length is 23–56 mm in year 1 and 58–84 mm by year 2. Maturity comes in the 2nd summer at about 24 mm (probably CL). Adults are 45–125 mm. — [USFWS ERSS](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf), quoting GISD/ANSIS and Momot 1967

**Moult and reproductive calendar (northern populations)**
- [ON, NW Ontario] Year-II and year-III males moult twice: Form I→II in mid-June and Form II→I in early August. Females moult once, after the young leave them in July. Mating runs August–September. — Weagle & Ozburn 1972, Can. J. Zool. ([doi:10.1139/z72-053](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/z72-053)) (snippet)
- [general] There are about 5 moults in the first summer and 1–2 a year in years 2–3. Form-I moult runs July–September in Michigan and NW Ontario. Females store sperm over winter and lay eggs in spring; eggs hatch 1–2 months later; the young stay attached through two moults. — [PMC10191776, growth across reproductive forms of *F. virilis*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10191776/) (snippet); [USFWS ERSS](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf)
- [MI] Young become available to fish in July, "shortly after being shed by the female". — [MI DNR RR1811](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1811.pdf)
- [MI] A differential seasonal (summer) migration was seen in three marl lakes. Most age-0 start in the immediate shore area and spread as summer progresses. — [MI DNR RR1767 (Momot & Gowing 1970)](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1767.pdf); RR1811

**Winter**
- [general] Breeding "extends until the adults retreat to deep water and become inactive". — [USFWS ERSS](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf) (quoting GISD)
- [IA] It "does not burrow extensively and apparently overwinters buried in stream beds". — [Proc. Iowa Acad. Sci. 76](https://scholarworks.uni.edu/pias/vol76/iss1/62) (snippet)
- [MN, popular source] Migrates to deeper water that does not freeze and becomes inactive. — [minnesotaseasons](https://blog.minnesotaseasons.com/?p=1122) (snippet, uncited)
- [FI, introduced range] Tolerates winter well but is "not very tolerant of dehydration or low oxygen". — [laji.fi](https://laji.fi/en/taxon/MX.254215) (snippet)

**Diet**
- [general] Omnivore: snails, insects, small fish, fish eggs, tadpoles and macrophytes. It is "believed to obtain most of its nutrition by scavenging dead animals". — [USFWS ERSS](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf) (quoting GISD/Collicut 1998)
- [AB] Males reduce the length and growth of some macrophytes; the species alters macrophyte biomass and diversity and littoral structure. — Chambers et al. 1990, Freshwater Biol. 24:81–91 (cited in USFWS ERSS)
- [other] *O. immunis* above 140–150 g/m² significantly cut macrophyte biomass in experiments. — 1991 experiment (snippet via search)

**Predation by fish and otters**
- [MI] Brook trout ate only age-0 crayfish (larger ones were too big). Trout took about 11–16% of the production of a crayfish year class's first 14 months (rainbow 9 of 82 kg; brook 13 of 83 kg). In 1962–63 brook trout took 2.9% of total annual production and 4.2% of age-0 production. Crayfish were 13–71% of trout diet by lake. — [MI DNR RR1811](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1811.pdf)
- [NY-Adk] Smallmouth bass, Little Moose Lake: crayfish were the most frequent prey and the largest share by number. Crayfish per stomach rose with bass length, with a diet shift from mayflies to crayfish and fish as bass approached about 150 mm. — [Weidel et al. 2000, MSU summary](https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/diet-and-prey-selection-naturalized-smallmouth-bass-oligotrophic-adirondack-la) (snippet; the PDF would not load)
- [other, river] Colorado: smallmouth diet was 51.5% crayfish by mass (n = 178). [WI rivers] Crayfish occurred in 24–40% of smallmouth stomachs. — search summary of [Wisconsin smallmouth study](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/fea52bc0-8fe1-4d74-9225-9c73f12b2be4) (snippet)
- [other] River otter scats:
  - crayfish in 57.6% (eastern North Dakota, n = 665) and 82% (Great Dismal Swamp, VA, n = 209);
  - West Virginia (1,104 scats): crayfish were the main prey in summer–fall and fish in winter–spring.
  - No Maine or New England otter diet study was found. — [search summary of otter diet studies](https://wvdnr.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Newsletter-Fall-2025.pdf) (snippet)
- [WI] Largemouth bass diet data (55% crayfish by weight April–October in a Wisconsin flowage) are already used in `src/config.js` CRAYFISH comments.

### Inferences
- **Whole-lake numbers.** A Hot Brook-type lake, with rocky shores and a muddy basin and fish present, would plausibly hold whole-lake **~0.5–1.5 crayfish/m² of age-1+ animals** in summer (adult females 0.1–0.4/m² × 2 for males, plus yearlings 0.3–0.4/m², from the Michigan census). In late summer add **1–1.5/m² of age-0**, and in early July **~10–15/m² of newly released young**. Biomass would be **~5–20 g/m²**. (My arithmetic.)
- **Where they sit.** Crayfish are concentrated on the rocky littoral and in weed beds, so per-m² densities there may run **3–10× the whole-lake average**: the Michigan lakes' shoal was only 11–15% of the bottom. The sim's 9 g/m² lake-wide is inside the measured range.
- **Calendar for a sim** (Maine ice-out ~late April):
  - inactive in deeper water through ice (≤4–5 °C);
  - eggs extruded April–May after ice-out;
  - hatch late May–June;
  - young released late June–early July;
  - female moult July;
  - males to Form I early August;
  - mating August–September (and some in spring);
  - age-0 growth to ~14 mm CL by September;
  - maturity in the 2nd summer;
  - death after the 3rd–4th summer.
- **Predation.** Fish predation falls mostly on age-0 and yearlings. In Michigan, trout cropped only 2–16% of production, and most age-0 mortality (60–90% per summer) was "other" — density-dependent cannibalism and invertebrates. A sim in which fish eat most crayfish production would be off. Shelter (rock) controls exposure.
- **Mud basins.** Virile crayfish should be sparse on bare muck. The muddy Hot Brook basin probably holds crayfish mainly in macrophyte beds and woody debris, with the highest densities on cobble shores.

### Gaps
- No quadrat or SCUBA density for virile crayfish in any Maine or New England lake, and no density split by substrate (rock vs mud) for this species in a native-range lake. Momot & Gowing 1977 and France 1985 (CJFAS 42:1096, Experimental Lakes Area) likely have per-m² values but were paywalled.
- No Maine/New England data on otter crayfish consumption, nor on the share of smallmouth, yellow perch or chain pickerel diets that is crayfish by mass in a lake.
- Winter behaviour in lakes (depth, burrowing, under-ice feeding) is described only qualitatively.

## 2. Freshwater mussels: eastern elliptio and other Maine lake unionids — density, biomass, size/age, filtration, glochidia hosts and timing

### Takeaway
Eastern elliptio is Maine's commonest mussel, "found in virtually every water body capable of supporting mussels and often very abundant" [ME].
- **Maine densities:** five Maine sites gave 0.4–27.6/m².
- **Northeastern lakes:** typically 1–25/m², with dense patches of 30–60/m² (a Connecticut lake) and up to 210/m² (a shallow polymictic Ontario bay).
- **Biomass:** at those densities mussels can outweigh all other macrobenthos combined, about 34–70 g/m² vs 1.5–9 g/m² in a Connecticut lake.
- **Depth and substrate:** density peaks at about 1–2 m in small lakes; the lower limit tracks the thermocline. Mud vs sand made no difference at 2 m in Ontario, but deep semi-liquid silt is avoided. Eastern floater is the species that tolerates soft deep silt.
- **Filtration:** per-mussel rates are poorly constrained: about 0.05–0.19 L/h in the lab vs 2.6–3.1 L/h claimed for summer field elliptio.
- **Reproduction:** elliptio is a short-term brooder (spawns late April–May, releases glochidia in early summer at about 15–18 °C). Its hosts include yellow perch, white perch, pumpkinseed, smallmouth bass and white sucker — all Hot Brook species.

### Cited Findings
**Maine status, habitat and species**
- [ME] Eastern elliptio and eastern floater (*Pyganodon cataracta*) "are widespread and abundant and are Maine's most common mussel species". — [MDIFW Freshwater Mussel Assessment (Nedeau & Swartz, 2007)](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/FWMussel_speciesassessment.pdf)
- [ME] Eastern elliptio is "found in clay, mud, sand, gravel, and cobble bottoms. The only habitats that appear to be unsuitable … are deep semi-liquid silt and the rocky bottoms of small high-gradient streams." — same source
- [ME] Lake species "usually do not show a strong habitat preference", but mussels generally occupy sand, gravel and cobble in shallow water (<30 ft) and "tend to avoid deep water and soft silt". The eastern floater "can tolerate deep silt and mud" and "is one of the few species that can tolerate deep silt substrates found in the deeper water of most lakes and ponds". — same source
- [ME] Eastern lampmussel is "very common in lakes and rivers of the central portion of the State, but is primarily absent from northern, western, and southernmost regions". — same source
- [ME] A 1992–1997 MDIFW statewide survey underlies the Maine atlas. — [Nedeau et al. 2000, The Freshwater Mussels of Maine](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/Nedeau,%20E.J.%20et%20al.%202000.%20The%20Freshwater%20Mussels%20of%20Maine.pdf)

**Densities**
- [ME] Excavated 0.25-m² quadrats at Sebasticook River, Unity Pond, Sandy Stream, Seven Tree Pond and Millinocket Lake: "densities of the common eastern elliptio ranged from 0.4/m² to 27.6/m²". In the same quadrats, yellow lampmussel was 0.1–1.6/m² and tidewater mucket 0–0.3/m². — [MDIFW 2007 assessment, summary of UMaine research](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/FWMussel_speciesassessment.pdf)
- [CT] Lake Pocotopaug: Elliptio 32–60 ind/m² with biomass 33.74–70.44 g/m². Other macrobenthos (excluding large mussels) in this and similar eastern Connecticut Highlands lakes was 2,320–16,200 ind/m² and 1.54–9.01 g/m² (Deevey 1941). Elliptio was absent below about 4 m, "the presence of soft substrata is probably one important factor". Shells were 48–101 mm (mean 73.9). Individual "biomass" was 0.18–2.19 g (mean 1.04 g; basis not stated). — [The Veliger 18(3):337, Lake Pocotopaug Elliptio study (BHL/biostor 132662)](https://archive.org/download/biostor-132662/biostor-132662.pdf)
- [ON] Lake Opeongo South Arm (22 km²), 18 sites at 2 m:
  - 0–23 ind/m², geometric mean 2.3/m²;
  - boulder sites geometric mean 1/m² vs mud or sand 4–5/m² (ANOVA P = 0.08), with "no systematic differences … between muddy and sandy substrates";
  - shells 44–83 mm;
  - Sproule Bay, a smaller polymictic basin (2.1 km², max 6.5 m), reached "up to 210 individuals/m² at 1 m depth".
  - Source: [Griffiths & Cyr 2006, Can. J. Zool.](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202000s/Griffiths%20and%20Cyr%202006.pdf)
- [ON] Seven Algonquin basins, depth profiles:
  - maximum density at 1–1.5 m in the smallest lakes and 3–4 m in the largest, often with a second peak at 0.5 m behind rock or sill refuges;
  - maximum depth of occurrence closely tracks predicted thermocline depth (Zlimit = −15 + 29 log10 PZthermo, r² = 0.92);
  - mussels were "commonly observed below the predicted mud deposition boundary", so "substratum type per se does not limit the lower range";
  - densities at depth: South Arm 0.25–4.5/m² at 7 m; Sproule Bay 4.8–6.3/m² at 5 m and 0.75/m² at 6 m;
  - mussels were smaller in deeper water (mean 36 mm at the lower limit in Costello Lake).
  - Source: [Cyr 2008, Freshwater Biology 53:2414–2425](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202000s/Cyr%202008%20Freshwater%20Biology.pdf)
- [ON/US] Several studies have suggested distribution "may be limited by very soft muddy substrata (Ghent et al. 1978; Strayer et al. 1981), possibly because mud interferes with their feeding". — Cyr 2008 (above)
- [MA] Six winter-drawdown and three control lakes: mussel assemblages were dominated by *E. complanata* and *P. cataracta*. At 0.5 m, densities were much lower in drawdown lakes than in controls. At 1 m, density was explained mostly by region. — [Roy, Carmignani, Hazelton & Giard 2019, Freshwater Biology 64:1519](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70223306)
- [VT] Elliptio "may comprise the largest proportion of animal biomass in many lakes and rivers, often far exceeding the combined biomass of other invertebrates and fish". — [Vermont Freshwater Mussel Atlas](https://val.vtecostudies.org/projects/vermont-freshwater-mussel-atlas/elliptio-complanata/) (snippet)
- [other] Habitat summary: most common at 1–1.6 m (3–5 ft); "most abundant in substrates composed of clay mixed with marl and fine sand, but rarely in mud". — [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Elliptio_complanata) (snippet; conflicts with the Opeongo mud ≈ sand result)

**Size, age, growth**
- [ME] Usually <5 inches (127 mm), heavy-shelled. — [MDIFW 2007](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/FWMussel_speciesassessment.pdf)
- [NH] Mirror Lake Elliptio lived 16–18 years (Strayer et al. 1981). Lifespan "may exceed 30 years (Kesler and Bailey 1993), though this is probably uncommon". — [Minnesota DNR rare-species profile](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/rsg/profile.html?action=elementDetail&selectedElement=IMBIV14060) (snippet)
- [CT] Lifespan >12 years (Matteson 1948). No small (<48 mm) mussels were found in Lake Pocotopaug (recruitment failure or sampling). — [Veliger 18(3)](https://archive.org/download/biostor-132662/biostor-132662.pdf)
- [RI] Marked Elliptio in Worden Pond grew only 4.3 mm in mean shell length over 14 years; growth in two nearby lakes was 3–8× greater. This is consistent with food limitation. — [Kesler, Newton & Green 2007, J-NABS 26:123 (USGS record)](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/long-term-monitoring-growth-eastern-elliptio-elliptio-complanata-bivalvia-unionidae) (snippet)
- [ON] Growth was 62% higher at the upwind (cooler, more exposed) sites than downwind in Lake Opeongo, contrary to expectation. — [Griffiths & Cyr 2006](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202000s/Griffiths%20and%20Cyr%202006.pdf)

**Filtration (clearance)**
- [lab] *E. complanata* chlorophyll clearance was 0.187 L/h per mussel. The authors thought this was depressed by two months of holding, and lower than field values. — [J. Applied Aquaculture 21(4), 2009 (EPA HERO record)](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/4638902/) (snippet)
- [VA, wastewater-pond enclosures] 53–134 mL/h per mussel depending on algal type. Clearance was highest at 25 °C, and smaller mussels had higher mass-specific rates. — [Virginia Tech thesis](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/73f767fb-7168-46ca-a7b9-27932ceb70c4) (snippet)
- [Delaware R., field-based estimate] "In the summer eastern elliptios can filter between 2.6 to 3.1 liter of water per mussel per hour". Activity is lower in winter than mid-summer. — [Anacostia Watershed Society, citing a Delaware River study](https://www.anacostiaws.org/anacostia-river-mussel-power-river-filters/)
- [lab] Clearance rises as particle size falls to about 3.2–4.0 µm, then declines. Filtration rises with particle concentration to a maximum and then declines. — [Paterson 1986, The Veliger 29(2)](https://archive.org/download/biostor-131965/biostor-131965.pdf) (snippet)

**Reproduction, hosts, behaviour**
- [ME] "This species is a short-term brooder. Fertilization takes place in early spring, and glochidia are released later in the summer." Hosts:
  - yellow perch, banded killifish, largemouth bass, bluegill and pumpkinseed;
  - UMaine work added white perch, smallmouth bass, redbreast sunfish, alewife, threespine stickleback, brook trout, black crappie and white sucker as potential hosts (Kneeland 2006).
  - Source: [MDIFW 2007](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/FWMussel_speciesassessment.pdf)
- [ME] Eastern floater is a long-term brooder: eggs are fertilized in August and glochidia released the following spring. Suspected hosts include pumpkinseed, yellow perch, threespine stickleback and white sucker. — same source
- [general] Spawning late April–late May (Matteson 1948); glochidia brooded about a month. In the lab, glochidia were released at 15–18 °C as mucous webs. — [Newly documented host fishes for E. complanata, JFWM 4(1):75](https://meridian.allenpress.com/jfwm/article/4/1/75/138863/Newly-Documented-Host-Fishes-for-the-Eastern) (snippet)
- [ON] Glochidia are "obligate parasites of littoral fish (e.g. yellow perch, smallmouth bass)". — [Griffiths & Cyr 2006](https://www.harkness.ca/PDFs/Contributions%202000s/Griffiths%20and%20Cyr%202006.pdf)
- [general] More than 90% of Eastern Elliptio were burrowed below the surface in December–January (Balfour & Smock 1995, a Virginia stream). Only 2–8% of lentic *E. complanata* moved during ice-free seasons. — [MN DNR profile](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/rsg/profile.html?action=elementDetail&selectedElement=IMBIV14060) (snippet); [PLOS ONE paper citing lake studies](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0173626) (snippet)
- [ME] Glochidial infection rarely harms host fish because infection rates are typically low. — [MDIFW 2007](https://www.maine.gov/ifw/docs/FWMussel_speciesassessment.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Density targets for a Hot Brook-type lake** (my synthesis):
  - **5–25 elliptio/m²** on firm sand, gravel, rubble or clay-mud margins at 0.5–3 m;
  - local beds of **30–60+/m²** in sheltered, firm-bottomed coves (the user reports "lots of mussels");
  - **0.5–5/m²** on the soft central muck, where the eastern floater, not elliptio, is the realistic deep-mud mussel.
  - Hot Brook is unstratified, so the thermocline limit does not apply. The lower limit is set by the softest mud, and possibly by under-ice O₂.
- **Biomass.** About 1 g dry soft tissue per 70–80-mm adult (Lake Pocotopaug's mean 1.04 g, if dry tissue) puts 10–30 mussels/m² at **~10–30 g DW/m²** — more than all other macrobenthos combined (1.5–9 g/m² in Connecticut). (My arithmetic.)
- **Filtration bracket.** At 10 mussels/m² over a 3 m column (3,000 L/m²), per-mussel rates of 0.05–3 L/h clear **0.4–24% of the overlying water per day** in summer. The high end is enough to matter for phytoplankton on the shallow margins; the low end is negligible. Activity should fall to near zero when mussels burrow in winter (Dec–Mar). (My arithmetic; the per-mussel rate is the dominant uncertainty.)
- **Life-cycle hooks for the sim:**
  - spawning after ice-out (late April–May, about 10–15 °C);
  - glochidia released in June (about 15–18 °C) onto yellow perch, white perch, pumpkinseed, smallmouth and white sucker;
  - juveniles drop off weeks later;
  - adults live 15–30 years and grow slowly;
  - winter burrowing.
- **Recruitment.** Elliptio recruitment is sporadic (Pocotopaug had no animals <48 mm), so a stable, long-lived adult stock with low recruitment is realistic.

### Gaps
- No Maine lake study gives Elliptio density by depth and substrate. The five Maine sites are a river, a stream and three lakes, without depth breakdown.
- No reliable in-situ, temperature-dependent clearance rate for *E. complanata* in a lake. Vaughn, Nichols & Spooner 2008 and Spooner & Vaughn 2008 (rates for other unionids) were not retrievable.
- Elliptio fecundity (glochidia per female): the Pocotopaug page cites Matteson, but the number was garbled in OCR.
- Strayer et al. 1981 (Mirror Lake Elliptio biomass and production) and Kesler & Bailey 1993 (Rhode Island density by depth) were not retrieved.
- Whether Hot Brook holds eastern floater or eastern lampmussel is unknown. The lakesofmaine.org mussel page lists species per lake but was blocked.

## 3. Benthic invertebrates of muddy shallow lakes: chironomids, *Chaoborus*, *Hexagenia*, caddisflies, amphipods, oligochaetes, snails, odonates

### Takeaway
Total zoobenthos in small northeastern lakes is a few g DW/m², with production about 5× biomass per year:
- Mirror Lake, NH: 2.5 g DW/m² mean biomass and 14 g DW/m²/yr production, all sizes;
- eastern Connecticut lakes: 1.5–9 g/m² of macrobenthos at 2,300–16,000 ind/m².

Group by group:
- **Chironomids and oligochaetes** dominate soft mud at hundreds to thousands per m². In a fish lake, *Chaoborus* runs from tens to hundreds per m².
- ***Hexagenia*** burrows in oxygenated soft mud at roughly 100–500 nymphs/m² where it thrives (≈1–9 g DW/m²/yr production in good habitat). It emerges in Maine from late June to mid-July, when surface water reaches about 18–21 °C.
- **Amphipods (*Hyalella*)** are a shallow-littoral (<1–2 m), weed-associated group: hundreds to about 2,000/m², ~0.4 g DW/m², P/B ≈ 4/yr.
- **Odonate larvae** average about 100/m² in lentic habitats (~0.09 g DW/m², ~0.75 g/m²/yr). Fish lower them.
- **Snails** are diverse in Maine (57 species) but poorly quantified there.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Soft-mud basin, 2–5.5 m, oxygenated in summer** (the sim's `BENTHOS` worms and scuds plus the insect larvae):
  - total macrobenthos **~1,000–10,000 ind/m²** and **~2–9 g DW/m²** (≈ 10–45 g WW/m² at DW ≈ 20% of WW — my assumed conversion);
  - chironomids and oligochaetes most of it;
  - *Chaoborus* **~50–500/m²**;
  - *Hexagenia* **~50–300/m²** where the mud is soft and organic.
  - Because Hot Brook never stratifies, *Chaoborus* has no anoxic daytime refuge. It must hide in the mud, so fish should depress it more than in a stratified lake.
- **Weedy and rocky littoral (0–2 m):** add *Hyalella* (**hundreds to ~2,000/m²**, ~0.4 g DW/m²), odonates (**~50–150/m²**, ~0.1 g DW/m²), snails, and caddisflies (no lake value found).
- **Production.** Total zoobenthic production should be about **5× standing biomass per year** (Mirror Lake 14 vs 2.5 g DW). Amphipods run P/B ≈ 4/yr. The sim's BENTHOS (CAP 15 g WW/m², production ~110–125 g WW/m²/yr) is at the eutrophic Bay-of-Quinte end. For a mesotrophic, coloured Maine lake, the Mirror Lake/Connecticut benchmarks (2–9 g DW ≈ 10–45 g WW standing stock, ~14 g DW ≈ 70 g WW/yr production) suggest that is at or above the realistic upper bound. (My conversion; check against the sim's DRY constants.)
- **Seasons.** Benthic larval biomass builds through autumn and winter, peaks in spring, and drops with emergence:
  - chironomids early May–July (bimodal possible);
  - *Hexagenia* late June–mid-July at 18–21 °C;
  - odonates June–August.
  - Amphipod density peaks in June and biomass in late summer.
  - Under ice, larvae persist but grow little.

### Gaps
- No per-m² density or biomass for any benthic group in a Maine lake, and none for a shallow tea-coloured northeastern lake by depth and season. Rasmussen & Kalff 1987 and Rasmussen 1988 (CJFAS: lake zoobenthic biomass regressions, littoral biomass vs slope and chlorophyll) were behind a 403.
- Strayer 1985's taxon-level (chironomid, *Chaoborus*, oligochaete) numbers for Mirror Lake were not retrieved; only whole-community totals.
- No lake caddisfly densities. No Maine chironomid emergence dates. No Maine-measured *Hexagenia* density.
- Phytomacrofauna per gram of plant (Cyr & Downing 1988) was not retrieved.

## 4. Zooplankton in coloured shallow lakes: community, biomass and numbers by season and under ice; planktivore effects

### Takeaway
Summer crustacean zooplankton in Atlantic-Canada lakes next to Maine (LakePulse, one summer sample per lake, 100-µm net) has a median of about **46–52 µg DW/L** (IQR roughly 17–129). In those lakes:
- calanoid copepods (mostly *Leptodiaptomus minutus*) and cyclopoids (mostly *Mesocyclops edax*) make up about 50–60%;
- *Daphnia* is about 23% within 250 km of Hot Brook, highly variable, and absent from about 30% of lakes;
- *Holopedium* is present in about 30–43% of lakes.

Adirondack lakes average about 66 µg/L of crustaceans plus 19 µg/L of rotifers. Under ice, density falls to about 25% of summer and copepods dominate. Planktivores (age-0 yellow perch, alewife, white perch) cause midsummer *Daphnia* declines and shrink body size. Soft water and falling calcium favour *Holopedium*, *Bosmina* and copepods over *Daphnia*.

### Inferences
- **Summer target for a Hot Brook-like lake: ~30–80 µg DW/L of crustaceans** (median ~50), plus rotifers at roughly a quarter to a third of crustacean biomass (Adirondack 19 vs 66).

  - *Holopedium* should be present in perhaps a third to a half of years or lakes.
  - The sim's current start (Daphnia ≈ 40 + copepods ≈ 24 µg/L ≈ 64 µg/L) is inside the range, but its Daphnia share (~60%) is high for this region.
- **Numbers per litre.** At the length–weight values in `zooplankton_dynamics.md` (*Daphnia* 1 mm ≈ 5 µg; most copepodites and *Bosmina* ≈ 1–3 µg), 50 µg/L corresponds to roughly **10–30 crustaceans/L** in summer and about **2–8/L** under ice. (My arithmetic; order-of-magnitude only.)
- **Seasonal course** (with the Maine calendar):
  - low copepod-dominated stock under ice (~25% of summer);
  - a spring rise after ice-out;
  - a *Daphnia* peak in late May–June;
  - a midsummer decline as age-0 perch, white perch and shiners feed;
  - copepods and small cladocerans through August;
  - a modest autumn recovery.

- No study quantifies white-perch planktivory on zooplankton in a northeastern lake.

## 5. Phytoplankton and clarity: chlorophyll a by season, the Secchi–colour relation, typical TP for mesotrophic coloured Maine lakes

### Takeaway
Maine treats lakes above 25 SPU of true colour as "coloured". In those lakes Secchi depth understates clarity and TP overstates productivity, so chlorophyll a is the deciding measure.

Mesotrophic Maine lakes run:
- TP 4.5–20 µg/L (statewide mean about 11 µg/L);
- chlorophyll a 1.5–7 µg/L;
- Secchi 4–8 m when clear, but only about 2–3.5 m when coloured at modest chlorophyll.

Coloured Maine examples include Bauneg Beg Lake (62 SPU, Secchi 3 m, chl 5.5 µg/L) and Madawaska Lake (Aroostook; 38 SPU, 3.3 m, TP 13, chl 6.9). Above about 8 µg/L of chlorophyll is a bloom. Diatoms peak in spring after ice-out and again at autumn turnover, with a midsummer minimum. Winter chlorophyll averages about 43% of summer.

### Cited Findings
- [ME] DEP trophic bands (long-term means):

### Inferences
- **Hot Brook water** (unmeasured): a tea-coloured (~30–60 SPU), soft, mesotrophic lake would plausibly sit at:
  - **TP ~10–20 µg/L**;
  - **summer chlorophyll a ~3–8 µg/L**, with a spring diatom pulse after ice-out and an autumn pulse at cooling and mixing;
  - **Secchi ~2–3.5 m**, set more by colour than by algae;
  - **winter chlorophyll ~40% of summer**.
  - Because it never stratifies, nutrients recycle all summer. A late-summer cyanobacterial pulse is possible in warm, calm spells.
- **Bloom threshold.** Use chl a ≈ 8 µg/L as the "bloom" line (Maine DEP), which also matches Secchi <2 m in clear lakes.

## 6. Aquatic plants: cover, biomass, dominant species, colonisation depth in coloured water, seasonal timing

### Takeaway
Maine lake plant communities sort by substrate:
- **pipewort–water lobelia** on mineral (sand, gravel, rocky) shallows <1 m;
- **water-lily beds** (*Nymphaea odorata* with *Utricularia purpurea*, *Nuphar variegata*, *Eleocharis robbinsii*) on organic mud at 0.5–1.5 m;
- **pickerelweed beds** on shallow organic soils;
- a possible **watershield (*Brasenia*)** type on organic soils;
- pondweed / *Vallisneria* / *Elodea* suites in higher-conductivity water.

Coloured lakes are dominated by bulrush (*Scirpus*) and floating-leaved plants. Late-summer submersed biomass in dense temperate stands is typically tens to a few hundred g DW/m². Lake-wide means are much lower (e.g. 30–150 g DW/m² in Lake Simcoe), and wave exposure cuts biomass several-fold.

At a coloured-lake Secchi of 2–3 m, rooted plants reach about 4–5.5 m by the borrowed Azzella regression, so a 4–5.5 m Hot Brook basin could in principle be vegetated nearly everywhere the substrate allows. No Maine plant biomass (g/m²) or whole-lake cover figure was found.

### Cited Findings
**Maine communities and species**
- [ME] Cameron (2000) surveyed 30 relatively undisturbed Maine lakes and ponds and found four community types:
  - pipewort–water lobelia aquatic bed: mineral soils, <1.0 m;
  - water-lily macrophyte bed: organic soils, 0.5–1.5 m; *Nymphaea odorata* dominant with *Utricularia purpurea*, *Nuphar variegata* and *Eleocharis robbinsii*;
  - pickerelweed bed: organic soils, usually shallow;
  - circumneutral–alkaline suite: *Vallisneria americana*, *Elodea canadensis*, *Zosterella dubia*, *Ranunculus aquatilis* and pondweeds (*P. perfoliatus*, *P. robbinsii*, *P. alpinus*, *P. vaseyi*, *P. strictifolius*).
  - Possibly a fifth type on organic soils dominated by *Brasenia schreberi* with few water-lilies.
  - Species per lake ranged 3–26.
  - Source: [MABP Ch. 6](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/aquatic/chapter_6_6.1to6.4.pdf)
- [ME] Highland Lake (Windham/Falmouth), 85 littoral plots in 2001, frequency of occurrence:
  - *Potamogeton epihydrus* 81.2%, *Eriocaulon aquaticum* 76.5%, *Leersia oryzoides* 78.8%;
  - *Juncus militaris* 63.5%, *Eleocharis acicularis* 58.8%, *Nymphoides cordata* 47.1%;
  - *Brasenia schreberi* 30.6%, *Stuckenia pectinata* 28.2%, *Utricularia intermedia* 25.9%, *Lobelia dortmanna* 23.5%;
  - *Nuphar variegata* 20.0%, *Nymphaea odorata* 12.9%, *Isoetes tuckermanii* 12.9%.
  - Some table cells were garbled, e.g. *Najas flexilis* and *Pontederia*. — same source, Table 6.2.2
- [ME] "Highly colored lakes had plant communities primarily composed of *Scirpus* and floating-leaved plants". *Pontederia* and floating-leaved plants were more abundant in more productive lakes. Clear rocky lakes had sparse *Eriocaulon*. — [USGS WRI 80-69](https://pubs.usgs.gov/wri/1980/0069/report.pdf) (earlier note)
- [ME] DEP plant survey cover classes: Dominant >60%, Common 21–60%, Sparse 2–20%, Present <2%. — [Maine DEP macrophyte SOP](https://www.maine.gov/dep/water/monitoring/biomonitoring/sop_wetland_macrophyte_surveys_2019_DRAFT20.pdf) (snippet)

**Biomass**
- [MA, 21 lakes] Macrophyte biomass and biovolume fell with winter drawdown magnitude (0.07–2.26 m). Annual taxa such as *Najas flexilis* gained share under drawdown. Values in g/m² were not visible. — [Carmignani & Roy 2021, Ecosphere 12:e03442](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70228529)

- [Norway] An *Elodea* invasion reached up to 310 g DW/m² averaged over the infested area. — [Aquatic Botany 1986](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304377086900306) (snippet)
- [Austria] Neusiedlersee maximum submersed standing crop was 17.5 g DW/m² at the most sheltered station vs 4.8 at the most exposed. — [Aquatic Botany 1976](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304377076900279) (snippet)

**Maximum colonisation depth**
- [other] Chambers & Kalff 1985: angiosperms' maximum colonisation depth lies at about 21% of surface irradiance and charophytes' at about 11%. Plants have been recorded below 1–2% (Hutchinson 1975). — [search summary citing Chambers & Kalff 1985, CJFAS 42:701](https://www.scielo.br/j/babt/a/PKQ8pWfKswCfFDvLKBNLM7x/?lang=en) (snippet)

**Crayfish and plants**
- [AB/WI] Virile and rusty crayfish reduce macrophyte biomass and diversity (Chambers et al. 1990; Lodge & Lorman 1987). *O. immunis* above about 140–150 g/m² significantly cut macrophyte biomass. — [USFWS ERSS](https://www.fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Ecological-Risk-Screening-Summary-Virile-crayfish.pdf); search snippet (§1)

### Inferences
- **Plant zones for a Hot Brook-type lake** (my synthesis):
  - rocky and sandy wave-washed shores: sparse pipewort / water lobelia / *Isoetes* rosettes (<1 m; low biomass, ~1–20 g DW/m²);
  - sheltered organic coves and the muddy shallows (0.3–2 m): water-lily, watershield and pickerelweed beds, with bladderwort and bulrush/*Eleocharis* fringes;
  - deeper soft mud (2–4.5 m): sparse-to-moderate submersed pondweeds (*P. epihydrus*, *P. robbinsii*), *Utricularia*, possibly *Nitella*. Biomass falls with depth to roughly the colour-set limit of about 4–5.5 m.
- **Biomass by zone**, in sheltered beds at peak (August):
  - floating-leaved beds plausibly **~100–300 g DW/m²** (above-ground);
  - dense submersed patches **~50–300 g DW/m²**;
  - exposed sites an order of magnitude less.
  - A whole-lake mean over the vegetated area of **~30–100 g DW/m²** is a reasonable target.
- **Seasonal timing** (inferred from the Maine climate; not directly sourced):
  - shoots start after ice-out (late April–May);
  - floating leaves reach the surface in late May–June;
  - peak biomass late July–August;
  - senescence September–October; floating leaves turn over 6–15 times a season.
  - Rosette species (*Eriocaulon*, *Isoetes*, *Lobelia*) stay evergreen under ice.
