## 1. Crayfish

- **The Maine list** (Maine Crayfish Project, UMaine/USM):
  - *Cambarus bartonii*;
  - *Faxonius immunis*, *F. limosus*, *F. obscurus*, *F. rusticus* (invasive), *F. virilis* (with *F. quinebaugensis*);
  - *Procambarus acutus*; NAS also lists *P. clarkii*.
- **Statewide catch,** MDIFW stream surveys of 1,061 sites in 2007–08: 94% *F. virilis*, 5% *F. limosus*, 1% *F. immunis*.
- **Rusty crayfish:** NAS holds records only in Somerset, Kennebec and Franklin counties (2003–13), none in Washington, Aroostook or
  Penobscot. It arrived as bait.
- **Near Hot Brook** (iNaturalist, within 50 km): *F. limosus* 7, *C. bartonii* 4 (all NB), *F. virilis* 2. None from the lakes.
- **Crayfish worms (Branchiobdellida):** *Bdellodrilus illuminatus*, *Cambarincola fallax* and *Xironogiton instabilis* on *C. bartonii*
  (Gelder & Smith 1987, northern Maine).
- **Calcium:**
  - *C. bartonii* nearly vanished from Algonquin lakes as Ca fell below 2.0 mg/L; the hypothesised crayfish minimum is 2–10 mg/L.
    Hadley et al. 2015, *Freshw. Sci.* 34:918, doi:10.1086/681910 (verified).
  - Crayfish are the most Ca-sensitive crustaceans: Cairns & Yan 2009, doi:10.1139/A09-005 (verified).
  - Shield-lake declines (Ca, Al, smallmouth bass): Edwards et al. 2009, doi:10.1899/08-148.1.
  - pH: France 1993, *Hydrobiologia* 271:65, doi:10.1007/BF00007542 (verified).
- **For Hot Brook:**
  - Likely present but unconfirmed. The best bets are *F. virilis* and *F. limosus*, and *C. bartonii* in the brook.
  - **"Crayfish not common" (the human) fits Ca ≈ 2–3 mg/L near the floor:** slow molts and soft shells, plus heavy predation by smallmouth
    bass and pickerel.
- **Role:**
  - Omnivore: detritus, macrophytes, snails, insect larvae, fish eggs.
  - Prey of smallmouth bass (main), pickerel, perch, bullhead, mink, otter, herons and loons.
  - Mates in fall; carries eggs in April–May; releases young in June.
  - The main predator of *Craspedacusta* medusae.

- **Fingernail and pea clams (Sphaeriidae):** *Sphaerium simile*, *Musculium securis* and *Euglesa* (= *Pisidium*) within 60 km.
  - Likely present.
  - General literature: hundreds to thousands per m² in mud; they tolerate soft, acid water and brood their young.
  - Eaten by pumpkinseed, perch, bullhead, sucker and diving ducks.
- **Calcium (unverified):**
  - Mussels persist in soft water but with thin shells. Elliptio is the most tolerant; lampmussels need more calcium.
  - *Unionicola* water mites live inside mussels.
- **Glochidia** are themselves fish parasites, on gills and fins in spring and summer.

## 3. Snails

- **Records within 60 km:**
  - ramshorns: *Helisoma anceps*, *Planorbella campanulata*, *P. trivolvis*;
  - *Physella acuta*;
  - lymnaeids: *Ladislavella* (= *Stagnicola*) *catascopium*, *Galba*, *Pseudosuccinea columella*;
  - *Campeloma decisum* (a native viviparid);
  - *Valvata tricarinata*; *Amnicola limosus*.
- **Calcium (unverified):**
  - Richness drops sharply below ~5 mg/L Ca, and few species occur below ~3 mg/L.
  - The soft-water tolerant groups: Physidae, *H. anceps*, *Planorbella*, *Gyraulus*, *Amnicola*, *Ferrissia*, *Campeloma*.
  - **So at Hot Brook: a low-diversity, thin-shelled community.**
- **Role:**
  - Graze periphyton and eat detritus.
  - Prey of pumpkinseed (a mollusc specialist), redbreast, perch, bullhead, crayfish, leeches (*Glossiphonia*, *Helobdella*), flatworms and
    ducks.
  - Pulmonates are mostly annual: they breed in spring and early summer, and the adults die off in midsummer.
- **First host of nearly every trematode in §5:**
  - *Planorbella*: black spot and yellow grub;
  - *Physa*: white grub and *T. physellae*;
  - lymnaeids: eye fluke and swimmer's itch.

## 4. Other invertebrates usually left out

- **Freshwater jellyfish *Craspedacusta*:** RECORDED at Lower in 2005. Non-native (from the Yangtze).
  - Medusa blooms are sporadic in summer and fall, thought to need water ≥ 25 °C.
  - Polyps stand 12–33 °C and overwinter as podocysts.
  - It eats copepods and *Daphnia*; crayfish eat the medusae.
  - **For the sim: medusae only in hot summers, in Lower.**
- **Freshwater sponges:**
  - Spongillidae on the Mattawamkeag at Winn (2026). Maine museum records: *Spongilla lacustris*, *Ephydatia muelleri*, *Eunapius
    fragilis*, *Racekiela ryderi* (a soft-water species).
  - Likely at Hot Brook. Their silica skeletons suit soft water.
  - They filter bacteria and small algae. Gemmules overwinter and colonies regrow in late spring; algal symbionts turn sunlit colonies
    green.
  - Spongillafly larvae feed on them.
- **Bryozoans:**
  - *Pectinatella magnifica* at Mud Pond, Springfield (2021), Drew (2024) and Island Falls (2026); 60 Maine GBIF records back to 1864.
    Native.
  - A Plumatellida colony on the Mattawamkeag; *Fredericella sultana*.
  - They filter phytoplankton and bacteria. Colonies grow from July to September and die back in fall; statoblasts overwinter.
- **Hydra and flatworms** (*Girardia*, *Phagocata*): likely.
  - Hydra eats cladocerans, copepods and **fish larvae**.
  - Planarians eat oligochaetes, snails and crustaceans, and scavenge.
- **Leeches** (iNaturalist, 100 km):
  - *Macrobdella decora* 35, *Placobdella parasitica* 5 (Haynesville 2025), *P. desseri* 2; one each of *Helobdella*, a fish leech,
    *Erpobdella obscura*, *Haemopis grandis* and *Glossiphonia elegans*.
  - Roles:
    - *Macrobdella* takes blood from frogs, fish, turtles and people, and eats frog eggs;
    - *Placobdella* lives on turtles;
    - *Helobdella* and *Glossiphonia* eat snails;
    - *Erpobdella* swallows chironomids and oligochaetes;
    - *Haemopis* is a predator and scavenger.
  - Eaten by perch, bass, pickerel, bullhead and ducks.
- **Water mites:** likely present; thousands per m² in weed beds.
  - Larvae parasitise chironomids, odonates and true bugs.
  - Adults eat microcrustaceans and insect eggs.
- **Isopods** (*Caecidotea communis*): shred leaf packs; intermediate hosts of acanthocephalans.
- **Amphipods:**
  - *Hyalella azteca* (Aroostook museum records; 2 iNaturalist within 100 km) and *Gammarus fasciatus* (2).
  - Gammarids need more Ca (Cairns & Yan 2009). **So at Ca ≈ 2–3 mg/L, expect Hyalella; Gammarus probably absent from the lakes.**
  - Hyalella is the intermediate host of *Leptorhynchoides thecatus* (bass and sunfish).
- **Ostracods, nematodes and oligochaetes:** likely.
  - A *Camallanus* nematode came from a fish in East Grand Lake in 1952 (USNM); copepods are its intermediate hosts.
  - Tubificids and lumbriculids are the alternate hosts of *Myxobolus* and *Eustrongylides*.
- ***Bythotrephes*:** absent, and the shallow warm unstratified lakes suit it poorly.
- **Calcium and *Daphnia*:** declines below ~1.5 mg/L (from memory). Jeziorski et al. 2008, *Science* 322:1374,
  doi:10.1126/science.1164949 (verified).

## 5. Parasites and diseases

None is documented at Hot Brook (maine.gov blocked; no "grubby perch" page found). Cycles from Hoffman 1999. The first hosts are in the
drainage.

| Parasite | Cycle | At Hot Brook |
|---|---|---|
| Black spot (*Uvulifer ambloplitis*; *Crassiphiala* on minnows/killifish) | *Planorbella* → fish skin cysts → **belted kingfisher**. Heavy loads cut young fish's first-winter survival (Lemly & Esch 1984, *J. Parasitol.* 70:466, doi:10.2307/3281394, verified) | likely, common on SMB, pumpkinseed, fallfish, dace, killifish |
| Yellow grub (*Clinostomum*) | *Planorbella* → perch, pumpkinseed, bass flesh → **great blue heron** | likely |
| White grub (*Posthodiplostomum*) | *Physa* → sunfish/bass viscera → heron | likely |
| Eye fluke (*Diplostomum*) | lymnaeid → fish lens (cataracts; easier to catch) → gulls | likely |
| Bass tapeworm (*Proteocephalus ambloplitis*) | copepod → small fish → bass (the larvae damage gonads) | likely |
| *Triaenophorus nodulosus* | copepod → perch liver → **pickerel** | likely (*T. crassus* absent: no whitefish) |
| *Ligula* | copepod → minnows/suckers (castrates) → mergansers, loons, gulls | possible |
| *Argulus* fish lice | eggs on hard surfaces in summer | likely |
| *Lernaea* anchor worm | breeds > ~20 °C | possible in hot summers |
| Myxozoans (*Myxobolus*, *Henneguya*) | oligochaete ↔ fish | likely |
| *Saprolegnia* | dead eggs (bass nests, perch strands); winter fungus at ice-out; post-spawn wounds | certain |
| Ich, columnaris | warm water > ~20–25 °C | columnaris likely, ich possible |
| Red-sore (*Aeromonas* + *Epistylis*) | stressed fish in spring and summer | likely |

| *Eustrongylides* | oligochaete → fish → herons, mergansers | possible |

**Amphibian disease:**
- Chytrid (Bd) was found in 7 of 9 Maine frog species in 2000–02 (Longcore et al. 2007, *J. Wildl. Manage.* 71:435,
  doi:10.2193/2006-345, verified).
  - Prevalence ran from 14.6% (toad) to 25.7% (leopard frog); none in gray treefrog or peeper.
  - It peaked in spring and autumn, with no die-offs.
- Ranavirus: wood-frog tadpole die-offs in Acadia (Gahl & Calhoun 2008, unverified).

## 6. Bacteria and fungi (general literature)

- **Aquatic hyphomycetes on leaf litter:**
  - Spore output peaks 2–4 weeks after leaf-fall (October–November).
  - They raise the leaves' N and make them edible for shredders.
  - Bärlocher's group in NB did the regional work.
- **Chytrids on phytoplankton:**
  - Host-specific, density-dependent epidemics that reshape the algal succession (Ibelings et al. 2004, *J. Phycol.* 40:437,
    doi:10.1111/j.1529-8817.2004.03117.x, verified).
  - The "mycoloop" moves carbon from inedible algae to *Daphnia* (Kagami et al. 2014, doi:10.3389/fmicb.2014.00166, verified).
- **Methane:**
  - It forms in the anoxic organic mud, bubbles out in summer and builds up under the ice.
  - Methane carbon reaches *Chironomus* and *Daphnia* (Jones & Grey 2011, *Freshw. Biol.* 56:213, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2427.2010.02494.x,
    verified).
- **Iron bacteria** (*Leptothrix*, *Gallionella*): orange floc at seeps and wetland outflows; the iron oxides bind P and DOM.
- **N-fixing cyanobacteria** (*Dolichospermum*, *Aphanizomenon*, *Gloeotrichia echinulata*):
  - *Gloeotrichia* recruits from akinetes in the sediment in early summer and carries sediment P up; its colonies are too big to graze.
    Carey, Weathers & Cottingham 2008, *J. Plankton Res.* 30:893, doi:10.1093/plankt/fbn055 (verified).
  - They fix N in late summer.
