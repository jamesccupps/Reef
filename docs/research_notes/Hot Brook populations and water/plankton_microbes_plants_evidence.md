## Key sources

1. Kejimkujik, Nova Scotia (the best analogue: 15 brown soft-water lakes, pH 4.8–6.6, June), Nussbaumer, Burgess & Weeber 2014, *Can. Field-Nat.* 128:1–24 — https://canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/index.php/cfn/article/view/1545. Densities and frequency of 26 zooplankton taxa; daphniids absent below pH 5.5.
2. Kezar Lake, ME (Cousins & Webster, UMaine, 2004–07; genera with body lengths) — https://kezarwatershed.org/zooplankton. Peritrich ciliates on copepods.
3. Damariscotta Lake, ME, 2008 (GSA 2009 abstract) — daphniids crashed soon after young-of-year alewives appeared.
4. Alewife effects in Maine:
   - Demi et al. 2015, *Hydrobiologia* 758:47 (8 lakes): young alewives cut cladoceran and copepod size; cladoceran biomass fell in 3 of 4 alewife lakes.
   - The Maine Alewife Interaction Committee review (https://www11.maine.gov/ifw/docs/AlewifeInteractionCommittee_LiteratureReview.pdf): large cladocerans "mostly extirpated" until the young emigrate (Post et al. 2008); TP lower with alewives (Kircheis et al. 2002).
5. Adirondack lakes (Siegfried 1988; Siegfried & Sutherland 1992):
   - lakes above pH 6 average > 33 phytoplankton and > 20 zooplankton species;
   - acid indicators are *K. taurocephala*, mallomonads and acidobiontic diatoms;
   - rotifer types: circumneutral = *Conochilus unicornis*, *Kellicottia*; coloured acid = *K. bostoniensis*, *K. taurocephala* [snippet].
6. "Jellification" (Jeziorski et al. 2015): *Holopedium* dominates below ~1.3 mg/L Ca [snippet].
7. *Gonyostomum*: Johansson et al. 2013, *PLoS ONE* 8:e62557.
8. *Gloeotrichia*:
   - present in 26 of 27 low-nutrient New England lakes (Carey et al. 2012);
   - recruitment up to 10⁴ colonies/m²/day (Sunapee); microcystin;
   - Maine peaks 58–192 colonies/L (LEA 2016) [snippet];
   - Maine DEP: most Maine lakes are not known to bloom.
9. GBIF "Hot Brook Lake" search.
10. iNaturalist species counts, box 45.48–45.78 N, 67.70–68.15 W.
11. GBIF herbarium records by family, box 45.40–45.85 N, 67.60–68.25 W.
12. MNAP natural communities [snippets].

## 1. Zooplankton

**Water chemistry.** Alkalinity ~5 mg/L CaCO₃ implies Ca ~1.5–2.5 mg/L, near the Daphnia/Holopedium threshold. Expect the two to
co-dominate in early summer.

### Cladocera

| Taxon | Evidence | Abundance and season | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Holopedium gibberum* | NE: in 100 % of the Kejimkujik lakes, mean 6.1/L, max 24.9 in June. **Nauplii and copepodids** are the largest share of numbers: calanoid stages ~25–28/L, cyclopoid nauplii ~6/L in June.

### Rotifers, protists and Chaoborus

- **Rotifers.** Expected taxa: *Keratella cochlearis*, *K. taurocephala*, *Kellicottia longispina*, *K. bostoniensis* (typical of coloured
  soft New England water), *Polyarthra*, *Conochilus*, *Asplanchna priodonta*, and *Synchaeta* in winter and spring.
  - Numerically dominant. Peaks after ice-out and in early summer.
  - Under ice: *Keratella*, *Kellicottia*, *Synchaeta*, *Polyarthra*.
  - Eat bacteria, small algae and flagellates; first food for perch, sunfish and alewife larvae. *Asplanchna* is a rotifer predator.
- **Ciliates and heterotrophic flagellates** [L, GL]. Always present. In brown lakes the bacteria → flagellates → ciliates pathway is a
  major carbon route; they are food for rotifers, nauplii and Daphnia.
- ***Chaoborus punctipennis***. Common across Maine (Washington County records; Kejimkujik benthos); *C. albatus* in Penobscot County;
  *C. americanus* A (fishless ponds).
  - Larvae hide in the sediment by day (their refuge from fish in an unstratified lake) and rise at night. Adults emerge May–September.
  - Predator of rotifers, nauplii, *Bosmina* and *Ceriodaphnia*; major food for perch, pumpkinseed and young fish.
- **The alewife effect (Lower).**
  - Young alewives remove large Daphnia and *Holopedium* from ~July until they emigrate in September–October; mean crustacean size falls
    by ~50 %.
  - Lower turns to *Bosmina*, *Ceriodaphnia* and cyclopoids in late summer; Upper keeps its large cladocerans unless alewives pass the
    brook.
  - Adult alewives import P, and the young export it when they leave.

## 2. Phytoplankton

No species record exists for Hot Brook or for Washington/Aroostook lakes. TP 9–10 µg/L implies chlorophyll a ~2–4 µg/L [GL].

| Group | Evidence | Abundance and season | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chrysophytes and synurophytes:** *Dinobryon*, *Mallomonas*, *Synura*, *Uroglena*, *Chrysococcus* | NE | Dominant to common; peaks under ice, after ice-out and in fall | Mixotrophs (*Dinobryon* eats bacteria; Bird & Kalff 1986); good food; *Synura*/*Uroglena* cause taste and odour |
| **Diatoms:** *Asterionella*, *Tabellaria*, *Aulacoseira*, *Discostella/Cyclotella*, *Urosolenia*, *Fragilaria* | NE | Spring bloom; fall. *Aulacoseira* can stay suspended in wind-mixed summer water | Silica; good food; chytrid hosts |
| **Cryptophytes:** *Cryptomonas*, *Rhodomonas* | L | Common all year; important under ice and in fall | Top-quality food (fatty acids); mixotrophic |
| **Dinoflagellates:** *Peridinium*, *Gymnodinium* | L | Under ice and in summer | Large, poor food. *Ceratium* occasional |
| **Greens and desmids:** *Staurastrum*, *Staurodesmus*, *Cosmarium*, *Closterium*, *Euastrum*, *Micrasterias*; *Sphaerocystis*, *Oocystis*, *Botryococcus* | L | Summer; desmids richest in the littoral metaphyton (*Utricularia*) | Mostly edible; gelatinous colonies pass through grazers alive |

These lakes' wide shallow beds favour it |
| *Dolichospermum*, *Woronichinia/Snowella*, *Merismopedia*, *Aphanocapsa*, *Chroococcus* | L | Occasional, late summer | — |
| *Microcystis*, *Aphanizomenon* | rare / A | — | — |
| **Picocyanobacteria** | L | Common in summer; a smaller share in coloured water | Food for flagellates, ciliates and rotifers |

**Seasonal sequence [GL]:**
- **Under ice:** cryptophytes, chrysophytes and dinoflagellates (motile, mixotrophic).
- **Ice-out to May:** diatoms with *Dinobryon*, *Synura* and *Uroglena*.
- **June–August:** chrysophytes, desmids, greens and *Peridinium*, with *Gloeotrichia* in late July.
- **September–November:** diatoms again and *Cryptomonas*.

## 3. Periphyton, metaphyton and the brook

- **Filamentous greens** (*Mougeotia*, *Zygnema*, *Spirogyra*, *Zygogonium*) as metaphyton in shallow coves, June–September. Grazed by
  snails, mayflies and chydorids.
- **Epiphytic and benthic diatoms** (*Eunotia*, *Frustulia*, *Brachysira*, *Tabellaria flocculosa*, *Achnanthidium*, *Gomphonema*,
  *Cymbella*) on plants and stones: the base of the littoral grazer chain.
- **The brook's riffles:**
  - *Fontinalis* moss (*F. antipyretica* recorded nearby; *F. dalecarlica* L);
  - red algae *Batrachospermum* and *Lemanea* [L];
  - diatom films and *Calothrix* crusts;
  - the moss shelters blackflies, scuds, mayflies and caddis.
- **Charophytes:** *Nitella* is confirmed at Hot Brook; *Chara* A or rare in water this soft.

## 4. The microbial loop and microbes [GL unless noted]

- **DOC and heterotrophy:**
  - 20–30 PCU implies DOC ~4–6 mg/L.
  - The lakes sit near metabolic balance, leaning net heterotrophic (Hanson et al. 2003); CO₂ supersaturated most of the year (Cole et al.
    1994).
  - Bacterial production is a substantial fraction of primary production (Jansson et al. 2000).
  - Under ice O₂ falls while CO₂ and CH₄ build up, then vent at ice-out.
- **Methane:**
  - Ebullition is the main path in warm, mucky, plant-filled coves (Bastviken et al. 2004).
  - Plant-mediated flux runs through *Nuphar*, *Pontederia* and *Typha* (Dacey 1980).
  - Methane-oxidising bacteria at the oxic sediment surface feed chironomids.
- **Iron, manganese and sulfur:**
  - Wetland iron–DOC complexes; oxic iron holds P.
  - Without stratification, P release is a calm-summer or late-winter sediment-surface effect.
  - Iron bacteria (*Leptothrix*, *Gallionella*) make orange floc at seeps and in the brook [L].
  - Sulfate reduction minor; purple and green sulfur bacteria A.
- **Chytrids** infect *Asterionella* and other diatoms in the spring bloom; their zoospores feed Daphnia (the "mycoloop", Kagami et al.
  2007) [L].
- **Viruses** lyse a significant share of bacterial and algal production daily.
