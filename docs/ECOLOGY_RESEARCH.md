# Ecology research: real north-temperate lakes against Synapse Reef

*Real lakes bank the zooplankton that Synapse Reef spends.* This is a literature pass written on 2026-10-14, before the zooplankton
and apex-predator slice, comparing published lake values with the sim's numbers as of commit cdc670a. The source notes are in
`docs/research_notes/Real lake ecology for calibration/` (six files: zooplankton dynamics, fish–zooplankton coupling,
largemouth bass, forage-fish early life, northern pike, whole-lake energy flow). They give every figure with its citation,
how it was read (full text, abstract or secondary) and the open gaps.

Real north-temperate lakes keep a large standing stock of zooplankton that grows by at most about a sixth of its mass a day. Their fish eat less than about a third of that growth, except in summers when a strong cohort of young-of-year (YOY) fish collapses the Daphnia. Synapse Reef already produces about the right amount of zooplankton, roughly 70–120 g wet per m² a year, against about 50 in oligotrophic Mirror Lake and 240 in the Bay of Quinte. But it holds **only a third to a sixth of the real standing stock** (16 µg dry/L in June, against roughly 45–100). It turns that stock over two to four times faster than Daphnia can grow, because its fish eat essentially every gram produced. That one distortion explains most of the downstream trouble. First-feeding fry meet 3–11 crustaceans per litre, where real larvae need 20–50. Bass fry starve at 11–15 mm, the size at which real fry are still guarded, growing 0.5–0.7 mm a day and dying mainly to predators. And an algae-proportional refill cannot produce a clear-water phase, a midsummer collapse, a two-week rebound or a tenfold trophic cascade. The apex problems are separate and partly a matter of scale. The slice holds up to four pike where a real lake would have 0.1–1 large pike per 0.07 ha, so each gets only 4–20% of its maintenance ration. The FB4 pike parameters (Bevelhimer) peak at a small-pike optimum of 24–25 °C. And bass enter spring at a relative weight (Wr) of 70–80, below every Midwestern population found (87–108). Several behaviours that look like bugs are realistic: fish outweighing zooplankton, 180–240 kg/ha of fish, about 22 sunfish fry per bass fry, a bass diet of crayfish and frogs with a low season-mean p, and pike landing about one sunfish in eight strikes. The shelved growth model did not fail because growth-based zooplankton is wrong; a 1 kg stock simply cannot produce the 12–18 kg a month the fish take. The next slice should rebuild biomass-driven zooplankton at a 3–6.5 kg target, together with a predation refuge and adult prey switching, then fix bass spawning order and spring condition, pike density and the pike thermal curve.

## Real lakes hold 3–6 times more zooplankton and turn it over a third as fast

Summer crustacean zooplankton in small mesotrophic-to-eutrophic lakes typically stands at **50–150 µg dry weight per litre**. Across 81 shallow European lakes, zooplankton biomass scaled with total phosphorus (TP) at a log–log slope of 0.88, with a median of 289 µg/L including rotifers. The regression predicts **about 72 µg/L at TP 20 µg/L and 102 µg/L at TP 30** ([Gyllström et al. 2005](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4775628/625280.pdf)). Oneida Lake's May–October means ran 97–315 µg/L for all zooplankton and 22–196 µg/L for Daphnia over five decades ([NYSDEC/Cornell 2024](https://dec.ny.gov/sites/default/files/2024-05/oneidalkfisheries.pdf)). Per square metre the benchmarks converge on about 4–5 g wet. Oligotrophic Mirror Lake averaged 2.0 kg C/ha ([Makarewicz & Likens 1979](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942575)). That is about 4.2 g wet/m², taking carbon as 48% of dry mass ([Andersen & Hessen 1991](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1991.36.4.0807)) and dry mass as 10% of wet ([Johannsson et al. 2000](https://www.reabic.net/publ/Johannsson_et%20al_2000_Dreissena.pdf)). The Bay of Quinte Ecopath model carries 4.7 g wet/m² ([Blukacz-Richards & Koops supplement](https://aehms.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Blukacz-Richards-and-Koops-Supplement.pdf)). The research notes converted these to a 3 m-deep slice. Recomputed for the sim's real geometry (700 m², 9.3 m mean depth, 6,500 m³), they give **about 3 kg wet on an areal basis and 3–10 kg on a volumetric one**. The volumetric benchmarks come from shallower lakes, so the areal figure is the conservative floor. Under ice, density averages a quarter of summer density across 101 lakes ([Hampton et al. 2017](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/ecology-under-lake-ice/)). One caveat applies: biomass tracked productivity poorly in 342 large, soft-water Norwegian lakes ([Hessen et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-073)), so the TP regression is a guide for small hard-water lakes, not a law.

**Production is set by temperature and taxon, not by food or crowding, and the field rates are modest.** Temperature, season length and taxon explained 76% of weight-specific production across 108 populations, while biomass was set by food, body size and depth ([Shuter & Ing 1997](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjfas-54-2-359)). Lake Ontario work applying these models gives the following daily production-to-biomass ratios (P/B) ([Johannsson et al. 2000](https://www.reabic.net/publ/Johannsson_et%20al_2000_Dreissena.pdf), drawing on [Stockwell & Johannsson 1997](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjfas-54-10-2350)). Herbivorous cladocerans produce **about 0.16 of their biomass per day above 10 °C and 0.04 below**. Cyclopoids produce about 0.10 and calanoids about 0.04 at 20 °C. Log P/B rises 0.043 per °C, a Q10 of about 2.7. A 1 mm Daphnia, weighing about 5 µg dry, therefore turns its biomass over roughly once a week in summer. Its eggs develop in about 20 days at 4 °C, 7.6 at 10 °C and 2.7 at 20 °C (the Bělehrádek coefficients in Johannsson et al.). It stops growing below about 0.06–0.10 mg C/L of good food, and the threshold is two to three times higher when the algae are phosphorus-poor ([Boersma & Kreutzer 2002](https://epic.awi.de/id/eprint/5157/1/Boe2002b.pdf)). Annual crustacean production runs from about **5 g dry/m² in Mirror Lake to about 24 g dry (240 g wet)/m² in the Bay of Quinte**.

The seasonal cycle follows from those rates. Daphnia carry over winter at 0–2.5 per litre, and resting eggs add at most a few thousandths per litre in spring ([Cáceres 1998](https://publish.illinois.edu/cacereslab/files/2020/09/Interspecific-variation-in-the-abundance-production-and-emergence-of-Daphnia-diapausing-eggs.pdf)). They then grow on spring algae into a **clear-water phase from mid-May to early June**, when the community can filter 170% of the lake's volume in a day ([Lampert et al. 1986](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1986.31.3.0478); [Luecke et al. 1990](https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.1990.35.8.1718)). They crash from food limitation, young fish hold them low through July and August, and an autumn rebound follows. The collapse threshold is quantified. Oneida's *Daphnia pulex* tolerated **about 10 kg/ha of age-0 yellow perch but vanished above 20–40 kg/ha**, once the perch ate more than the Daphnia produced. In a year when daphnids persisted, the perch's maximum daily consumption was under 27% of production ([Mills & Forney 1983](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1983)112%3C154:IODPOP%3E2.0.CO;2)). Collapses in limnocorrals came at 30–50 kg/ha ([McQueen & Post 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00008241)). Larvae under about 20 mm barely dent large cladocerans, whereas late-summer juveniles do ([Mehner & Thiel 1999](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007532720226)). Once fish were removed, total biomass recovered within about two weeks ([Ersoy et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0212351)). Refuges help Daphnia persist. A fish bile salt triggers diel vertical migration (DVM), whose amplitude grows with fish density ([Hahn et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6559785/)). Migrants trade a lower birth rate in cold water for lower mortality, and outnumber non-migrants ([Stich & Lampert 1981](https://doi.org/10.1038/293396a0)). Plant-associated taxa make up about half of zooplankton biomass in shallow lakes ([Gyllström et al. 2005](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4775628/625280.pdf)). Hypolimnetic refuge volume has been proposed as the driver of Daphnia supply to bluegill across Michigan lakes ([Mittelbach & Osenberg 1993](https://doi.org/10.2307/1939589)).

**The sim's production is right; its stock and turnover are not.** At 1 kg wet in June the sim holds **16 µg dry/L, or 1.4 g wet/m²: a third to a sixth of the real benchmarks**. Even its cap of 2,025 swarms (3.6 kg, 55 µg/L) sits at the bottom of the real summer range, leaving no headroom for a spring peak. Its summer production of 10–17 kg wet a month, if sustained for about five months, comes to roughly 70–120 g wet/m²/yr (7–12 g dry). That is squarely between Mirror Lake and the Bay of Quinte. Taken together, these figures mean the sim's zooplankton replaces **0.33–0.57 of its mass per day: two to four times the field rate for Daphnia, and three to fifteen times that for copepods**. Its fish remove 40–60% of the standing stock every day. By comparison, adult fish removed about 1% a day in eutrophic Lake Aydat ([Jamet 1994](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00877183)). Even at the YOY collapse threshold the figure is only about 5–18% a day; that is my arithmetic from the 4.5%-of-body-mass daily ration of juvenile perch ([Ersoy et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0212351)). In effect the sim runs an Oneida collapse year all summer without ever collapsing, because swarms are refilled in proportion to algae from a source that never runs out.

The low stock, the fast turnover and the near-total consumption of each month's production are unrealistic. The root cause is the refill rule (`PLANKTON.RATE_BASE × algae`), which ties new swarms to today's algae rather than today's zooplankton. So a population cannot build from a thin overwintering stock, lag behind and overgraze the algae, crash, or rebound. The refill also has no temperature term, even though real P/B nearly triples with each 10 °C. And the 3.6 kg cap limits any trophic cascade to about a 3.6-fold rise in zooplankton, where Wintergreen Lake's total zooplankton rose about tenfold when bass returned ([Mittelbach et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.2307/2265812)). Other features only look wrong. The areal production is realistic. So is the copepod escape chance of 0.4: lake herring catch 49% of copepods and 87% of cladocerans per attack ([Link 1996](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281996%29125%3C0139:CPOLSZ%3E2.3.CO%3B2)). A Daphnia share that rises with warmth and a weed-bed zooplankton source are realistic too. The shelved growth model's Q10 of 2.5 also matches Shuter and Ing's 2.7, and its collapse is no evidence against it. Even at its 0.4 per day, a 1 kg stock makes at most about 12 kg a month, below the 12–18 kg the fish were taking. At a field P/B of about 0.15, a stock would need 2.7–4 kg just to meet that demand, even if fish ate every gram.

**For the next slice, the evidence supports growth-based zooplankton aimed at 45–100 µg dry/L (about 3–6.5 kg wet) in summer.** The cap should allow a late-May-to-June peak above that, and the under-ice level should sit near a quarter of summer. Each kind should grow at a temperature-scaled, food-limited rate. Its maximum can exceed the field P/B values above, which already include food limitation. This change must ship with predation relief, or it will repeat the shelved collapse. At 3–6.5 kg and P/B 0.10–0.15, the lake makes roughly 9–29 kg a month. In a summer when Daphnia persists, fish should take about **3–9 kg a month (under 30% of production)**, against 12–18 kg now. Consumption should overtake production only once YOY biomass passes about 1.4–2.8 kg in the slice (20–40 kg/ha). The sim's 9.3 m mean and 14.4 m maximum depth, unlike the 3 m lake the notes assumed, allow real migration amplitudes. A rule that sends Daphnia deeper by day as a saturating function of local fish density is therefore realistic. It should be paid for through colder-water development: 7.6 days per brood at 10 °C against 2.7 at 20 °C. The only quantitative refuge figures found come from a modelling study seen in summary: amplitudes of 5–20 m in lakes, with little protection below about 6 m ([Limnologica 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0075951108000297)). Tune the refuge's strength against the calendar targets above rather than hard-coding it.

## Larvae need 20–50 crustaceans per litre, and predators, not hunger, kill them

**Real larvae start on the smallest prey and climb a size ladder.** The young of six Lake Opinicon species, including yellow perch, bluegill and pumpkinseed, all began feeding at 4.5–6.0 mm on nauplii and small cyclopoids 0.1–0.3 mm long. They took cladocerans from about 8–10 mm ([Keast 1980](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00005185)). Bluegill moved from rotifers and nauplii to cladocerans from 8 mm ([Siefert 1972](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281972%29101%3C219:FFOLYP%3E2.0.CO%3B2)). Bass first feed at 5.9–6.3 mm ([Fishes of Texas, citing Kramer & Smith](https://fishesoftexas.org/taxa/micropterus-salmoides)). Age-0 perch switch to preferring Daphnia only at 30–35 mm, when they catch nearly every daphnid they attack ([Mills, Confer & Ready 1984](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281984%29113%3C579:PSBYYP%3E2.0.CO%3B2)). When prey suffice, larvae eat near their physiological ceiling. Oneida perch grew 0.48 mm a day from 8 to 18 mm ([Irwin et al. 2009](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/138/1/99/7888582)). That implies about 0.45 g/g/d, roughly 0.9 of FB4 perch Cmax at 12 mm and 20 °C. Limnetic bluegill growing 0.4 mm a day imply about 0.36 g/g/d, *above* FB4 bluegill Cmax. Michigan pond modellers had to raise the FB4 bluegill consumption intercept 50% to reproduce observed growth ([Breck 1997](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1995.pdf)). Metabolism runs high too. Young-of-year perch showed active metabolism **4.4 times adult standard respiration** ([Post 1990](https://doi.org/10.1139/f90-063)), and early-life metabolic rate is nearly independent of mass ([Post & Lee 1996](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-278)). Prey density matters steeply. Pond bluegill growth fell from about 0.6 to 0.2 mm a day once crustaceans dropped **below about 50 per litre** ([Breck 1997](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1995.pdf)). Larval walleye consumption rose with density up to **20–30 per litre** ([Hoxmeier et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1577/T01-082)). Perch of 10–20 mm die of starvation after losing 46–55% of body mass ([Letcher et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281996%29125%3C0014:SDEOCA%3E2.3.CO%3B2)).

**Freshwater larvae die mostly from predation, and slowly.** They are predicted to survive to metamorphosis about 44 times better than marine larvae, 5.3% against 0.12% ([Houde 1994](https://doi.org/10.1006/jmsc.1994.1008)). Oneida perch larvae die at **Z ≈ 0.04 per day**. That mortality was not density-dependent, while their growth was ([Irwin et al. 2009](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/138/1/99/7888582)). A Nebraska lake ran 0.15–0.52 per day, with mortality falling as zooplankton rose ([Kaemingk et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00028487.2014.886622)). Predation is "the principal, proximal agent", and food acts mainly through growth and stage duration ([Houde 2008](https://journal.nafo.int/Volumes/Articles/ID/418/Files)). Starvation becomes decisive in the first winter. Under a simulated winter, 1% of fed age-0 perch died against 46% of starved ones ([Post & Evans 1989](https://doi.org/10.1139/f89-246)). In a northern lake, overwinter mortality fell on bass entering winter **below 50–60 mm** ([Post, Kitchell & Hodgson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1139/f98-139)). Survival often ignores zooplankton entirely while growth tracks it. Bluegill larval survival was uniformly high regardless of zooplankton ([Welker et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281994%29123%3C0703:GASOLF%3E2.3.CO%3B2)). Opinicon sunfish survival was unrelated to edible zooplankton at swim-up ([Garvey et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1890/1051-0761(2002)012%5B0194:PRISTT%5D2.0.CO;2)). Oneida age-0 perch weight, by contrast, tracked Daphnia biomass ([Mills, Sherman & Robson 1989](https://doi.org/10.1139/f89-113)).

**Adult planktivores eat less, and less zooplankton, than the sim's.** Minnesota panfish ate **1–2% of body weight a day** in summer ([Seaburg & Moyle 1964](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659%281964%2993%5B269:FHDRAG%5D2.0.CO%3B2)). That family of stomach-content methods can read 50% low, according to a study that measured perch at 5.5–6.7% a day in July and 2.2–2.4% in October ([Nakashima & Leggett 1978](https://doi.org/10.1139/f78-249)). Predatory fish keep two to three times more digestive capacity than they use ([Armstrong & Schindler 2011](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10240)). Diets are size-structured. Bluegill under 50–83 mm stay in vegetation on littoral invertebrates ([Werner & Hall 1988](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941633)). Pumpkinseed over about 75 mm eat snails, which make up more than 80% of their diet ([Osenberg & Mittelbach 1989](https://doi.org/10.2307/1943074)). Perch over 50 mm switch to benthos when zooplankton runs low ([Wu & Culver 1992](https://doi.org/10.1139/f92-214)). Pond bluegill moved from open water to the sediments within days as relative profitability changed ([Werner et al. 1983](https://doi.org/10.2307/1937507)).

**Against the sim, the fry's problem is prey density, not physiology.** At 16 µg/L, the sim's June water holds about 3 Daphnia-sized (5 µg) or 11 Bosmina-sized (1.5 µg) animals per litre. That is an order of magnitude below where real larval feeding saturates, so scramble starvation is the predictable result. The notes' break-even calculation uses the sim's floored respiration. It shows that a 12 mm bass school stays at maintenance as long as its realised feeding fraction `f.p` exceeds **0.32 at 20 °C or 0.48 at 15 °C**. Real larvae with adequate prey feed at roughly 0.8–1. The fry's 2–5 kg a month is about what 1.4–2.8 kg of juveniles eat at 4.5% of body mass a day. That is the YOY biomass that collapses Oneida's Daphnia, so the fry alone are a realistic strong-year-class load. The adults are the anomaly. Their 10–13 kg a month equals **2–3% of the entire fish stock's body mass per day in zooplankton alone**. That is about the whole field ration of adult panfish, so zooplankton must be supplying most adult food, where real fish draw 50–65% from the bed. At p 0.6–0.9, a 100 g sim sunfish at 22 °C eats 2.6–3.9% of body weight a day by the sim's own FB4 parameters. That is about twice the classic field figure, though within the bias-corrected 2–4%.

Several features look like simplifications but match the evidence. The sim's starvation rule (50% daily survival at K 0.55, about 45% below standard weight) matches perch dying after a 46–55% mass loss. The respiration floor at the 0.1 g rate matches Post and Lee's mass-independent early metabolism. And letting fry crop both swarm kinds is right, since first-feeding larvae favour copepods and nauplii. What is unrealistic is summer starvation as the main killer of 1–1.5 cm fry, the prey density those fry meet, adults leaning on zooplankton instead of the benthos, and a constantly high adult p. The evidence favours a season-averaged p of about 0.3–0.6 for adult sunfish, falling when Daphnia crash in late summer. No verified lake p-value exists for bluegill or pumpkinseed, so this rests on rations and the excess-capacity argument.

**For the next slice, the zooplankton fix is also the larval fix.** A stock of 45–100 µg/L gives roughly 9–67 crustaceans per litre, the band where real larvae grow. The copepod swarm should be treated as carrying the nauplii and copepodites that first-feeding fry under 8–12 mm need. Fry respiration currently omits activity costs, which Post 1990 justifies adding. If they are added, larval Cmax should rise by about 50% alongside them, as Breck 1997 found necessary, so that break-even p stays near 0.3–0.5. The validation targets are growth of 0.4–0.7 mm a day at 20–25 °C and summer larval Z of about 0.04–0.15 per day, dominated by predation. Summer starvation should be rare, and first-winter starvation concentrated below 50–60 mm. Adults need size-gated diet shifts, and a switch to benthos when zooplankton is scarce. Without that relief, growth-based zooplankton cannot survive the current adult demand.

## Northern bass spawn first, guard fry past 15 mm, and stay near relative weight 90–100

**Bass spawn early, invest heavily and guard long.** In Minnesota, bass spawned 2–5 days after mean water temperature passed **15.6 °C (60 °F)** ([Kramer & Smith 1962](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1962)91%5B29:FOYCIL%5D2.0.CO;2)). Wisconsin bass lay eggs at **16.7–18.3 °C** ([Wisconsin DNR 2008](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Species_lmbass.pdf)). In Deep Lake, Michigan, bass were **always the first of four species to spawn**, and in 1938 they finished before bluegill began. Their seasons averaged 26 days against bluegill's 65, and their nests yielded about 5,650 fry each ([Carbine 1948](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1101-RR1200/RR1166.pdf)). Eggs hatch in 3–7 days, and fry swim up at about 6 mm roughly a week later. Males guard the brood until the fry reach **15–20 mm**, for three to six weeks in all ([Bremigan et al. 2008](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/Special-ManagementReports/SR44.pdf)). Whole-lake nest success in Michigan was **45–77%** ([Wagner, Jubar & Bremigan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1577/T05-198.1)). Poor condition reduces reproduction rather than cancelling it. After a poor-growth year, ovaries were 4.7% of body mass against 6.6–10.9% normally, fecundity fell and spawning came later, yet the eggs looked normal ([Laarman & Schneider 1985](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1931.pdf)). Only a variable fraction of adults nest in any year. In one Wisconsin smallmouth population, 39% of males nested, with a range of 17–55% ([Bremigan et al. 2008](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/Special-ManagementReports/SR44.pdf)).

**The first year is limited by growth, not by fry starvation.** In Michigan ponds, bass fry ate microcrustaceans in proportion to their abundance (79% cladocerans, 19% copepods) and **did not depress zooplankton**. Bluegill fry in the same ponds made it "virtually disappear by mid-July" ([Laarman & Merna 1980](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1886.pdf)). Bass switch to insects and fish near 50 mm. The switch depends on outgrowing the YOY bluegill that become their prey, and one cold year prevented it in all but one Michigan lake ([Olson 1996](https://doi.org/10.2307/2265667)). In Paul Lake, only a few fish became piscivores in their first summer, and those needed at least ten days' hatch advantage ([Post 2003](https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658(2003)084%5B1298:IVITTO%5D2.0.CO;2)). Bluegill do compete with young bass ([Olson, Mittelbach & Osenberg 1995](https://doi.org/10.2307/1940708)), but the cost shows up as slower growth and delayed piscivory. Minnesota YOY reached 56–85 mm by the end of the season, roughly 0.5–0.7 mm a day ([Kramer & Smith 1960](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1960)89%5B222:FGOTLB%5D2.0.CO;2)). In the same lake, wind and temperature set year-class strength between egg deposition and two weeks after the fry rose. "Food habits, growth rate, and condition" were *not* factors ([Kramer & Smith 1962](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1962)91%5B29:FOYCIL%5D2.0.CO;2)). Deep Lake recovered **0.01–0.25%** of fry as September YOY, a minimum figure ([Carbine 1948](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1101-RR1200/RR1166.pdf)). First-winter survival then turns on reaching 50–60 mm.

**Adults feed late in the season, eat almost anything, and stay plump.** Lake Rebecca subadults ate "almost zero in mid-May and early June" but "over 5%" of body weight a day in late August. They gained weight only once age-0 bass and bluegill entered the diet ([Cochran & Adelman 1982](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00002501)). A single seasonal p fitted badly; three intervals were needed ([Rice & Cochran 1984](https://doi.org/10.2307/1938045)). Against the sim's FB4 Cmax (3.5% a day for a 250 g bass at 20 °C, 4.9% at 24 °C), that implies p near 0 in early summer rising to about 0.9–1 in late August. Northern diets run "from zooplankton … to vertebrates" ([Hodgson & Kitchell 1987](https://doi.org/10.2307/2425789)). In Voyageurs lakes, insects made up 0–80% of bass diet by mass, alongside crayfish and cannibalism ([Soupir et al. 2000](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70174039)). Spring condition stays high even where growth is poor. Slow-growing Upper Peninsula lakes in May give Wr of about 90–94 ([Wagner 1988](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1945.pdf)). Southeastern Michigan lakes give 87–108 ([Goudy 1981](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1896.pdf)). Food-limited lakes show slow growth at Wr near 90, not thin fish. FB4 overestimates metabolism below 6 °C: resting costs for age-0 bass had to be cut about 16% to fit Wisconsin-type winters ([Wright et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1999)128%3C0603:PHWAEO%3E2.0.CO;2)). Adult density runs 5–25 per hectare, with legal-size bass at 0.6–2.7 per hectare in Upper Peninsula lakes. Adult biomass is about 1–2 kg/ha in the north and about 9 kg/ha in southern Michigan. Natural mortality of legal bass is about 0.11 a year ([Wagner 1988](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1945.pdf); [Goudy 1981](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1896.pdf)).

**Against the sim, bass fry die of the wrong thing at the wrong size.** The sim's 11,600 fry include 8,600–9,600 that starve at 11–15 mm by July. At that stage real fry are still in a guarded school, two or three weeks past swim-up. The *number* lost is not unrealistic in itself, since real survival from swim-up to September is at most a fraction of a percent. What is wrong is the cause and the size. The fry ratio is realistic: about **22 sunfish fry per bass fry, inside Deep Lake's 20–85**. Fry per hectare (about 166,000) is three to eight times Deep Lake's 22,000–53,000. That is mainly a slice artefact: real nest densities of 3.8–9.3 per hectare mean 0.3–0.65 nests per 0.07 ha, so any whole nest is dense. The spawning windows are right (bass 15–21 °C and sunfish 20–28 °C in `species.js`), and the year-1 overlap is an artefact of opening in mid-May at about 17 °C. The adult diet of crayfish, frogs and tadpoles, and a season-mean p of 0.17–0.4, are realistic. What is unrealistic is p staying low through late summer, when real bass binge on the new year class. The sim also expresses low intake as **spring K of 0.70–0.80**, a condition loss below every Midwestern population, rather than as slow growth. Most adults then skip spawning in most years, where real bass simply invest less. The two failures are coupled. When every bass fry dies and young bluegill shelter in weeds, adults lose their main late-summer prey and enter spring thin. The sim's `M` of 0.40 is high for northern lakes, although a Florida Ecopath model assumed the same value ([Rogers & Allen 2009](https://archives.waterinstitute.ufl.edu/news/publications/downloads/AllenLakeFishFinalReport.pdf)). The cap of about 10 bass equals about 140 per hectare, against 5–25; a real 0.07 ha slice holds 0.35–1.75 adults.

**For the next slice, bass should spawn first and lose fry to predators, not hunger.** Open year 1 with water at about 12–14 °C, a design choice, so bass nest one to four weeks before sunfish. Keep fry in a school guarded to 15–20 mm that forages around the male rather than within a fixed nest reach, and confirm with realistic zooplankton that `f.p` stays above break-even. Move starvation to winter, below 50–60 mm. Aim for spring Wr of 90–100, replacing the hard K ≥ 0.8 gate with graded fecundity and a fractional nesting share. Test whether the below-6 °C metabolic bias explains the overwinter mass loss. Finally, let late-summer p reach about 0.9–1 when 20–60 mm YOY are available.

## Forage-fish young grow on food but survive on timing, cover and predators

**Bluegill spawn late and often, and most of each year class is decided in the days after swim-up.** In northwestern Wisconsin, spawning began only once water reached **21 °C**, in bouts: 4 in a 31-day season and 11 in a 112-day one. Eggs hatched in 2–3 days above 21 °C. Fry stayed on the nest 4–10 days, left at 5 mm and spent 30–40 days in open water growing about 0.5 mm a day, returning to the littoral at 15–20 mm. Spawns after the first week of July "will not add appreciably to the year class". In those predator-free lakes, October fingerlings ranged from a trace to 376,000 per hectare. That is a median of roughly 9% of potentially hatching eggs, with mortality concentrated in the first four days after dispersal ([Beard 1982](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/MKE6SE25RQNXU8T/E/file-57b89.pdf)). Predators cut that hard. In one Illinois lake, young bass ate 58–93% of littoral bluegill fry, and first-winter losses were 75–88% ([Santucci & Wahl 2003](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(2003)132%3C0346:TEOGPA%3E2.0.CO;2); [Neuswanger et al. 2015](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf)). In ponds, overwinter losses rose from 10% without predators to 55% with walleye. Timing matters as much as food: Lake Opinicon's first-trimester fry increased their share of yearlings by 231% ([Cargnelli & Gross 1996](https://doi.org/10.1139/f95-193)). Michigan bluegill average only 46 mm at age 1 ([MI DNR 2023](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/StatusoftheFisheryResourceReports/0417_2023_MiddleLake.pdf)).

**Pumpkinseed, perch, fatheads and bullheads each have a distinct early life.** Pumpkinseed share the bluegill's vegetation niche below about 75 mm SL, then specialise on snails. Across Michigan lakes the bluegill:pumpkinseed ratio ran from 25:1 to 1:1, tracking snail supply ([Mittelbach 1984](https://doi.org/10.2307/1941412)). Yellow perch spawn earliest, at 6–12 °C ([Ontario Freshwater Fishes Life History Database](https://www.ontariofishes.ca/fish_detail.php?FID=149)). Their egg-to-8-mm survival was 1.6–18.4% in Oneida, driven by temperature and wind ([Clady 1976](https://doi.org/10.1139/f76-241)). Their larvae live offshore and move fully inshore by about 30 mm ([Post & McQueen 1988](https://doi.org/10.1139/f88-214)). They eat nauplii, then adult copepods from 7 mm, then cladocerans, before turning to macroinvertebrates near 50 mm ([Fisher & Willis 1997](https://doi.org/10.1080/02705060.1997.9663552)). Fathead minnows spawn 16–26 clutches of roughly 400–480 eggs, 6,800–10,200 eggs a season ([Gale & Buynak 1982](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1982)111%3C35:FASFOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). In ponds, 37–76% of their eggs hatched and 24–47% of hatchlings survived to autumn ([Silver 2008](https://doi.org/10.7939/r3-jrj8-xs83)). Prairie populations mostly spawn in their second summer and then die ([Held & Peterka 1974](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1974)103%3C743:AGAFHO%3E2.0.CO;2)). Detritus made up 40–79% of their diet in prairie wetlands ([Herwig & Zimmer 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0633.2006.00220.x)). Brown bullhead spawn once, in a 10–15-day window in late May and early June, and parents care for the young for up to 20 days ([Blumer 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/2425607)). The young eat about 60% crustaceans (mostly ostracods and cladocerans) and 25% chironomids, and average no more than 76 mm by October ([Raney & Webster 1940](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1939)69%5B205:TFAGOT%5D2.0.CO;2)).

**Against the sim, the recent slices moved in the right direction.** Feeding young bullheads on the bottom from swim-up matches the Cayuga diet. Giving bluegill fry a limnetic phase matches Beard and Werner & Hall. Perch at 15–17 (about 230/ha) and fatheads at 75–300 (about 1,100–4,300/ha) are plausible, though no lake benchmark with piscivores exists for fatheads. Two things look off. First, about 3.6–4.3 million sunfish fry per hectare each summer is two to four times Deep Lake's 1.0–1.8 million, and above Beard's 0.4–1.5 million potentially hatching eggs per hectare: high, though within an order of magnitude. Second, lumping bluegill and pumpkinseed into one sunfish means no fish specialises on snails. Snails then have no specialist predator, and sunfish abundance cannot respond to snail supply. As with bass, the realistic pattern is density-dependent growth plus size-dependent winter loss, not summer survival set by starvation.

**For the next slice, survival should come from predation and timing rather than from finding food.** Larval mortality should be front-loaded through predation, late bouts after early July should contribute little, and winter loss should be size-selective. Perch YOY should be able to collapse Daphnia once their biomass passes 1.4–2.8 kg in the slice. If bluegill and pumpkinseed stay lumped, a snail share of the diet should phase in from about 75 mm. Cutting sunfish fry output two- to fourfold is only weakly supported. It can wait until the zooplankton changes show whether it is still needed.

## A 0.07-hectare slice can feed one large pike at most

**Pike eat small prey, prefer soft-rayed fish, and need a lot of them.** Across 2,233 prey items, the median prey was 10–18% of pike length, about **73 mm for a 65 cm pike**, and the 99th percentile reached 30–45%. Across predator taxa, the smallest prey grew only 7–14 mm from small predators to large, so large pike still take 2–4 cm fish ([Gaeta et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5854328/)). Within each prey species, pike prefer smaller, shallow-bodied individuals ([Nilsson & Brönmark 2000](https://lup.lub.lu.se/record/146726)). Bluegill become nearly invulnerable at **100–125 mm** ([Schneider & Breck 1997](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/PUBLICATIONS/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1901-RR2000/RR1992.pdf)). In the lab, esocids captured 0.67 fathead minnows per strike, but only **0.14 bluegill** ([Neuswanger et al. 2015, citing Wahl & Stein 1988](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Fishing/Pubs_BluegillLiteratureReviewFinal.pdf)). Tiger muskellunge needed about four times as many strikes to catch bluegill as fatheads, consistent with that 0.14 ([Moody et al. 1983](https://kb.osu.edu/items/2d9c1542-ae6e-5da4-8e7d-802b170a400f)). In Voyageurs lakes, fish made up more than 60% of pike diet by mass in every season, mostly yellow perch ([Soupir et al. 2000](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70174039)). In Lac Ste. Anne, occasional large prey dominated calorie intake ([Diana 1979](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/z79-279)). Pike eat **3–4 g of prey per gram of pike a year at ages 3–6**, and only 10–30% of that becomes growth ([Heikinheimo 1996](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0633.1996.tb00035.x)).

**Large pike are cool-water fish.** The FB4 pike parameters were fitted to 9–53 g fish with a 25 °C optimum. Larger Ontario pike grew best at 19 °C, stopped growing at 27.5 °C and died at 29.4 °C. Minnesota's large-pike model peaks consumption at 21.9 °C and stops growth at 26 °C ([Jacobson 1992](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/pre2003/other/930346.pdf)). A secondary source reports that the esocid bioenergetics model overestimates field consumption by 32–59% ([Wahl & Stein 1991, via a Colorado DNR document](https://dnrweblink.state.co.us/CWCB/0/doc/134067/Page6.aspx)). According to a search summary of the paper, large Minnesota pike followed the thermocline in August and chose **16–21 °C** when 28 °C water was available ([Pierce et al. 2013](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2013/other/131208.pdf)). In Nebraska lakes, pike growth fell as July bottom temperature rose from 20 to 25 °C ([Paukert & Willis 2003](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/population-characteristics-and-ecological-role-northern-pike-shallow-natural-lakes)). Small Minnesota lakes hold 3.2–59 pike over 350 mm per hectare, but only 1.6 over 600 mm ([Pierce & Tomcko via Gibson & Haedrich](https://naturenl.ca/pike-population-in-minipi-lake-labrador/)). A high-density Nebraska population reached 35.8 per hectare and 22 kg/ha ([DeBates et al. 2003](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70025129)).

**Against the sim, the shortfall is density and prey mass, not strike success.** A cap of about four pike is 57 per hectare. Four 65 cm pike (about 1.9 kg each) come to about 107 kg/ha, and even one is 27 kg/ha. A real slice this size holds 0.2–4 pike over 35 cm and about 0.1 over 60 cm. At 23–26 °C the sim's own FB4 set puts a 65 cm pike's maintenance at **28–33 g a day, about 8–10 six-centimetre sunfish**, or 380–450 per 45 days. So the sim's 17–80 kills per 45 days cover only 4–21% of maintenance, even if one pike made them all. At 3–4 g/g a year, one pike needs 5.6–7.5 kg of prey. Real lakes produce only 50–200 kg/ha of fish a year, or 3.5–14 kg in the slice ([Randall, Minns & Kelso 1995](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40EMMM3l)). A resident adult pike would therefore eat most of the slice's fish production, and four could not be fed at all.

Several pike behaviours only look wrong. The sim's per-strike success of about 0.12 on a 6 cm sunfish (`capture` 0.6 × `vuln` 0.35 × the size refuge) matches the lab's 0.14. The pike notes argued that the spiny-prey penalty was stacked too heavily, but they read the garbled abstract value "0A4" as 0.44; the review's 0.14 and Moody's fourfold ratio both contradict that reading. The sim's capture rate on fatheads, about 0.6, matches the lab's 0.67. The ≥100–125 mm bluegill refuge is real. Summer stress at 23–26 °C is realistic in direction. What is unrealistic is the 80% fall in kills, which is steeper than the literature implies. Jacobson's curve keeps consumption near its peak at 23–26 °C and lets growth collapse instead, and FB4's Bevelhimer curve cannot cut intake at those temperatures. The sim's decline must therefore come from encounter and capture, perhaps partly from weed density. In the lab, bass captures of bluegill fell to near zero at 250 or more plant stems per square metre ([Savino & Stein 1982](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8659(1982)111%3C255:PIBLBA%3E2.0.CO;2)), so dense weeds would be a realistic contributor. The `PREY_MIN` of 0.07 (4.6 cm for a 65 cm pike) is also high. It excludes the smaller fatheads, the easiest prey a pike has.

**For the next slice, pike should be scarce, cool-seeking and fed by heavier meals.** Treat adult pike as transients averaging at most one per slice. Replace or scale the small-pike thermal curve: either a consumption peak near 22 °C with zero growth near 26 °C, or FB4 Cmax × 0.6–0.75. Let pike seek 16–21 °C water with at least 3 mg/L O₂ when the lake stratifies. Lower `PREY_MIN` to about 0.03–0.05. Make perch the preferred prey, and give access to heavier meals: 10–20 cm perch, young bass and frogs. Keep the sunfish strike success and the bluegill refuge as they are. The warm-water asymmetry should then emerge on its own. Bass near their 27.5 °C optimum should outcompete pike in summer, while pike regain the edge in spring, autumn and under ice. Under warming scenarios, pike growth fell 3–45% while bass changed little ([Breeggemann et al.](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70154842)).

## Fish biomass is right; most fish food should come from the bed

**The sim's fish standing stock is realistic.** Its 180–240 kg/ha falls inside the 100–350 kg/ha typical of productive north-temperate lakes. It sits near Michigan's slow-bluegill lakes (about 204 kg/ha) and the Bay of Quinte (about 165) ([Schneider 1973](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/Publications/PDFS/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1794.pdf); [Blukacz-Richards & Koops](https://aehms.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Blukacz-Richards-and-Koops-Supplement.pdf)), and well below hypereutrophic Lake Wingra's 494 ([Churchill 1976](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/AFT5MWSMAMSY3J86)). Across 60 Florida lakes, trophic state explained only about a quarter of the variance in fish biomass. Centrarchids averaged 66% of that biomass and piscivores about 22% ([Bachmann et al. 1996](https://lake.wateratlas.usf.edu/upload/documents/f95-236.pdf)). Biomass can be top-heavy while production narrows upward. In the Bay of Quinte, fish (16.5 g/m²) outweigh zooplankton (4.7 g/m²), yet production narrows from about 2,650 to 365 to 20 g/m² a year. Fish outweighing zooplankton in the sim is therefore normal. Only the degree is off, 13–17:1 against Quinte's 3.5:1, because the zooplankton stock is low.

**Most fish production runs through the bed.** Fish communities produce **82 kg/ha a year on average across 22 lakes** ([Randall, Minns & Kelso 1995](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40EMMM3l)). Production rises with biomass and falls with body size ([Downing & Plante 1993](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/production-of-fish-populations-in-lakes/)). In the Bay of Quinte, adults turn over at 0.3–0.6 a year and YOY at 6–9. Production per unit consumption (P/Q) is about 0.08–0.15 for adults, 0.18–0.36 for YOY and about 0.20 for the whole community. Zooplankton production there is about 9% of phytoplankton production, and fish production about 5% of invertebrate production. Both ratios sit inside a lake compilation whose typical step is about 10% ([Mehner et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10398700-trophic-transfer-efficiency-lakes)). Across 470 fish populations, zoobenthos supplied about half of all prey eaten, and about 65% counting fish that had themselves eaten zoobenthos ([Vander Zanden & Vadeboncoeur 2002](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/biology/585)). Littoral carbon averaged 57% of fish carbon across 546 populations, although benthic algae supplied only 36% of whole-lake primary production ([Vander Zanden et al. 2011](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/biology/587)). Eutrophication shifts primary production from periphyton to phytoplankton ([Vadeboncoeur et al. 2003](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/biology/617)). Non-mussel zoobenthos in the Bay of Quinte produces about **125 g wet/m² a year**, about 70 of it chironomids.

**Against the sim, the benthos is about the right size but the diet split is backwards.** Insect production of 50–70 g wet/m²/yr in the shallows matches Quinte's chironomids. Adding snail and crayfish production brings the shallows to roughly 70–90 g/m²/yr, about two-thirds of the Quinte total. Snails at 3 g/m² are about three times Quinte's 0.9 g/m², plausible for a weedy littoral but only weakly supported. Crayfish at 9 g/m² could not be checked, because the one benchmark found lacked a retrievable source. Meanwhile adults take 2–3% of the fish stock's body mass a day as zooplankton. Zooplankton therefore supplies most adult food, where real lakes get a third to a half of it there. The piscivore caps, if filled, would put bass and pike far above the 15–25% of fish biomass that marks balanced communities (Swingle's forage-to-carnivore ratio of 3–6, per [AFS teaching material](https://education.fisheries.org/wp-content/uploads//fisheriesTechniquesVisuals/Chap_15.pdf)).

**For the next slice, the balance tool should measure the sim's own budget.** It should report fish production, consumption and P/Q. The targets are fish production of 50–200 kg/ha a year (3.5–14 kg in the slice), adult P/Q of 0.05–0.15, and invertebrate production at least about ten times fish production. If adult diets shift toward the bed, benthic production may need to rise toward Quinte's 125 g/m² a year. Bass and pike together should hold about 1–3 kg in the slice. That covers both the 6–30 kg/ha of real bass and pike populations and the 15–25% piscivore share.

## Conclusion

The sim's food web is mostly right in its flows and wrong in its stocks. Production, fish biomass and the fry's grazing load all fall in realistic ranges. What is missing is the banked zooplankton biomass that lets a real lake absorb a hungry summer and collapse only in the years it should. Seen this way, several apparently separate defects become one problem. Bass fry starve because there are too few prey per litre. Adult bass lose condition because the fry that should feed them in August are gone. And adult fish crop zooplankton to the bone because nothing sends them to the bed. The order of fixes therefore matters. Biomass-driven zooplankton without a refuge and prey switching will collapse again, and fixing bass condition before fry survival treats only a symptom. The second lesson is about scale. A 0.07 ha slice cannot hold whole apex predators at real densities; one 65 cm pike already makes it a high-density lake. Pike and bass in the slice should behave as visiting fractions of a larger population, which is what the existing immigration and refuge machinery is for, rather than as residents whose food the slice must supply.

Three evidence gaps should temper confidence. There are no verified field p-values for bluegill or pumpkinseed in lakes. No Ecopath model of a small Upper-Midwest lake was found. And the refuge strengths rest more on mechanisms than on numbers. Each change should therefore be judged against dynamic targets rather than single static means: clear-water timing, collapse thresholds, cascade size and first-winter size selection. The project's existing multi-seed A/B is the right test bed for that.
